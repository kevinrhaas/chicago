BENEDICT ARNOLD.

[The opening sentence is partly faded.]

His biography forms a volume by Mr. Sparks; and that part relating to his treason and its causes is especially interesting.

Benedict was born at Norwich. He had an excellent mother, but his father was a “man of suspicious integrity.” He was apprenticed to a druggist, to whom his conduct was pleasant [illegible]. He began his career by running away from his master, and [illegible] the young [illegible] to a sight of the old cause, that he might be diverted with the cries of battle. He took [illegible] as an exciting frolic. He was as daring as he was cruel—sometimes he would astonish his mates by clinging to a large water-wheel, making revolutions on it, beneath and in the water.

Of course with cruelty and restlessness, he had the requisites for a soldier, and enlisted at the age of sixteen, but soon after deserted and became a druggist at New Haven. He soon became a merchant, trading in cattle to the West Indies, a business in those times a decided [illegible].

There were two companies of militia called the Governor's Guards, and in 1775 Arnold was chosen to command one. After the battle of Lexington, sixty of them marched to Cambridge. Arnold applied to the Committee of Safety, and proposed the surprise of Ticonderoga, and was commissioned to raise a colonel's command in the Massachusetts service for that purpose. But Ethan Allen had arrived on the march, having brought with him [illegible], and effected the surrender of the fortress “in the name of the Great Jehovah and the Continental Congress.”

He set out against the stream, and was gifted with the power of penetrating by land through the eastern wilderness. Congress gave him the command of eleven hundred picked men, and such officers as Christopher Greene, Enos, Meigs, Bigelow and Morgan. This was sufficient adventure for any lover of military enterprise; but the advantages which they gave him to menace the French settlements were better than might have been expected. The main event that ever happened to him was the assault on Quebec.

When the expedition was over, Arnold was a brigadier, continuing at Montreal; and when he quitted it, he took goods from the merchants for army use. Col. Hazen was his bitter enemy; and charges, investigation, and neglect followed. Arnold demanded a court martial, which was long delayed. When the enemy's approach toward the lake was announced, the flotilla under Arnold met the British. After desperate fighting—and left with one vessel—he was not beaten: he ran his vessel on shore as it was in flames, lest it fall to the enemy.

When the second battle commenced, Arnold was in a state approaching to phrenzy, and after a while, without order or permission, rode full speed to the field. Gates sent Major Armstrong after him with orders, which Arnold, surmising, kept himself out of the way. He sought the hotter parts of the field, and issued commands where he thought it necessary. His conduct was represented as rash in the extreme. But the “crowning glory” was his assault on the redoubts, and driving the Hessians from their camp. He was shot through the leg, and his horse killed under him. The success secured the victory. Without command, he directed one of the most important battles of the revolution—yet he appeared demented: some supposed him to be intoxicated; others, to have taken opium. He struck an officer, who, instead of killing him on the spot, on the next day demanded redress—when Arnold expressed his regret and entire ignorance of the offence. After the battle, Congress relented, and gave him the full rank he claimed.

He was next invested with the chief command at Philadelphia, a situation of too much delicacy for his temper. His pecuniary embarrassment pressed hard upon him—but he tried profusion instead of retrenchment. He had stepped into debt so far, that “retrieving was as bad as to plunder.” He involved himself in quarrels with the Pennsylvania authorities, and was reprimanded by sentence of a court martial.

But the devil had entered into his soul; and before the blow to his pride he made advances to the enemy under a feigned name. He thirsted both for revenge and gold. He lived in a style of aristocracy, keeping a coach and four, when more honest men walked in the mud. He had more ways of raising means than were ever tried before. More, and he entered into desperate speculations and privateering.

He requested a loan of the French Minister on pretence of making himself useful to the British Empire. But the minister was a high-minded ally, and could not listen to what would injure the interest of the colonies. He gave Arnold good counsel—when, like the Italian beggar, he asked for money, not counsel. About that time he was married to a beauty of great wit and attractions, Miss Shippen, whose predilections were of the royal school. [One phrase illegible], because beauty is despotic.

Arnold was now chewing the bitter cud of mortification, and he finally concluded that a connexion with his new friends would be cemented by the injury he could do to his old. The most important place was West Point; and, in raising up to his enemies that he might be in command there, required caution and dissimulation. He managed it at last.

The correspondence was now begun. Arnold was a correspondent with Sir Henry Clinton, communicating without exciting suspicion. “Anderson” and “Gustavus” were the assumed names. André wrote to Anderson. Both wrote in a disguised hand. Arnold's communications gave important intelligence to General Clinton, and at length a personal interview with the British officer was arranged.

“Count de Rochambeau?” Arnold demanded what he meant, supposing that all was discovered.

André was positively instructed by Sir Henry Clinton not to change his dress, nor to go into the American ranks—in both particulars his instructions were broken. He expected himself that André would come off to the Vulture. But after many obstacles they met at a house on shore; Major André was clothed in his uniform, but enveloped in a blue great coat. On the next day a cannonade was commenced by Col. Livingston upon the Vulture, which was obliged to drop down the river. Arnold beheld it not without emotion.

At last the plot was finished, and the conditions settled. The conditions have never transpired—but the payment was no doubt to be large. Clinton wrote to the ministry that a plan of such vast importance ought to be pursued at any expense. The post was to be weakened by sending out detachments of troops, and other facilities afforded the captors.

André was furnished with a plan of the works, and other papers. He demanded to be put on board the Vulture. Arnold assented, but suggested difficulties, and recommended the return by land. Arnold put the papers between André's stockings and feet, and the unfortunate Major left the house in the last hours of night. He had a pass from Arnold, and at night set out with Smith.

He had no chance to get on board the Vulture; there was no course but to try to reach New York by land. He put on a citizen's coat, leaving his own clothes behind. The sense of his danger must have been uppermost in his mind, for he was reserved, grave and thoughtful.

At the outposts Arnold's pass was exhibited, but the officers were very inquisitive to know what was the urgent business of Smith and his companion beyond the lines. Their answer was, to meet a person who would communicate important intelligence. The representations of the dangers of the night journey moved Smith, who, much to André's regret, determined to rest in the vicinity till morning.

In the morning André's countenance brightened as he had passed the last patrol; he became cheerful and entertained Smith. Two hours' travel carried them onward; Smith left him to pursue his way alone, and returned to report to Arnold.

On this same morning, seven persons living near the river had agreed to watch the road and intercept suspicious strangers and droves of cattle. They were stationed on a hill, in sight of a long line of road, and concealed in the bushes by its side. Their rank was humble, but their lives are immortal. The circumstances of André's capture, the trial and the condemnation, are well known to the youngest child.

Washington sent [André's papers] to the commander-in-chief, and at the utmost moment the packet was opened by Hamilton. Washington, Lafayette and Knox, to whom he showed the papers, were silent. “Whom can we trust now?”

His feelings were severely tried by the grief of Mrs. Arnold, who was frantic with distress. She was not acquainted with the treason till it was discovered. She sent to her husband, who had but one concern visible—his attachment to her.

Arnold carried on that small part of the war that the British commander entrusted to him, in the spirit of a bandit. He plundered and burnt the settlements on James River, in Virginia. In this expedition he made prisoner of a captain, and asked him what the Americans would do to himself should he be made their prisoner? The captain answered, “They will cut off your leg which was wounded in a good cause, and bury it with the honors of war; and hang the rest of your body on a gibbet.”

His next buccaneer expedition was in the neighborhood of his native place, where he pillaged his home some thirty years before, for the purpose of hearing their curses. He returned to New London. His officers were men of his own stamp: one of whom, Col. Ledyard, at Fort Griswold, surrendered his sword, killed thirty-seven officers with his own weapon, with a fierce imitation of Nero mounted a steeple to see the burning of the city. This was his last military exploit. He had done enough for glory, and retired from the field.

He lived, however, twenty years, and the only subsequent acts that are recorded of him are such as any petty scoundrel might perform. They were unworthy his pen and reputation: they denote a hero that would rob a hen-roost as well as his country.

A MOST DISTRESSING FIRE.
AWFUL CATASTROPHE.

A sad accident occurred at New Orleans on the night of the 14th ultimo. The particulars are subjoined.

[Extract of a letter, dated New Orleans, May 15, 1835.]

This morning, about three o'clock, I was aroused by a sudden and tremendous crash, which I supposed at first to be an earthquake; but upon venturing out, I discovered the Merchants and Planters Hotel in Canal Street had fallen in. Great exertions were immediately made during the day to remove the ruins and save the lives of the next inmates—twenty-four have been got out—four dead, and many of the others badly bruised. It is still supposed that there are many bodies under the rubbish, as there were 60 lodgers on the register, and many are unaccounted for. Our part of the city has been covered with spectators through the day, and consequently little business doing.

New Orleans, May 13, 1835.

The three story brick building on the north side of Canal-street, and between Camp and Magazine-streets, occupied as the Planters' Hotel, and kept by Charles Armstrong, fell to the earth just about six o'clock. Repairs had lately been making to the lower story, and it is presumed too much of the support had been incautiously cut away.

The hotel was occupied as an eating and lodging house and, it is calculated, contained at the moment of the dreadful accident between sixty and seventy inmates. The first alarm was given by a boarder who had left the house but a short time before the fall of the building. He had passed to the opposite side of the street, and, on looking toward the house, heard three distinct and loud cracks, resembling the discharge of cannon; then a sound like an earthquake, as the mass fell. For a moment he saw no one, appalling cries issuing from the sufferers. The alarm was given to the citizens, and the engine companies turned out, displaying their habitual spirit of humanity. Dr. Campbell was early on the spot rendering surgical aid.

At half past three o'clock, twenty-four of the sufferers had been rescued—five dead. The voices of many could still be heard beneath the crushed mass, and the citizens, after a brief respite, were exerting themselves to reach the wounded and bruised bodies.

Who can expect that the notes shall all be cast for one man? Unanimity we shall doubtless have, but it will be produced by concession and compromise. Such will in all probability hereafter be the condition of the Republican party—bound together by common principles and sustained in harmonious action by a magnanimous sacrifice of local and personal feeling.

The selection at Baltimore of the candidate for the Vice Presidency, although not made with the same degree of unanimity, is, we have no doubt, in strict accordance with public expectation and the wishes of a large majority of the republicans of the Union. Col. Johnson is emphatically a favorite with the people—his popularity is perhaps beyond that of any other man. He has been long in public life, and his career has been eminently distinguished by acts which have won the respect and affection of his countrymen. As a soldier and a statesman, he is everywhere known and admired; the honors of war and of peace he has won, and now wears each with a plainness and simplicity every way becoming a republican patriot.

Of his competitor, Mr. Rives, we entertain a very exalted opinion. Had he been preferred by the convention, the choice would have been satisfactory to New-York, and he would have received an electoral vote with the same cordiality that it will now be given to Col. Johnson; but the democracy having spoken, we confidently anticipate a united and vigorous effort by the friends of both candidates—that the only rivalry will be which shall do the most to secure our triumph. In this state there is little to be done—New-York is no battle field—the enemy discomfited and routed, it is admitted by the Whig papers, “cannot be rallied.” We shall therefore have here an easy victory; but in every state in the Union where success may be considered at all doubtful, now is the time for our friends to prepare for the contest.—Utica Observer.

NATIONAL CONVENTION.
MR. STEVENSON'S ADDRESS.

To be called, unambitious, to preside over the deliberations of an assembly so numerous and enlightened as this, at such a time, and on such an occasion, is an honor that any man might be proud of. It would be an unworthy affectation in me, gentlemen, if I did not say I receive with deep sensibility this high and distinguished mark of your confidence and favor, and shall cherish it through life with feelings of profound respect and affectionate gratitude. On my part I can only promise an honest effort to justify the choice you have made, by the manner in which the duties of the station shall be performed. To enable me to do this with honor to myself and satisfaction to you, I shall need and expect your firm co-operation and considerate indulgence.

Although I am quite sure that you require no admonition from me as to the manner in which your proceedings and deliberations ought to be conducted, I shall be pardoned by you, I hope, in seizing the present to offer one or two suggestions for your consideration. The objects for which we have assembled, apart from considerations of personal honor and character, are of a nature which especially demand that the proceedings of the Convention should not only be conducted with deliberation, dignity and order, but marked by a spirit of the utmost harmony and union.

We are here, gentlemen, to perform a high and important duty to our country! Our venerable Chief Magistrate, following the example of illustrious predecessors, and with a patriotism worthy of himself, has already made known his determination, at the end of his present term, to retire to the abodes of private life, and a successor is to be appointed. As the period for the important election approaches, efforts will no doubt be made to divide and distract the republican party, and put in jeopardy, and possibly defeat, the election of a President by the people in their primary colleges—an event deeply to be deprecated by all who love their country, its repose and union. Under such circumstances, we must all be sensible that the union of our friends, and an election by the people, can only be secured by harmony and concert, and by an adherence to the good old usages of our republican fathers.

The New-York Star, one of the leading and most respectable of the papers opposed to Democracy, says, “that inasmuch as the question is now whether the Baltimore Convention or Congress shall elect the President, it goes for the latter.” The plain English of this is that the Whigs have no hope of electing any man in opposition to the nominations made by that convention, and intend now to direct all their efforts to the prevention of any election by the people! Mr. Webster will probably be run in Massachusetts, Mr. Clay in Kentucky, and Judge White in Tennessee, and probably in other western states, in the hope that each candidate may draw off some strength from the candidate of the Democratic party, in the particular section in which each is run. In this state the fag ends of all parties and of no party appear disposed to make a display in favor of Judge White. New papers have been started on the non-committal principle—others are committed only to oppose the Democratic party, and hold themselves ready to go for the candidate who can be made available in this state. The people of this state are Democratic—almost wholly; and they will never sanction by their votes those movements of the Whig party to take the election of President out of the hands of the people, and bestow it on the House of Representatives.

The harbor at the mouth of Calemink River possesses great natural advantages, and at a moderate expense may be made one of the best and safest upon Michigan. The Calimink River empties into the lake twelve miles below Chicago, and is navigable for steam boats and lake vessels of the smaller class for the distance of eighty miles, and for flat-bottomed boats and lighters one hundred and fifty miles. There is at all times [uncertain: six] feet water on the sand bar at the mouth of the harbor, and a depth of ten feet may easily be obtained.

The tributary streams are the Grand Calumet, Deep River, and Salt River, each of which are navigable for many miles, watering a widely extended and fertile region of the country. The borders of these rivers will furnish an almost inexhaustible supply of pine timber, and enterprising settlers are rapidly pouring in. A glance at the map will at once satisfy the reader that the mouth of the river must eventually become the market for the middle and northern sections of Indiana and a part of Illinois.

At the mouth of the river the village of Calumet is located, the mail road to Detroit passing directly through it. A county road has also been laid out and a free bridge is to be shortly constructed, crossing the river in the very heart of the village. Saw-mills will soon be in active operation upon the banks of the river in the timber country, and large quantities of timber, saw logs, &c. are now floating upon its waters.

We understand that a town has been laid out at the mouth of the Calumet River, and that some of the lots will be offered for sale in this place, in the course of the present month. A more favorable opportunity for investment has seldom been offered to capitalists.

TOWN MEETING.

Pursuant to previous public notice, a public meeting of the citizens of Chicago was held at the Presbyterian church yesterday morning. The meeting was called to order by Col. G. S. Hubbard, and E. K. Hubbard was chosen Chairman, and J. Curtiss was chosen Secretary.

The object of the meeting was stated by Col. G. S. Hubbard, and pertinent addresses were severally made by Messrs. Peck, Morris, Fullerton, and others; and on motion the following resolutions were unanimously adopted:

Resolved, That the situation of this town, abounding in nuisances, is such as to require immediate and energetic action on the part of its officers—that it is only by such action that our present state of health and prosperity can be preserved.

Resolved, That the present Trustees be requested to exercise the necessary powers to cleanse and purify the streets of the town, and to do whatever may be necessary for them to do to preserve the health of the citizens; and that they be directed to obtain the requisite funds on the faith of the town, or in any other way which may be feasible.

Resolved, That we will sustain the present Trustees in prompt and efficient action upon this subject.

Resolved, That if the Trustees find the same necessary, they be requested to call another public meeting of the citizens, to act upon this subject, and to take any powers which may be necessary and which they may be found not to have.

Resolved, That there be a committee of seven persons appointed to co-operate with the Trustees in effecting the objects of this meeting; and Messrs. J. Curtiss, Peck, B. S. Morris, A. N. Fullerton, A. Sweet, J. T. Temple, and B. King were chosen said committee.

E. K. HUBBARD, Ch'n.

Canal Tolls.—From the 1st to the 14th of May (two weeks), there has been received for tolls on all the State canals the sum of $116,527 87. From the 15th to the 30th of April, both inclusive, there was received $130,075 01. Total amount received in 30 days, $246,602 88.

It is thus seen that, from the opening of navigation, the receipts for toll have averaged 8,220 dollars per day for thirty successive days.

In 1831 the canal was navigable on the 16th of April; and up to the 14th of May there was received for tolls the sum of $225,143 77. In 1835 the canal opened on the 15th of April, one day earlier than in 1831; the rates of toll on merchandize and most other articles have been reduced 37 7-8 per cent, and yet the aggregate of tolls collected in four weeks exceeds that of 1831 by the sum of $21,458 11. Deduct 37 7-8 per cent from the rates, and it shows an increase of about $100,000 in the canal business for four weeks in 1835, compared with the same time in 1831.

In 1834 the receipts for tolls up to the 14th of May were less by $30,505 22; and in 1833, up to the same period, less by $51,580 than in 1835. In 1833 the merchandise rate was 25 per cent higher.

Instead of a falling off in the early spring business on the canals, as has been confidently predicted by some of the panic makers, the results of the first month of navigation show an unprecedented increase. These great results, however, are not accomplished without heavy expenditures. It is necessary to make the spring repairs and put the canals ready for navigation; but it costs from $1,500 to $2,000 per day to pay for superintending the canal and keeping them in repair.—Daily Albany Argus.
