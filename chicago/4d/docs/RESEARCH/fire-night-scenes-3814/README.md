## What the people in the streets did (by kind)

**Carrying.** "Sober men and women were hurrying through the streets from the burning quarter — some
with bundles of clothing on their shoulders; others, dragging trunks along the sidewalk by means of
strings and ropes fastened to the handles, children trudging by their sides or borne in their arms.
Now and then a sick man or women would be observed, half concealed in a mattress doubled up and borne
by two men. Droves of horses were in the streets, moving under some sort of guidance to a place of
safety." (A p. 734, White.) "The sidewalks were crowded with men, women and children, all carrying
something ... One woman carrying an empty bird cage: another, an old work box; another, some dirty,
empty baskets. Old, useless bedding" (A p. 732). On the North Side at dawn: "one man carrying the
rubber tube and broken standard of a drop-light; another was trundling a wheelbarrow on which was a
cook stove, while on his back was a huge feather bed. One woman had a live hen in her arms, several had
cats, and numbers had canary birds in cages. We met Dr. Tolman Wheeler pulling a trunk along the
sidewalk by one of the straps" (A p. 746). "Elegant ladies ... astonished themselves by dragging trunks
for a long distance ... Poor women staggered along with their arms full" (A p. 729).

**Wagons.** "Drays, express wagons, trucks, and conveyances of every conceivable species and size,
crowded across in indiscriminate haste. Collisions happened almost every moment; and when one
over-loaded wagon broke down, there were enough men at hand to drag it and its contents over the bridge
by main force" (A pp. 728-729). "Expressmen, and owners of every description of wagons, were extortionate
in their demands, asking from twenty to fifty dollars for conveying a small load a few blocks ... often
followed by howling crowds, who would snatch the goods from the wagons" (A p. 729). On the North Side,
carters "wanted fifty dollars now for moving a load. Having found a victim, they would stop midway, and
assess him again ... off went his goods into the street" (A p. 757). "The draymen and expressmen,
stimulated by the immense prices ... hurried their heavy teams ... the southern streets, leading to
Division, were also jammed with wagons, which occasionally caught fire" (A p. 756). White loaded "a
quantity of trunks and odd articles" into a wagon and took "a cage, containing ... 'a talented parrot'"
(A p. 734). Field, Leiter and Co.'s men carried goods from the lower floor into wagons (the jaunt's stop
10, `field-leiter` claim).

**Runaways and burning loads.** Horace White: "a horse came tearing down the avenue, with the wreck of a
buggy at his heels ... This was the only runaway I saw; and I was continually surprised at the sober,
matter-of-fact way in which the horses did their work, showing no sign of alarm" (A p. 730). Alexander
Frear, near the Court House and the St. James Hotel: "a runaway truck dashed her to the ground. Loads of
goods passed us, repeatedly, that were burning on the trucks" (A p. 736); his horse "shied, and ran into
a burning dray-load of furniture, smashing the wheel of the wagon" (A p. 736). And "the brute creation
was crazed. The horses, maddened by heat and noise ... neighed and screamed ... or stood with drooping
tails and rigid legs" (A p. 726; C and G print the same passage). Luzerne: horses "often lay down in
their harness under the rain of sparks" (L). So: one runaway in view, many burning loads, most horses
working steadily.

**At the places of the stops.**
- The Court House, about 1:30-2 a.m.: the prisoners, "all bareheaded, many barefooted, rushed into the
  street ... A large truck, loaded with ready-made clothing, was passing the corner of Randolph Street
  ... the ex-prisoners swarmed upon it, emptied it" (A p. 725).
- The Sherman House and the Tremont: "Women, half-dressed, and many of them with screaming children,
  fled out of the building. There was a jam in the doorway" (A p. 736, Frear).
- The LaSalle Street tunnel: "a struggling mass of humanity" (A p. 725); in the dark, "the oft repeated
  cry of 'Keep to the right'" (A p. 746); "a stream of fugitives issued in solid mass" (A p. 752).
- The North Side sands (Water Works, Ogden stops): "thousands of men, women and children, and hundreds
  of horses and dogs ... The grounds were dotted all over, at short intervals, with piles of trunks,
  chairs, tables, beds" (A p. 744); "Some persons drove their horses into the lake as far as the poor
  beasts could safely go, and men, women and children waded out and clambered upon the wagons ... the
  lake was lined with people who were standing in the water at various depths, from their knees to their
  waists, all with their backs to the storm of fire" (A p. 744). Arnold's gardener sat "upon the horse
  far out in the lake, ... holding on ... to the pony by its halter and to the cow by her horns" (A p. 748).
- Roofs: "men with buckets of water were stationed upon the roof" of the Water Works (A p. 742); "I went
  upon the roof of my house, and ordered the servants to pass me up buckets of water" (A p. 743). Goods
  buried in pits in the yards (A p. 749).
- Monday: buildings blown up with gunpowder at Harrison Street and Wabash Avenue (A pp. 735, 737, 739);
  water carts selling water on the West Side (A p. 753); crowds on the prairie.

**Not for the student tellings** (they stay in the 18+ jaunt or its text, by the owner's rulings): the
woman kneeling with her skirt burning and the runaway that struck her (A p. 736), the girl whose hair
caught (A p. 736), the dead on the sands (A p. 755), looting with violence.

## Firemen, engines and water (T-3830)

The owner, 2026-11-28: "would there have been fire wagons pulled by horses and would people be putting the
fire out with hoses? ... Or other people operating water wagons". Yes. From Andreas vol. 2:

- The department: "seventeen steam engines, fifty-four hose carts, four hook and ladder trucks, two hose
  elevators, one fire-escape, eleven alarm bells, and forty-eight thousand feet of hose" (A p. 702). The
  steamers were horse-drawn and their men rode; companies were named ("Little Giant" No. 6, "Chicago" No. 5,
  "R. A. Williams" No. 17, "John B. Rice" No. 10).
- At the barn, William Musham's "Little Giant" No. 6 was "in position at the corner of DeKoven and Jefferson
  streets" two and a half minutes after a still alarm; "they laid a line through Patrick O'Leary's lot, and
  sent a stream directly on the fire"; "For more than thirty minutes this engine battled alone"; "one man could
  not hold the pipe"; the men "were obliged to call for such aid as could be obtained, in carrying and holding
  their hose and pipes" (A p. 711). Christian Schimmels's "Chicago" No. 5 "took the plug at Forquer and Taylor
  streets", broke down, and its men "tore up the sidewalk and took boards from the fence near by to use as quick
  fuel"; then "the supply of coal gave out" (A p. 712).
- The engines "had all been working on the West Side; and they could not reel-up six hundred feet of hose each,
  and cross the river" in time (A p. 710). "After the Water Works burned, the firemen could do little good with
  their engines, except on the banks of the river": one engine and 7,500 feet of hose lost, fourteen engines
  after eleven on Sunday night, half the hose by daybreak (A p. 710). Firemen of "nearly every city, far and
  near ... came with their apparatus" (A p. 710).
- Saturday night (the West Side fire the night before): "A dozen leads of hose ... the pipemen stood their
  ground within a rod of the fiery tempest" (A p. 705); men "who carried portable fire-extinguishers ...
  directed their miniature engines on the saloon walls" (A p. 706).
- Water without the mains: buckets on roofs (A pp. 742-743), Field, Leiter's "own pumping engines" (A
  pp. 734-735), water carts on Monday (A p. 753).

So the barn stop gets the Little Giant and its two lines, the bank stops after 3:20 a.m. get engines
drafting from the river, and no engine works a hydrant after the Water Works fell. No engine model exists
yet; a period steam fire engine, hose cart and ladder truck are T-3830's to build.

## How the crowds moved and sounded

**Direction.** Away from the front and down the wind's flank, to the lake, the prairie, the bridges and
the tunnels; and against them, sightseers: "a continual stream of curious people from the distant
regions crowding eagerly forward to see the vast illumination. The counter-currents, as they met,
caused frightful mishaps and confusion" (G pp. 292-293). Bridges and the tunnel jammed.

**Pace.** Mostly a hurried walk under loads, with rests: "Every block or two we would sit down on the
edge of the sidewalk, and rest until the fire made us move onward" (A p. 746). Running where the fire
was close (the Sherman House, Michigan Avenue).

**Sound, and the "screaming" question.** The accounts disagree, and both are true at different places:
near the front and in the hotels, "A mob of men and women, all screaming and shouting, ran about wildly,
crossing each other's paths" (A p. 736, Frear on Michigan Avenue; also A p. 729); further off, on the
North Side at dawn, "Very little was said by anyone; there was no loud talking or shouting, though the
streets were crowded" (A p. 746), and "all were apparently stupefied into silence by the magnitude of
the disaster" (A p. 752). So the soundscape is a function of distance to the front: shouting, crying
children, horses and wagons near it; the roar of the fire, wheels and feet, and silence further away.

## Crowd dynamics, as it bears on what we draw

- **Speed falls with density** (the fundamental diagram: Weidmann 1993, about 1.34 m/s free walking,
  stopping near 5 people/m²; Fruin 1971's levels of service). Free running for an unladen adult is
  2.5-4.5 m/s (the gait layer's own range); a man dragging a trunk or under a feather bed walks at
  1.0-1.5 m/s. Our staged paces (`hurry` 2.2, `brisk` 1.75, `slow` 0.9 m/s) fit this.
- **Lanes form in counterflow** (Helbing and Molnár 1995, the social force model): two streams going
  opposite ways self-organise into lanes. That is the "counter-currents" of G, drawn as lanes, not as
  people passing through each other.
- **Panic at bottlenecks**: "faster-is-slower" and clogging at exits (Helbing, Farkas and Vicsek 2000,
  *Nature* 407: 487-490); herding (following the crowd rather than the best way out); Moussaïd,
  Helbing and Theraulaz 2011 (*PNAS* 108: 6884-6888) on how simple visual rules give lanes and crowd
  turbulence. For us: jams at the bridges and the tunnel mouth, not a smooth flow.
- **Steering at scale**: a flow field over the streets (continuum crowds, Treuille, Cooper and Popović
  2006) is cheap for hundreds of agents going to a few goals; local avoidance (RVO/ORCA, van den Berg et
  al. 2008/2011) only for the few near the eye. Our goals per stop are few (away from the fire, toward
  the lake/bridge), so a field per stop, read from the fire map's front (fire-map.js `minutesToFront`)
  and the street graph, is enough.

## Rendering a crowd within the budgets

What the town already has (T-3555, `renderers/web/js/crowd.js`): three tiers of one person. **Near**:
the whole skinned figure (humans.js), to about 28 m. **Middle** (28-110 m at full, 28-70 m at light):
one instanced body per dress, its LOD3 figure merged and clustered, animated on the GPU from a
vertex-animation texture (VAT) baked from its walk clip, one draw per dress in use. **Far** (to 600 m
full, 320 m light): impostor cards from one atlas baked from the same bodies (8 headings × idle and two
strides). Caps per tier (`CAPS`: full 160 middle / 1,400 far; light 60 / 500). Cross-fades in time, no
pop. This is the standard industry recipe (GPU instancing and skinning on the GPU, NVIDIA GPU Gems 3
ch. 2, Dudash 2007; impostors for the far field, Tecchia et al. 2002; Millán and Rudomín) and it is
already measured on a phone (docs/measurements/T-3555-crowd.json).

What the fire night needs beyond it (T-3816):
1. **A source that is not the people's day.** crowd.js reads `days.outdoors`; at 2 a.m. on the fire
   night the day has everyone indoors, so the streets beyond the staged few are empty. A fire-night
   source hands crowd.js synthetic people (place, heading, speed, dress, what they carry), stepped on
   the CPU along the stop's flow field, a few hundred at most, O(n) with a spatial hash for spacing.
2. **A running clip in the VAT** beside the walk (WALK_FRAMES more frames), and the speed driving the
   clip's rate, so a far runner runs and does not skate.
3. **Carrying baked into the shared body**: a carried pose (arm held, as human-actions.js setCarry holds
   it) and its load (a bundle, a trunk on its cord, a feather bed) merged into the LOD3 body as a few
   extra "dresses", so the middle tier draws a crowd with goods for the same one draw per kind.
4. **Wagons in the middle tier**: the parked-transport far levels (one instanced team) for the jams.
5. **Budgets**: the light tier keeps its CAPS and ceiling (people are never dropped: fewer and coarser,
   per the owner's 2026-11-10 ruling); measure at each stop on the UHD 630 class and a phone, and write
   the row to docs/measurements.

The near tier is what T-3814 does now: the staged figures each stop holds (whole people, picked and
named), carrying their goods with a held arm and a prop, loading a wagon, bearing a sick neighbour in a
mattress. A `tier: "desktop"` castmate is left out at Light, so a phone keeps the scene with fewer.
