# Gardens and fields of Chicago, 14 August 1812: research for planting every plot

Scope: every 1812 property in `data/sidecars/1812/`. Coordinates are local ENU metres (data/datum.json), worked out from each record's `placement` and `footprint`. Grades follow the project's tiers. **"Attested" applies only to what a quote below says.** Potawatomi fields and camps are outside this report.

## 0. Evidence that applies to every plot

Mid-August stage of each crop. All of these are `reconstructed` (M'Mahon plus ordinary horticulture): flint "Indian corn" tasselled, at roasting-ear or milk stage, with pumpkins and squashes netted beneath it; potato tops yellowing; onions with their tops falling over; cabbages heading and late savoys newly set; turnips just sown, so drills of bare soil with seedlings; dwarf beans in pod; pea haulm pulled up; melons and cucumbers fruiting; currant bushes picked over; spring wheat, where it was grown, already reaped, leaving stubble.

**Two cautions to read before planting anything:**
- **Not 1812 evidence.** Wau-Bun's "company gardens, well filled with currant-bushes and young fruit-trees" (ch. XVII) describes **1831**. Quaife's "Colonel's garden" court-martial (p. 163) is from **Detroit, 1811**. Neither puts fruit trees or a garden at Chicago in 1812.
- **The guard order.** Gardens close to the fort could be worked. Lee's Place, three miles off, probably was not; see §7.

## 1. Kinzie's kitchen garden (`kinzie_garden_1812`: paling, E 1136–1182, N 360.5–376, 46 × 15.5 m, gate on the south side)

**Attested.**
- Whistler 1808 puts the house "inside a fenced yard lettered as Mr Kinzie's kitchen garden" (trace no. 30). Quaife p. 166: "the house of John Kinzie, with outbuildings and a 'Kitchen' garden."
- Wau-Bun ch. XVII, describing 1831: "A fine, well-cultivated garden extended to the north of the dwelling."
- **New, and the strongest boyhood witness.** John H. Kinzie (born 1803, lived in the house 1804–12), as recalled in the Chicago Magazine of 1857 and quoted by Andreas 1884 v1 p. 76: "the garden and the shrubbery, the fence paling that surrounded it, and the green lawn at the front of the house". This supports the paling and **shrubs** in the garden. It is a memory spanning 1804–1830s, so the grade is `inferred` for 1812.
- Kirkland 1893, App. D: the house "surrounded by out-houses, trees, fences, grass plats, piazza and garden" (a compilation).
- Labour, August 1812: "Black Jim, one of the servants" (Wau-Bun ch. XIX). The household also had engagés. Who worked the garden is not stated.
- Conflict to pass to the structures thread: Andreas p. 92 has the 1816 school in "a small log building which stood in the back part of Mr. Kinzie's garden … formerly used as a bakery". That is post-war, but it would put the bake-house at the garden's north end, not close behind the house.

**Recommended layout** (plan frame: u east from the west paling, v north from the south paling):
- Walk: 1.2 m wide, running north from the south gate at u 22.4–23.6. Perimeter path 0.6 m.
- **North border** (v 13.3–15.3, full length): currants and gooseberries. This is the "shrubbery", graded `inferred` from J. H. Kinzie's recollection and from Ouilmette's currants next door.
- **South row of beds** (v 0.6–6.4), west to east: roots (onion, beet, carrot, parsnip); cabbages and newly set savoys; | walk |; potatoes; musk-melons and cucumbers.
- **North row of beds** (v 7.0–12.7): garden Indian corn with running beans on poles; a turnip bed freshly sown this week (bare drills with seedlings); | walk |; dwarf kidney beans and peas with the haulm pulled; squashes and pumpkins.
- Herbs (sage, thyme, parsley) on either side of the gate.
- No fruit trees: none is attested. If any are wanted, two or three young apple trees, `reconstructed`, on the grounds of Kinzie's Detroit connections. My recommendation is to leave them out.

**What makes it Kinzie's:** the largest and best-kept household garden, the shrub border, and a full kitchen range. Field corn was ground at his "Old Grist Mill Worked by Horses" (index no. 32). Corn came in by trade, so it did not need to be grown in the garden.

**Grades:** garden `attested`; paling and shrubs `inferred`; beds and crops `reconstructed`.

## 2. Ouilmette's garden (house E 1116.3–1122.0, N 344.9–348.3; six currants already in `data/flora/plantings/ouilmette_garden_1812.json`)

**Attested.**
- Wau-Bun ch. XIX, 17 August 1812: the sergeant "ventured to steal cautiously into the garden of Ouilmette, where he concealed himself for a time behind some currant-bushes", then "climbed up through a small back window". So the garden was behind the house.
- Andreas p. 92 repeats it: "It was in Ouilmette's garden that William Griffith … hid himself behind the currant bushes".
- Later, and not 1812 evidence: Ouilmette and Robinson "cultivated the field formerly used as the garden of the fort, raising there good crops of corn" (Andreas p. 92, 1814–16). It shows the household farmed corn.
- Household (record): Antoine Ouilmette, his Potawatomi wife, their children, and Mrs. Bisson. A Métis household.

**Recommended garden:**
- About 16 × 10 m at E 1109–1125, N 349.5–359.5. This keeps 1 m clear of the horse mill at E ≥ 1126 and takes in the existing currants.
- Two currant rows (the existing ones), plus 6–8 more bushes, dense enough to hide a man.
- Three hill rows of corn with beans climbing the stalks and squash between them.
- A potato block and an onion bed.
- No fence: Wau-Bun names none. If one is built, a low stake-and-rail, `reconstructed`.

**What makes it Ouilmette's:** smaller and plainer than Kinzie's, currant-heavy, and mixing hill-corn with a creole kitchen patch.

**Grades:** garden and currants `attested`; position behind the house `inferred`; everything else `reconstructed`.

## 3. La Lime's house (E 1099.7–1106.7, N 344.6–349.3)

**Attested:** nothing about a garden.
- The interpreter was killed by Kinzie before the evacuation (record; Kirkland App. F).
- Gordon 1910 p. 9: his grave in front of the Kinzie home, "Flowers were planted on it and it was kept in most beautiful order". Hubbard via Kirkland App. F puts the grave "two hundred yards west of the Kinzie house" inside "a picket fence". That puts it near E ≈ 970. It is not a garden; I am flagging it for whoever owns the grave.

**Recommended (`reconstructed`):**
- A dooryard patch of about 11 × 8 m behind the house, E 1095–1106, N 351–359.
- Planted in May and **neglected since June**: corn hills going to weeds, squash vines sprawling, purslane and lamb's-quarters between the hills.
- This reading is the household's own story (its head killed in June). It carries the caveat that nobody knows who lived in the house after him.

## 4. Coursoll's field / Le Mai's house (`coursoll_field_1812`: worm fence, 31.0 × 18.2 m, about 560 m², origin E 1025.8, N 326.25, rotated 330.5°)

**Attested:**
- Quaife p. 167: "a house and inclosed field north of the river, belonging to Mr. 'Coursoll.'"
- The draught letters the fence "Worm Fence".
- No crop is named. Le Mai "a half-breed, married to a Pottawatomie squaw" (Quaife p. 166).

**Recommended (`reconstructed`):**
- Indian corn in hills about 1.1 m (3½ ft) apart each way, roughly 400 hills, with pumpkins between every third hill. This follows the creole field mix "Corn, pumpkins, and spring wheat" (Brackenridge).
- One end strip, about 31 × 4 m, of reaped spring-wheat stubble with two or three shocks. This is the one place in the scene with a small-grain stubble.
- Laid along the field's rotated axes.
- No separate garden at Le Mai's.

**Grades:** field and fence `attested`; crops `reconstructed`.

## 5. Burns house (E 793.5–800.8, N 107.2–112.7)

**Attested:**
- No garden.
- Burns "was a discharged soldier who had made Chicago his permanent home" (Quaife p. 234), with a family and an infant born 7 April 1812 (Wau-Bun ch. XVIII).
- Kirkland 1893 ch. IV, imagining the view from the blockhouse in August 1812: "The north side of the river is all wooded, except where little garden-patches are cleared around the human habitations". This is Kirkland's reconstruction, not evidence. It supports the *form* of the plot.

**Recommended (`reconstructed`):**
- A stump-studded clearing garden of about 16 × 12 m behind the house, E 789–805, N 114.5–126.5, edged with brush and rail.
- Potatoes, corn hills, pumpkins, cabbages and beans. This is a soldier-settler's subsistence patch, rougher than any garden on the bank.

## 6. Lee's house on the lake shore (E 1127–1135, N −440 to −434.2; dry beach back, ground +2.4 m)

**Attested:**
- Andreas p. 92: "the widow of Mr. Lee (the former proprietor of the cabin and garden on the lake shore near the fort …)". Lee's town garden is therefore attested, at Andreas's tier.
- Cooper via Quaife p. 167: "a house a mile to the southeast of the fort, owned by a farmer who supplied the garrison with butter and eggs". Quaife: "The farmer mentioned by Cooper was probably Lee." That is an `inferred` identification.
- Lee "having received the contract to supply the garrison with provisions" (Quaife p. 212).

**Recommended:**
- A garden of about 20 × 16 m landward (west) of the house, E 1103–1123, N −446 to −430, behind a paling against blowing sand and stock.
- On the sand: musk-melons, water-melons, cucumbers and squash in hills, with potatoes, beans, cabbages and onions.
- A poultry yard of about 8 × 6 m north of the house and a small cow pen. Graded `inferred` from "butter and eggs", conditional on Quaife's identification holding.
- Check the terrain before seating; the beach must be dry there.

**What makes it Lee's:** a contractor-farmer's town garden on sand, with poultry and dairy.

**Grades:** garden `attested` (Andreas); poultry and cow `inferred`; layout and crops `reconstructed`.

## 7. Lee's Place / Hardscrabble (`lees_place_field_fence_1812`: 150 × 90 m; cabin centre about E −1653.4, N −4223.3, facing 151°)

**Attested:**
- Heald, 15 April 1812: "Messrs. Russell and Lee's cabin, in a field on the Portage branch" (Moses & Kirkland v1 p. 43).
- Currey: "a tract of land under cultivation"; "The products of the farm found a ready market at the fort".
- **A garden by the cabin.** Wau-Bun ch. XVIII: "as the corporal jumped over the small enclosure he placed his hand upon the dead body of a man". Moses & Kirkland v1 p. 44: "feeling his way into the little inclosure … finding the dog still keeping watch beside the body in the garden; that of Debou, the Frenchman."
- Cattle "standing among the haystacks on the opposite bank" (Wau-Bun ch. XVIII).
- **After 6–7 April:** White and Cardin (Debou) were dead, the soldier and the boy had fled, and the guard order applied. Nothing read says the farm was worked again in 1812. Moses & Kirkland p. 70 say the cabin was "afterwards, in 1817, fitted up" by Crafts.

**Recommended:**
- **The "little inclosure".** About 14 × 10 m on the **river side of the cabin**, from 1.5 to 11.5 m in front of the front wall, centred near E −1648.9, N −4231.4. Its position is `inferred`: the corporal reached the enclosure coming up from the canoes. It is a low pale or rail garden; in August it has been untended for four months.
- **The field: unplanted in 1812** (`reconstructed`, from the April killings and the guard order). Last year's corn hills show under a waist-to-head-high annual weed stand (ragweed, lamb's-quarters, foxtail, smartweed), with a few volunteer pumpkin vines. Around the garden itself, only perennial pot-herbs survive.
- **Alternative B, for the owner** to choose with `ticket.mjs ask`: the field was planted in May under guard and shows corn as at the forks. No source supports B.
- Tell the haystack thread: stacks seen in April, made from 1811's hay, would be eaten down or rotting by August.
- Sensitive: this is the site of the April killings, and the record already carries T-0472's review. Draw no bodies and no grave.

## 8. Commanding officer's garden: already planted

Nothing to add for 1812. Do not add fruit trees: the 1831 evidence does not apply.

## 9. Sutler's garden (`first_fort_outer_sutlers_garden`: E 1210.4–1225.1, N 104.3–138.8, 14.7 × 34.6 m)

**Attested:**
- Lettered "a Sutlers Garden", with one small house drawn inside it.
- Quaife p. 165: "Somewhat to the east, between the fort and the mouth of the river, was a smaller garden and an Indian graveyard."
- The 1812 sutler is not named in what I read. Wau-Bun ch. XVII names "Mr. John Dean, the sutler of the post", earlier.

**Recommended (`reconstructed`):** a market garden for selling to the soldiers.
- Walk running north–south down the middle. Beds 6 m wide on either side, cut into four blocks a side.
- Blocks: cabbages and savoys, onions, potatoes, cucumbers, musk-melons, beans and a fresh turnip sowing.
- Plainer and more of a production garden than the officers', with no small fruit.
- Keep the graveyard out of it: depicting it is the owner's question.

## 10. Company garden and public cornfield at the forks (`forks_company_cornfield_1812`: E 142.4–292.3, N −263.3 to −46.5, 8.03 acres, worm fence)

**Attested:**
- Draught: "Company Garden & public Cornfield Eight acres".
- Quaife p. 166: "a small field of eight or nine acres had been reduced to cultivation and made to serve as the company gardens and public cornfield."
- Meadow beside it: "good medow and supplyes the Garrison with hay" (Whistler's description, Quaife p. 164 n. 413).

**Recommended:**
- About **7 acres of corn** in hills at roughly 1.2 m (4 ft), rows square to the fence. Tasselled, at roasting-ear stage. Pumpkins between the hills (Brackenridge analogue).
- About **1 acre of company gardens** (about 64 × 64 m) in the field's north-east corner, nearest the bank path toward the fort.
- Mess plots in strips 4–6 m wide: potatoes, cabbages, beans, onions, and a drilled turnip strip just sown.
- Optional: a scatter of hay cocks on the meadow south of the field. Haymaking in July is `reconstructed`.
- Work done by soldiers on fatigue. Nobody drawn.

**Grades:** field `attested`; corn `attested` by the sheet's word; corn stage `inferred` from the month; division into corn and garden `reconstructed`.

## 11. Clark's house at the forks (E 127.6–132.3, N −92.4 to −87.5) and the discharged soldier's house (E 37.4–42.7, N 52.1–57.9)

**Attested:**
- Clark is "a man named Clark who was a cattle dealer" (Quaife p. 167).
- The other is "one occupied by a discharged soldier".
- No garden is attested for either.

**Recommended (`reconstructed`):**
- **Clark:** about 12 × 10 m north of the house, E 124–136, N −85 to −75. Potatoes, cabbages and pumpkins, plus a rail cattle pen of about 15 × 15 m. He is a cattle dealer, so the pen is what marks the household, not the garden.
- **Soldier:** about 10 × 8 m east of the house, E 44–54, N 51–59. Potatoes, a few corn hills, cabbages and beans, like Burns's patch but smaller.

## 12. Agent's house and factory (the draught's paled yards); interpreter's house

**Attested:**
- The draught draws the factory "with a paled yard lettered 'Paling' east of it", and the agent's house with "its paled yard east of it" (trace, outside_the_stockade). Neither yard is built yet.
- The yards' use is not stated.
- The factor Matthew Irwin wrote a report of the April attack (Quaife p. 212 n. 568), so he was at the post in 1812. In 1812 refugee families crowded into the Agency House (§0).

**Recommended (`reconstructed`):**
- **Factory yard,** E 1072–1090, N 210.5–224.5: a neat kitchen garden inside the 'Paling', a government official's household garden. Cabbages, onions, beans, salad stuff, herbs, a melon bed.
- **Agent's yard,** E 1072–1090, N 162–176: a crowded, makeshift vegetable yard, potatoes and cabbages, kept by the families sheltering there.
- **Interpreter's house (no. 19):** no garden. Its 1812 occupant is unknown, so leave the yard in grass.
- **Other buildings:** none for the shops, the bake-house and the stables. Whistler's **"Old field"** west of the road is fallow ground, not to be planted. Its origin, possibly an Indigenous or du Sable-era field, is a question for the owner or the Potawatomi thread.

## New sources worth adding as records

1. **M'Mahon, Bernard. *The American Gardener's Calendar*. Philadelphia: B. Graves, 1806 (first edition).** https://archive.org/details/americangardene00mmagoog. Public domain. August, pp. 451–453: turnips, savoys, dwarf beans, melons, clearing the haulm. It is closer to 1812 than the 1857 edition the record holds.
2. **Brackenridge, H. M. *Views of Louisiana*. Pittsburgh: Cramer, Spear & Eichbaum, 1814.** Bk II ch. V. https://archive.org/details/viewsoflouisian00brac. Public domain. Creole house-lots enclosed with "cedar pickets", gardens and orchards, and the common field's "Corn, pumpkins, and spring wheat". Comparative only.
3. **Gordon, Eleanor Lytle Kinzie. *John Kinzie, the "Father of Chicago": A Sketch*. 1910.** https://archive.org/details/johnkinziefather00gord. Public domain. La Lime's grave and its flowers (p. 9); the Lee's Place account.
4. **Chicago Magazine, 1857, on the Kinzie home (John H. Kinzie's recollection)**, as quoted by Andreas 1884 v1 p. 76 (andreas_1884_v1). Worth a passage record of its own: garden, "shrubbery", "fence paling". The repo has chicago_magazine_1857_kinzie_house_engraving for the picture, not this text.
5. **Andreas 1884 v1, pp. 76, 80 and 92** (existing record): passage claims for Lee's "cabin and garden on the lake shore", Ouilmette's garden, and the fort's south ground "enclosed and cultivated as a garden" (p. 80).
6. **Quaife, M. M. "Property of Jean Baptiste Point Sable." *Mississippi Valley Historical Review* 15:1 (1928), 89–96.** JSTOR 1891669. Rights uncertain (1928). The 1800 bill of sale: barns, horse mill, dairy and so on. Not read; it may list fields or fences for the Kinzie ground.
7. **Wilson, James Grant. *Chicago from 1803 to 1812*** (Surgeon Cooper's account; Quaife's n. 412). Not found online yet. It is the primary for "melons and other small fruit" and the butter-and-eggs farmer.

The full text of Hurlbut's *Chicago Antiquities* could not be fetched (archive.org returned 500), so nothing from it beyond what the repo already holds was checked.
