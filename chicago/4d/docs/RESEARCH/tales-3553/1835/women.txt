# Tales told by the women of the town, 1 July 1835.

## s35-tw-first-winter
roles: townswoman, lady
title: Our first winter

We came in October, on the last schooner of the year, and my husband had not built the house yet. We lived in a shanty of rough boards with a canvas roof, my husband and I and three children and a stove. In January the snow blew in through the cracks and lay on the quilts in the morning like sugar.

The lake froze out as far as you could see. No boats came for five months. No letters, no news, no fresh food. We ate salt pork and potatoes and corn bread every day from Christmas to April. In April the first schooner came round the point and every woman in town went down to the river to cry. I was one of them.

## s35-tw-shopping
roles: townswoman, lady, needlewoman
title: Shopping on Lake Street

When I came here two years ago there was one store worth the name. Now there are forty on Lake Street and South Water Street, and you can buy anything if you can pay for it: silk from France, tea from China, a piano from Boston, a bonnet in the latest New York style, only a season late.

But you cannot buy fresh milk, or a decent egg, or a vegetable that has not travelled a thousand miles. I keep my own cow behind the house and my own hens and a garden. My neighbours laugh at me. Then they come and ask to buy my eggs. I charge them twice what the store does. They pay it.

## s35-tw-boarders
roles: boarding_keeper, townswoman
title: Twelve boarders and one stove

I keep a boarding house. Twelve men, three to a room, two meals a day and a cold supper. I cook on one stove in a kitchen the size of a wagon. I bake twelve loaves every morning. I buy a barrel of flour every week and a barrel of pork every month and I scrub the floors on Saturday.

My boarders are clerks and carpenters and young lawyers, all of them going to be rich next year. They pay me three dollars a week, when they pay. I have one who has owed me since March. He tells me he has bought a lot that will be worth a fortune by the fall, and he will pay me then with interest. I have heard that before.

## s35-tw-laundry
roles: laundress, domestic, townswoman
title: Washing for the town

I wash for six families and two hotels. Monday I collect the bundles. Tuesday I boil. Wednesday I scrub and rinse and wring. Thursday I hang it out on the lines behind my house, and pray it does not rain, and pray the dust does not blow. Friday I iron with irons heated on the stove. Saturday I deliver.

On Sunday I rest, and my hands crack and bleed from the lye. I am paid fifty cents a dozen pieces. It is more than I could get anywhere else. My husband died last year of the fever. This is how I feed my children. I am not complaining. I am only telling you why my hands look the way they do.

## s35-tw-dressmaker
roles: needlewoman
title: The latest fashion

I am a dressmaker, and every lady in Chicago wants a dress like the ones in the New York magazines, with sleeves as big as hams and a waist as small as a wrist. The magazines arrive three months late, and so the fashions do too, and the ladies do not know it, and I do not tell them.

I learned my trade in Albany. Here I can charge double what I could there, because there are only three of us and two hundred ladies who suddenly have money. I have taken on two girls to help me. If this goes on I will have a shop of my own on Lake Street by next spring. A woman can do that here. Not many places she can.

## s35-tw-milliner
roles: needlewoman, townswoman
title: Bonnets

A bonnet tells you everything about a woman in this town. A plain straw one with a ribbon: a farmer's wife, just off the boat, going west. A silk one with feathers: a merchant's wife, three months in town and making sure everyone knows her husband has done well.

I make both kinds. I prefer making the plain ones, if I am honest. The farmers' wives are so grateful, and they hold the bonnet in both hands as if it were made of glass. The merchants' wives complain that the feathers are not tall enough. I make the feathers taller. They complain about the price.

## s35-tw-domestic
roles: domestic
title: Hired girl in a Yankee house
meet: rc_flynn_mary

I am a hired girl. I cook and clean and carry water and mind the children for a lawyer's family on the South Side. I sleep in the attic and I have Sunday afternoons to myself. I am paid a dollar and a half a week, which is more than my father earned in Ireland in a month.

The mistress is kind but she watches me as if I might steal the spoons. In Yankee houses they think every Irish girl is a Catholic spy. I am a Catholic. I am not a spy. Go and meet Mary Flynn, who works on the next street. She has a worse mistress than mine and a better sense of humour.

## s35-tw-cow
roles: townswoman, lady
title: The cow on the prairie

Every family that can afford one keeps a cow, and in the morning the cows go out on the prairie to graze, and in the evening a boy goes out to bring them back. Except the cows do not come back. They wander miles. They get stuck in the marshes. They get taken by wolves, or by people who are not wolves.

My cow, Beatrice, has a bell on her neck so I can hear her. Last week the bell came home without her. A man brought her back three days later and asked for a dollar for his trouble. I am almost certain he was the one who took her. I paid him. You must pick your battles in this town.

## s35-tw-schoolteacher
roles: teacher, townswoman, needlewoman
title: The school in the church
meet: watkins_john

There are more children in Chicago than benches to put them on. The first school was only three years ago, when John Watkins taught a dozen children in a little log building. Now there are several, in churches and storerooms and rented rooms, and still not enough.

I teach thirty children in a room meant for fifteen. They are from everywhere: Vermont, Ohio, Ireland, Canada, one little French girl who speaks no English at all. I teach them reading and arithmetic and geography, and they teach me how to break up a fight between a Yankee and an Irish boy without being knocked down. Go and see Mr. Watkins. He will tell you how it was in the old days.

## s35-tw-lady-dance
roles: lady, townswoman
title: A dance at the fort

When the officers at the fort hold a dance, every lady in town is invited, and there are four gentlemen for every lady, so you dance until your feet bleed. The music is a fiddle and a flute, and sometimes a soldier with a drum who cannot keep time.

At the last dance I danced with a lieutenant, a land agent, two lawyers, a doctor, a fur trader with a scar on his face, and Mr. Beaubien, who dances like a whirlwind and sings at the same time. My husband danced with nobody, because there was nobody left. He sat by the punch bowl and talked about lots. He enjoyed himself, he says.

## s35-tw-letter-home
roles: townswoman, lady
title: A letter to my mother

I write to my mother in Connecticut every week, and every week I try to make Chicago sound like a place she would approve of. I tell her about the churches and the school and the ladies' sewing circle. I do not tell her about the mud, or the drunks in the street, or the wolves, or the Indians camped on the edge of town.

She writes back and asks when I am coming home. I do not know how to tell her that I am not coming home. That this is home now, with all its mud. That I like it here, God help me. That I have never felt so alive.

## s35-tw-baby
roles: townswoman, lady
title: A baby born in Chicago

My baby was born in March, in our house on Clark Street, with the snow blowing against the windows. The doctor came through the drifts on horseback and arrived just in time to say congratulations. My neighbour, Mrs. Hamilton, delivered her. She has delivered a dozen babies in this town.

She is a Chicago baby. She will grow up here. She will never know a place without the lake. When she is old, I suppose, Chicago will be a great city, and she will tell her grandchildren that she remembers when it was a few wooden houses in the mud. They will not believe her.

## s35-tw-washing-lake
roles: laundress, townswoman
title: Water from the lake

The river water is filthy. Everything goes into it: the slops, the slaughterhouse, the stables. So the water carrier brings water from the lake in a cart with a barrel, and sells it by the bucket, door to door. A shilling a barrel.

I cannot afford a barrel every day for my washing. So I go down to the lake myself with two buckets on a yoke, three times a morning, a quarter mile each way. My shoulders are like a man's now. My husband says I could beat him at arm wrestling. I could. I have not told him so.

## s35-tw-sewing-circle
roles: lady, townswoman, needlewoman
title: The sewing circle

The ladies of the Presbyterian church have a sewing circle on Thursday afternoons. We sew for the poor, and for the missionaries, and for the new church we are trying to build, and we talk about everybody who is not in the room.

It is the only time in the week I sit down. It is the only time I talk to other women about something besides the price of flour. Last week we talked for an hour about whether it was proper for a lady to walk on Lake Street alone in the afternoon. We decided it was not. Then we all walked home along Lake Street alone. That is Chicago.

## s35-tw-irish-girl
roles: domestic, irish
title: Sending money home

Every month I send three dollars home to my mother in County Cork, sewn into the lining of a letter. It takes two months to get there. Sometimes it does not get there at all. My brothers and sisters eat because I work in a Yankee kitchen in a town at the end of the world.

My mother writes, through the priest, that the potatoes were bad again last year and that half the village has gone to America. She says I am a good girl. I do not feel like a good girl. I feel like a tired one. But on Sunday I go to Mass at the little church and I light a candle, and for an hour I feel like I am home.

## s35-tw-widow
roles: townswoman, boarding_keeper, laundress
title: A widow in a boom town

My husband died last summer of the ague, six weeks after we arrived. I had two children, a trunk of clothes, eleven dollars and a lot on the North Side that he had bought the week before he died.

Everybody told me to go home. I did not. I sold the lot in the spring for four hundred dollars, which is more money than I had seen in my life, and I rented a house and took in boarders. Now I have six and a hired girl. I am not rich. But I am not going home, either. This town gave me a second chance. I do not intend to waste it.

## s35-tw-fever-nursing
roles: townswoman, lady, domestic
title: Nursing the sick

In the summer, when the fevers come, every woman in town is a nurse. You go next door with a basket of broth and clean linen, and you sit up all night with your neighbour's husband while she sleeps, and the next week she does the same for you.

There are not enough doctors, and the ones there are often worse than none. So the women do it. We know which herbs ease a fever and which ones only make you feel better. We know when to send for the minister. I have sat with eleven people who were dying, in three years. I remember every one of their names.

## s35-tw-chickens
roles: townswoman, domestic, boarding_keeper
title: The hens and the pigs

Pigs run loose in the streets of Chicago. Nobody knows who owns half of them. They root in the mud and sleep under the sidewalks and eat anything that is dropped. They are the town's garbage collectors, my husband says.

They also ate three of my hens last month, and my whole row of beans, and a pair of my husband's boots that he left on the porch. I chased one down Clark Street with a broom in my nightgown. The neighbours still talk about it. I do not care. I got one boot back.

## s35-tw-native-neighbour
roles: townswoman, lady
title: My Potawatomi neighbour

When we first came, a Potawatomi family camped near our house on the North Side for the summer, and the woman used to come to my door with baskets of berries to trade. I gave her flour and needles. She gave me berries and maple sugar and once a pair of moccasins for my baby that were the most beautiful thing I have ever owned.

We could not speak to each other. We used our hands and laughed a lot. This summer she came again, and when I tried to ask about next year, she shook her head and pointed west, and made a motion with her hand like a long road. Then she gave me the berries and would not take anything for them.

## s35-tw-lady-house
roles: lady
title: A frame house with a piano

My husband built us a frame house last year, two storeys, white-painted, with green shutters and a picket fence, the finest on our street. He ordered a piano from Boston and it came round the lakes in a crate, and four men carried it up from the river.

It is out of tune. There is no one in Chicago who can tune it. I play it anyway, on summer evenings, with the windows open, and people stop in the street to listen. I am not a good player. But I am the only one on the street with a piano, and so I am the best. That is how it is in a new town.

## s35-tw-hotel-wife
roles: innkeeper, boarding_keeper, townswoman
title: The hotel keeper's wife

My husband keeps the hotel, they say. My husband greets the guests and drinks with them and tells stories. I keep the hotel. I cook for forty people, wash for forty people, make forty beds, and keep the accounts, because he cannot add.

I have four children and two hired girls and a cook who drinks. I sleep five hours a night. When a guest says what a fine house your husband keeps, I smile and say thank you. Then I go and find out which guest broke the window in room six.

## s35-tw-sleigh
roles: townswoman, lady, youth
title: Sleighing on the river

In winter, when the river freezes solid, everybody goes out sleighing. You hire a sleigh and a horse and race up and down the river and out onto the lake ice, with buffalo robes over your knees and the bells jingling. Young men race each other. Everybody shouts.

Last winter a sleigh went through the ice near the mouth of the river, where the current keeps it thin. The horse drowned. The two young men got out, soaked and freezing, and walked into the Sauganash looking like icicles. Mr. Beaubien gave them hot whiskey and played them a song about it. They are still telling the story. So is everybody else.

## s35-tw-mother-west
roles: townswoman
title: Going on west

We are only stopping in Chicago for a week. My husband has bought a quarter section on the Fox River, sight unseen, and we are going out there with a wagon and two oxen, five children and a cow. I have never seen it. He has never seen it. A land agent showed him a map.

I asked him what we will do if it is a swamp. He said we will drain it. I asked what if there is no water. He said we will dig a well. I asked what if the Indians come. He said they are going west, too, far beyond us. I did not ask any more questions. I am going to buy needles and thread and seeds and a Bible, and pray.

## s35-tw-kinzie-lady
roles: lady, townswoman
title: Mrs. Kinzie's parlour
meet: kinzie_juliette

Mrs. John Kinzie is the most interesting woman in Chicago. She came from Connecticut, married Mr. Kinzie, and went to live at a fort in the Wisconsin wilderness among the Winnebago, where her husband was the agent. She rode horseback through the forests in winter with a baby. She tells the best stories of anyone.

Her husband's family were here at the first fort, in the old war, and his mother told her everything that happened. Mrs. Kinzie says she means to write it all down someday. I hope she does. Go and call on her if you can. She will give you tea and tell you something that will keep you awake at night.

## s35-tw-bridget
roles: domestic, irish, townswoman
title: The lad from Galway

There is a lad from Galway who works on the harbour piers, and he walks past my mistress's kitchen door every evening and says good evening, and I say good evening, and that is all. For a month. Good evening, good evening.

Last Sunday after Mass he walked me home and asked if he could call on me. I said he could. My mistress says an Irish girl should not walk out with an Irish labourer, she should marry a respectable Yankee mechanic. I said, with respect, ma'am, I will marry who I like. She did not speak to me for two days. I did not care. He is calling on Sunday.
