# Personal tales of 1835: told by one named person, in their own voice, from what is known
# of them. The telling is reconstructed.

## p35-mark-fiddle
for: beaubien_mark
title: I keep tavern
meet: beaubien_jean_baptiste

I came here from Detroit nine years ago, following my brother the Colonel, and I bought a log house by the forks of the river and started keeping tavern. Then I built the Sauganash next to it, named for my friend Billy Caldwell, and painted it white with blue shutters, the finest house in Chicago for a while.

I play the fiddle. Not well, but loud, and long, and nobody leaves my house without dancing. I have more children than I can count without using both hands twice, and more guests than beds, and I have never turned a man away. If a man has no money, he eats anyway. My brother says I will die poor. I say I will die happy. Go and find my brother. He will tell you I am a fool, and then he will laugh.

## p35-jb-beaubien-fort
for: beaubien_jean_baptiste
title: I bought the fort
meet: greene_john

I came to Chicago more than twenty years ago as a trader, and I have lived on this land beside the fort for most of that time, in the old house I bought from Charles Lee's widow. So this spring, when the land office opened, I went in and entered the whole fort reservation in my name, as a settler living on it. All of it, for less than a hundred dollars. Here is my receipt.

The government says it is theirs. The major is very angry. The lawyers are very happy. I say: the law says a man may buy the land he lives on, and I have lived on it longer than any soldier now at the fort. Go and ask Major Greene what he thinks. Stand back a little when he answers.

## p35-josette
for: beaubien_josette
title: I was in the boat
meet: kinzie_john_h

Twenty-three years ago, in the summer of the old war, I was nurse to the Kinzie children, a girl of French and Ottawa family. On the morning the soldiers marched out of the first fort, I sat in the Kinzies' boat at the river mouth with Mrs. Kinzie and the little ones, and we watched the column go down the shore. I will not tell you what we saw.

Afterwards I married Jean Baptiste Beaubien, and I have raised his children and my own in this town ever since. The boy I minded in that boat, John Kinzie, is a grown man now with a business on the river. Go and find him. Ask him if he remembers the pockets I sewed into his clothes, with corn and sugar inside. He will remember.

## p35-johnh-kinzie
for: kinzie_john_h
title: The boy in the boat
meet: beaubien_josette

I was nine years old in August 1812, the eldest of my father's children, sitting in the family boat at the mouth of the river while the soldiers marched out. My father walked with the column. My half-sister rode beside her husband. Josette, our nurse, held my baby brother. I remember the sound more than the sight.

My father lived, by the help of his Potawatomi friends. He died seven years ago, here in Chicago. Now I am in the forwarding and commission business on the river, and I have been president of the town board, and I live a few hundred yards from where I sat in that boat. Go and find Madame Beaubien. She was Josette then. She still calls me Johnny.

## p35-juliette
for: kinzie_juliette
title: The agent's wife
meet: kinzie_john_h

I came from Connecticut as a bride five years ago and went with my husband to Fort Winnebago in the Wisconsin country, where he was agent to the Winnebago. I rode through the forests in winter with my baby, slept in bark lodges, and learned more in three years than in my whole life before.

Now we are in Chicago, which is a very different kind of wilderness. My husband's mother told me before she died everything she remembered of the old fort and the war, and I have it all in my head, and some of it on paper. One day I may write it down properly. Go and meet my husband. He was in that story, as a boy. He does not tell it. I do.

## p35-caldwell
for: caldwell_billy
title: Sauganash
meet: robinson_alexander

They call me Sauganash, the Englishman. My father was an Irish officer in the King's service, and my mother's people were of this country, and I have lived my life between the two. In the old war I rode with Tecumseh. Afterwards I made my peace, and the Americans made me a justice of the peace here in Chicago, which amuses me still.

Two years ago at the great treaty I spoke for the united bands, and signed. Some of our people say I sold them. I say I got them what could be got, and land in the west, when the alternative was nothing and a soldier's bayonet. Within three years they must go. I will go with them. Go and find Robinson. He signed too. Ask him if we did right.

## p35-robinson
for: robinson_alexander
title: Che-che-pin-qua
meet: caldwell_billy

I am Alexander Robinson. My father was a Scotsman and my mother was Odawa, and the Potawatomi call me Che-che-pin-qua. I have traded in this country since before the old war. In 1812, after the battle, my wife and I paddled the wounded captain and his wife all the way up the lake to Mackinac to get them to safety.

By the treaties I have a reservation on the Des Plaines, and I will stay there when the people go. Some of them hold that against me. I signed the great treaty two years ago, with Billy Caldwell, because I believed it was better than what would happen if we did not. I still believe it. Go and see Billy. He will tell you the same. He sounds better when he says it.

## p35-hubbard
for: hubbard_gurdon
title: The Swift Walker
meet: kinzie_john_h

I came to Chicago at sixteen, in 1818, a clerk for the American Fur Company, in a canoe from Mackinac. The Potawatomi called me Swift Walker, because I could walk seventy-five miles in a day. I married into the Kankakee band and traded all through the Illinois country.

In 1827, when there was a scare that the Winnebago were rising, I rode a hundred and twenty-five miles to Danville in a day and a night to bring the militia. Now I have a warehouse on the river and I pack pork and beef for the east, and I have more lots than I can walk to in a day. I knew John Kinzie the elder. His son is my friend. Go and find him. Tell him Hubbard says the canal will come.

## p35-calhoun
for: calhoun_john
title: A press across the lake
meet: hogan_john_s_c

I brought a printing press, type, ink and paper across the lake on a schooner two years ago, and I set up the Chicago Democrat in a little building on South Water Street. The first issue came out in November of 1833, and there were so few people in town who could pay for it that I printed for the whole Northwest, by mail.

Paper is my curse. It comes from the east, round the lakes, and when the boats stop in winter I run out, and I have had to print on whatever paper I could buy or beg. Every week I print the list of letters waiting at the post office. Go and see Mr. Hogan. Without his letters and my list, half this town would never hear from home.

## p35-hogan
for: hogan_john_s_c
title: The postmaster
meet: calhoun_john

I keep the post office, in a corner of my store, with a set of pigeon holes for the letters and a ledger for the money. When the mail comes, by horseback from the south or by boat from the east, the whole town crowds into my store to see if there is anything for them.

There are letters here that have waited a year. Letters for men who have died, or gone west, or never arrived at all. Love letters. Letters with money in them. Letters from mothers. I cannot open them, and I cannot throw them away. So they wait. Mr. Calhoun prints the list in his paper every week. Go and see him. Tell him the list is getting longer.

## p35-carpenter
for: carpenter_philo
title: The first druggist
meet: watkins_john

I came here in 1832 with a stock of drugs and medicines and opened the first drugstore in Chicago, in a log building at Lake Street. Quinine for the ague, calomel for the fevers, laudanum for pain, and a great many patent remedies that do more good to the seller than the buyer.

But my real work is the Sunday school. I started it to teach the children of this town to read, and to know their Bible, in a place where there was no school and no church. There are a great many children here and not enough teachers. Go and see Mr. Watkins. He taught the first school in Chicago. He can tell you how many children there were then, and how many there are now.

## p35-watkins
for: watkins_john
title: The first school
meet: carpenter_philo

I taught the first school in Chicago, three years ago, in a little log building on the North Side. I had twelve pupils, and only four of them were children of Yankee families; the rest were French and Potawatomi children of the old trading families, and none of them had ever been in a school before.

We had no books worth the name and no slates and no benches. I taught them their letters on boards with charcoal. One of the French boys learned to read in a month and then taught his mother. Now there are many schools and many teachers, and I am very proud. Go and see Mr. Carpenter. His Sunday school taught as many children as I did.

## p35-dole
for: dole_george_w
title: The first packer
meet: hubbard_gurdon

I came here in 1831 and opened a store, and in 1832 I slaughtered and packed the first beef and pork that was ever shipped out of Chicago. A hundred and fifty head of cattle and some hogs, salted in barrels, sent east by schooner. Everybody thought I was mad.

Now Mr. Hubbard packs more than I do, and others besides, and the barrels go out every week in the season. The prairie is all grass, and the grass will feed cattle, and the cattle will walk to Chicago. Mark my words: this town will be the great meat market of the West. Go and see Hubbard. He thinks he invented it.

## p35-clybourne
for: clybourne_archibald
title: Beef for the fort
meet: dole_george_w

I came from Virginia as a young man, before there was any town at all, and I drove cattle up from the south to sell to the fort. I built a slaughterhouse on the North Branch, which is still there, and I have supplied beef to the garrison and to the Indian payments for years.

At the great treaty two years ago I sold beef to feed eight thousand Indians for two weeks. I have never been so busy in my life. Now George Dole and Hubbard ship meat east by the barrel. I sell mostly to the town. There is room enough for all of us. Go and see Dole. Ask him who sold him his first steers.

## p35-ogden
for: ogden_w_b
title: Under water
meet: newberry_walter_loomis

I came here from New York only last month, to look after a tract of land my brother-in-law bought sight unseen. When I walked out to see it, I found most of it under water. I wrote to him that he had been badly sold.

And yet. I have been walking this town for a month now, and talking to everybody, and I begin to think I was wrong. There is a river, a lake, a portage, and a prairie that goes all the way to the Mississippi. When the canal comes, this will be the gate of the whole West. I may stay a while. Go and see Mr. Newberry. He has been here two years and he has bought more land than anybody.

## p35-newberry
for: newberry_walter_loomis
title: Buying land
meet: ogden_w_b

I came here two years ago from Detroit and I have bought land, and land, and more land. Some people say I am a fool. Some people say I am a genius. Both groups are waiting to see which, and so am I.

I do not buy lots on Lake Street for two thousand dollars. I buy acres on the North Side for a few dollars each, out where there is nothing but prairie, and I wait. The town is coming that way. I can see it from my window, one house at a time. Go and meet Mr. Ogden, the new man from New York. He thinks the way I do. One day he will own half of Chicago, and I will own the other half.

## p35-hamilton
for: hamilton_richard_j
title: Every office in the county

I came here in 1831 and they made me clerk of the county court, recorder of deeds, probate judge, and commissioner of schools, all at once, because there was nobody else who could write a fair hand. I had six jobs and one desk.

Now every deed for every lot in Chicago comes through my office. I record them all, in big leather books, in ink, by hand. Some lots have changed hands five times since the spring. I see the price go up every time. One lot I recorded at a hundred dollars last year was sold this week for three thousand. I wrote it down without a word. Then I went home and lay down for an hour.

## p35-kimberly
for: kimberly_edmund_s
title: A doctor in the mud
meet: egan_william_b

I came here in 1832 and I was one of the first doctors in Chicago. That summer the cholera came with General Scott's soldiers, and I learned more medicine in a month than in all my years of study, and buried more patients than I like to remember.

Now the ague is my daily bread. Every second patient shakes. I give quinine and calomel and tell them to drink lake water, not river water, and to keep their windows shut at night against the marsh air. Some of them listen. Dr. Egan, my colleague, thinks I am too gloomy. Go and see him. He will tell you nobody ever died of laughing.

## p35-egan
for: egan_william_b
title: An Irish doctor
meet: kimberly_edmund_s

I came from Ireland by way of New York and Newark, two years ago, and I took one look at Chicago and decided it was the most ridiculous and wonderful place I had ever seen. I have not changed my mind.

I treat the rich and the poor, the Yankees and the Irish, the soldiers and the sailors. I have set legs, delivered babies, pulled teeth, and once removed a fishhook from a judge. I sing at dinners and give speeches at the Fourth of July and argue politics with anyone who will stand still. Go and see Dr. Kimberly. He is a better doctor than I am. I am a better guest.

## p35-greene
for: greene_john
title: Commanding the fort
meet: beaubien_jean_baptiste

I command Fort Dearborn and its garrison, a few dozen men of the infantry, and I have commanded harder posts with fewer comforts. But I have never commanded a post where the enemy was a land office.

The town presses on my walls from every side. My soldiers desert to become carpenters. The taverns sell them whiskey at the gate. And now Colonel Beaubien has bought the fort reservation out from under me with a receipt from the land office. I have written to Washington. Washington has not answered. Go and see the Colonel. Tell him I said good day. He will know what I mean.

## p35-allen
for: allen_lieut_james
title: Taming the river mouth

I am an officer of the infantry, and for two years I have been an engineer too, because somebody had to build the piers at the mouth of this river, and I was here. Great cribs of timber, filled with stone, run out into the lake to keep the channel open.

The river is a stubborn creature. It wants to go south along the beach, the way the sand drifts, and close its own mouth. The piers push it straight out. In a big storm the lake throws everything back at us. One day there will be a harbour here as good as Buffalo. I will not be here to see it. The army moves its lieutenants about like chess pieces.

## p35-peck
for: peck_philip
title: The first frame store

I came here in 1831 with a stock of goods on a schooner, and I put up one of the first frame buildings in Chicago, a store on South Water Street with my living quarters above it. Before that everything was logs.

I have sold to the Potawatomi and the French, to the soldiers and the settlers, to everyone who has come through this town in four years. I have bought lots with my profits, and the lots have made me richer than the store. But I still open the shutters every morning at six, because a merchant who stops minding his store is no longer a merchant. He is a speculator. I do not want to be a speculator. Mostly.

## p35-mary-flynn
for: rc_flynn_mary
title: My mistress's spoons
meet: rc_mahoney_patrick

I am a hired girl in a Yankee lawyer's house. I came from County Cork with my brothers last year. I cook, scrub, wash, iron, mind the children and carry the water, from five in the morning until nine at night. I have Sunday afternoons and every second Thursday.

My mistress counts the spoons every night after supper. Every night. I have never taken so much as a crust. One night I counted them for her before she could start, and told her the number, and she went red and did not count them again for a week. Go and find Patrick Mahoney. He is from the next parish to mine at home. He will tell you I have a temper. That is a lie. Mostly.

## p35-mahoney
for: rc_mahoney_patrick
title: The hay wagon
meet: rc_flynn_mary

I came off the boat last summer with nothing, and on my first morning in Chicago I found a silver dollar in the mud. My mother gave me a holy medal when I left Cork, and I have had nothing but luck since.

On the third day I was working on a roof on Lake Street and I fell off it. Two storeys. And I landed flat in a hay wagon that was passing just at that moment, and the driver did not even notice, and he drove me halfway to Wolf Point before I sat up. Go and ask Mary Flynn. She saw it. She says it was one storey. She is jealous of my luck.

## p35-bardwell
for: rc_bardwell_otis
title: My horses
meet: rc_mahoney_patrick

I am a teamster. I have the best two horses in Chicago, Dan and Duke, bay geldings out of Kentucky, and I would not sell them for a lot on Lake Street. I haul lumber from the wharves and goods to the stores and baggage to the emigrant wagons, from dawn to dark.

In the spring I got stuck in the great hole on Clark Street with a load of bricks, and it took six men and two more teams to pull me out. I lost half the bricks. They are still down there, I expect, at the bottom of the mud. Some day a man will dig a cellar there and find them and wonder. Go and see Patrick Mahoney. He was one of the six. He fell in.

## p35-caton
for: caton_john_dean
title: The young lawyer

I came here two years ago, a young lawyer with no clients and a borrowed suit, and I tried one of the first jury cases in Chicago in a room over a store, with the jurors sitting on barrels. Now I cannot keep up with the work.

Every case in this town is about land. Who owns it, where its lines run, whether the man who sold it had any right to sell. I have had clients who bought the same lot from three different men. I have had a lot that turned out to be in the river. The law has not caught up with Chicago. I am trying to help it.
