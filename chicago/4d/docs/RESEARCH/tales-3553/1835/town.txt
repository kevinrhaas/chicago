# Tales told by the men of the town, 1 July 1835: newcomers, labourers, mechanics,
# merchants, clerks, lawyers and the men who keep the houses.

## s35-tm-lots
roles: townsman, merchant, gentleman, clerk, professional
title: A lot on Lake Street

When I came here two years ago a man offered me a lot on Lake Street for a hundred dollars and I laughed at him. A hundred dollars for a square of mud with a frog in it! He sold it that afternoon to a fellow from Ohio.

This spring the fellow from Ohio sold it for two thousand. I know because he bought everyone in the Sauganash a drink to celebrate, and he was buying for two days. Every man in this town is a speculator now. The blacksmith, the barber, the boy who sweeps the store. They buy on paper and sell on paper and the lots never see a building. It cannot last. I said that last year too.

## s35-tm-mud
roles: townsman, labourer, mechanic, merchant
title: The mud

You have not seen mud until you have seen Chicago mud in April. It is black and deep and it has no bottom. Wagons sink to the hubs on Lake Street. A man I know lost both boots in front of the post office and walked home in his stockings.

Somebody put up a board in the worst hole on Clark Street that said No Bottom Here. Somebody else, they say, stuck an old hat on top of the mud with a sign: Man Underneath. That is a joke, I hope. In July it dries out and the dust is worse. There are two seasons in this town: mud, and dust. And mosquitoes in both.

## s35-tm-erie
roles: townsman, labourer, mechanic, merchant, clerk
title: Coming by the Erie Canal

I came here from Vermont by every kind of boat there is. A wagon to Albany, then the Erie Canal to Buffalo, behind a mule at four miles an hour for a week, ducking every time the steersman shouted Low bridge. Then a steamboat from Buffalo, round through the lakes, past Detroit and Mackinac, eleven days with every berth full and half the passengers sick.

When we came round the point and saw Chicago, a woman beside me burst into tears. I thought she was happy. She said: is that all? A few hundred wooden houses on a swamp. I told her it would be a great city. I do not know why I said it. I think I wanted to believe it myself.

## s35-tm-drawbridge
roles: townsman, labourer, mechanic
title: The drawbridge

The town built a drawbridge last year across the river at Dearborn Street, so the North Side people need not take the ferry. It lifts in the middle for the schooners. In theory.

In practice it is always stuck, or always up when you want it down, or a schooner comes in too fast and knocks a piece off. The North Siders love it and the South Siders hate it, because they say it brings custom to the wrong side of the river. There has been talk of pulling it down. I would not be surprised if somebody did it some night with an axe. This is not a patient town.

## s35-tm-harbour
roles: townsman, labourer, merchant
title: The river cut through the bar

For years the river could not get into the lake. A sandbar lay across its mouth, and the schooners had to anchor out in the lake and send everything ashore in little boats through the surf. Then the government sent engineers to build piers and cut a channel.

Last spring the river did it for them. The ice broke up, the floods came down, and the water burst through the bar in a night. In July a schooner, the Illinois, sailed right in past the fort and tied up in the river, and the whole town came down to cheer. That was the day Chicago became a port. I was there. I cheered until I had no voice.

## s35-tm-sauganash
roles: townsman, labourer, mechanic, innkeeper
title: A night at the Sauganash
meet: beaubien_mark

If you want to see this town at its happiest, go to Mark Beaubien's Sauganash Hotel on a Saturday night. Mark plays the fiddle himself, badly and joyfully, and the whole house dances: soldiers, traders, Frenchmen, Yankees, half-breeds, lawyers, all in one room, stamping the floor until the candles jump.

He has more children than chairs and more guests than beds. If the house is full he puts you on the floor with a blanket, and if the floor is full he puts you in the barn. He never turns anyone away. Go and find him. Ask him to play the one about the girl from Detroit. He will play it whether you ask or not.

## s35-tm-wolves
roles: townsman, labourer, youth
title: Wolves on the prairie

Last winter the wolves came right up to the edge of town at night. You could hear them howling out on the prairie past the last houses, and in the morning there would be tracks in the snow right up to the pig pens.

Some of the young fellows organised a wolf hunt, on horseback, with dogs, and rode out across the frozen prairie whooping like Indians. They caught one wolf and lost two dogs and one man broke his arm falling off his horse. They called it a great success. The wolves are still out there. You can hear them some nights, even in summer.

## s35-tm-ague
roles: townsman, labourer, mechanic, clerk
title: The ague
meet: kimberly_edmund_s

Everybody in this town has had the ague. You shake until your teeth knock together, then you burn up with fever, then you sweat, then you feel fine, and the next day it comes back at the same hour, regular as a clock. Some men shake every other day for a whole summer.

The doctors give you quinine and calomel and bleed you. Doctor Kimberly says it comes from the marsh air. Old French settlers say it is because we drink the river water. The Potawatomi have their own cures. I have tried all of them. The ague does not care. Go and see the doctor if you start to shake. He will tell you it is nothing. He will be wrong.

## s35-tm-balloon
roles: mechanic, townsman, labourer
title: Houses made of sticks
meet: snow_george_w

The old way to build a house is with great heavy timbers, cut and pegged together, and you need a dozen men and a week to raise the frame. Here they have a new way. Thin boards, two inches by four, nailed together with machine-made nails, no pegs, no joints. Two men can put up a house in a few days.

The old carpenters laughed and said a wind would blow it away like a balloon. So they call it a balloon frame. But the balloons are still standing, and there are hundreds of them now. George Snow says he thought of it. Others say it was the man who built the Catholic church. Find Mr. Snow if you like. He will tell you all about it, at length.

## s35-tm-ships
roles: townsman, labourer, merchant, clerk
title: Count the sails

This summer I have counted the sails coming in from the lake, from the top of the hill by the fort. Some days there are three, some days ten. Schooners from Buffalo and Detroit and Cleveland, full of people and barrels and boxes. Steamboats, smoking like chimneys. Every one of them goes back nearly empty, because Chicago has nothing yet to sell.

That will change, the merchants say. When the canal is dug, all the grain and pork of the Illinois country will come through here and go east on these same boats. I do not know about that. I know that I counted eleven sails yesterday, and that they were all full of people like me, coming to get rich.

## s35-tm-land-office
roles: townsman, merchant, gentleman, professional, clerk
title: The land office sale

The government land office opened here this spring, and in June they began to sell the public land around Chicago at auction. You never saw such a crowd. Men came from St. Louis and Detroit and New York with saddlebags full of money, and they bid against each other on the courthouse steps like boys at a cockfight.

There were men who had squatted on their claims for years, with cabins and fences, and they stood by with rifles to make sure nobody outbid them on their own land. Nobody did. There is a sort of law here that is not in any book: you do not bid against a man on his own claim. Everybody knows it.

## s35-tm-canal
roles: townsman, labourer, merchant, irish
title: The canal is coming

They have been talking about a canal from Chicago to the Illinois River since before I was born. Joliet talked about it, they say, a hundred and sixty years ago. Now the legislature has passed a law to borrow money to build it, and everybody says the digging will start next year.

When it comes, there will be thousands of Irishmen with shovels, and a road of water from the lake to the Mississippi, and Chicago will be the door between them. That is why everyone is buying lots. They are not buying mud. They are buying the canal. If it does not come, they are buying mud.

## s35-tm-newspaper
roles: townsman, merchant, professional, clerk
title: The Democrat
meet: calhoun_john

When I first came, there was no newspaper. You heard the news from the last boat, and it was a month old and half wrong. Then John Calhoun brought a printing press across the lake on a schooner and started the Chicago Democrat, two years ago this winter.

It comes out once a week, four pages, and it is full of advertisements for lots and patent medicines and letters waiting at the post office, and long angry pieces about General Jackson. Paper is so scarce that some weeks he has to print on whatever he can find. Go and find Mr. Calhoun at the print shop. He will be covered in ink and complaining about the price of paper.

## s35-tm-letters
roles: townsman, labourer, clerk, mechanic
title: A letter at the post office
meet: hogan_john_s_c

Every week the Democrat prints the list of letters waiting at the post office, and every week I look for my name. A letter from home costs twenty-five cents to collect, and the man who gets it pays, not the man who sends it. Some letters wait for months because nobody can afford them.

Mr. Hogan keeps the post office in his store. He knows who is waiting for what, and he knows who never comes to collect. He told me once there is a letter that has been there for a year for a man nobody has ever heard of. He keeps it on a shelf, just in case. Go and ask him about it. He likes a mystery.

## s35-tm-forks
roles: townsman, labourer, mechanic
title: Wolf Point

Where the river forks into the north and south branches, there is a point of land they call Wolf Point. There used to be wolves, they say. Now there are taverns. The first in Chicago was there, and the first store, and the first church meetings were held in a tavern because there was nowhere else.

Before the town was laid out on the South Side, Wolf Point was the town. Now the South Siders call it the old end of town. The Point people do not care what anyone calls them. They were here first, and they will tell you so.

## s35-tm-mosquitoes
roles: townsman, labourer, mechanic, youth
title: Mosquitoes

In July the mosquitoes come up off the marshes in clouds so thick you can hear them like a hum in the air, and they bite through your shirt, your trousers, your boots. You burn green wood in a smudge pot in the doorway to keep them out, and then you cannot breathe for the smoke, and they come in anyway.

The Potawatomi rub themselves with bear grease. It works, I am told, but you smell like a bear. My wife says she would rather be bitten than smell like a bear. I told her she could have the choice. She took the bites. I took the grease.

## s35-tm-fire
roles: townsman, mechanic, labourer
title: Fire in a wooden town

This whole town is made of wood. Wooden houses, wooden stores, wooden sidewalks, wooden barns, all jammed together along Lake Street with no space between. And every one of them has a stove and a candle and a pipe-smoker inside.

Last winter a store on Water Street caught fire at night, and every man in town came running with a bucket. We made a line from the river and passed the buckets hand to hand, and we saved the buildings either side. The store burned to the ground. Somebody said it was a miracle the whole town did not go. I said, give it time. Nobody laughed.

## s35-tm-carpenter
roles: mechanic
title: A carpenter can name his price

If you can drive a nail, you can make your fortune in Chicago this summer. Every man who has bought a lot wants a building on it yesterday. I have more work than I can do. I charge two dollars a day and they pay it, and complain, and then a fellow down the street offers three dollars and I go to him.

I came here with a chest of tools and a debt. In one year I have paid the debt and bought a lot. I live in a room behind my shop with two other men. We sleep in shifts. That is how you get rich in Chicago. Work, and do not sleep, and do not think about the future too much.

## s35-tm-blacksmith
roles: mechanic, labourer
title: The blacksmith's day

I am at the forge at five, and I do not stop until dark. Horseshoes, wagon tyres, nails, hinges, chains, ploughshares for the farmers going west. Every emigrant wagon that comes through needs something mended. Some of them need everything mended.

The Potawatomi bring me their gun locks and their kettles too. They pay in furs, or venison, or sometimes in silver brooches, which I give to my wife. She has quite a collection now. When the Potawatomi go west, and they will soon, I will miss their custom. They are better at paying than the Yankees.

## s35-tm-merchant-goods
roles: merchant, clerk
title: Goods from New York

Everything I sell comes from New York. Cloth, tea, sugar, nails, hats, shoes, crockery, books. It comes up the Hudson and along the Erie Canal and across the lakes, and by the time it gets here it costs twice what it cost in New York, and I sell it for three times.

That sounds like a fortune. It is not. Half my goods are damaged on the way. A third of my customers buy on credit and never pay. And every month a new store opens on Lake Street selling the same things for a penny less. Still, I would not go back. In New York I was a clerk. Here I am a merchant. That is worth a lot of damaged crockery.

## s35-tm-clerk
roles: clerk, youth
title: Sleeping under the counter

I am a clerk in a dry goods store on Lake Street. I sleep under the counter on a buffalo robe, with a pistol beside me in case of thieves. I open the shutters at six and close them at nine at night. I am paid a hundred dollars a year and my keep.

Every clerk in this town is going to be a merchant. We all say so. We save our money and we learn the trade and we talk about the store we will open, with our own name on the sign. Some of us will do it. Most of us will go home with the ague and a debt. I am going to do it. Everybody says that.

## s35-tm-lawyer
roles: professional, gentleman
title: A town full of lawyers
meet: caton_john_dean

When I came here there were two lawyers in Chicago. Now there are a dozen, and every one of us is busy. That is what land does. Every time a lot changes hands somebody's title is wrong, somebody's boundary is in the river, somebody's deed was signed by a man who never owned it.

I spend my days in the courthouse and my nights reading. My clients pay me in lots, mostly, which means I am rich on paper and poor in my pocket, like everyone else. Mr. Caton is the best of us, though do not tell him I said so. Go and find him. He will be arguing with somebody.

## s35-tm-doctor
roles: professional, gentleman
title: Doctoring in a new town
meet: egan_william_b

A doctor in Chicago treats everything: fevers, broken bones, gunshot wounds, babies, cholera, drunkenness, and wounds from fights over lots. I ride out to farms twenty miles away in the middle of the night through mud to my horse's belly.

Doctor Egan came here from Ireland by way of New York, and he is the best company in town. He can sing, he can tell a story, he can recite poetry by the yard, and he can set a leg while doing all three. Find him if you need a laugh. Do not find him if you need a quiet evening.

## s35-tm-cholera
roles: townsman, professional, soldier
title: The summer of the cholera

Three summers ago, in the Black Hawk war, General Scott brought his soldiers here on steamboats from the east, and they brought the cholera with them. Men who were well at breakfast were dead by supper. They turned the fort into a hospital and buried the dead in trenches on the lake shore.

The settlers who had come into the fort for safety from the Indians ran out of it again for safety from the sickness. Some of them camped on the prairie for weeks. Everybody who was here then has a name they do not say any more. If you see an old settler go quiet when someone mentions 1832, that is why.

## s35-tm-black-hawk
roles: townsman, labourer, canadien
title: When the settlers ran to the fort

Three years ago, when Black Hawk crossed the Mississippi with his people, every farmer in northern Illinois packed his wagon and ran to Chicago. Hundreds of them, with their cattle and children and chickens, all crammed into the fort and around it. There were families sleeping under wagons on the parade ground.

Black Hawk never came anywhere near Chicago. He went up the Rock River and the army chased him into the Wisconsin country and destroyed his people at the Bad Axe. But the settlers who came here saw the lake and the river and the prairie, and some of them stayed. So in a way, Black Hawk built this town.

## s35-tm-pork
roles: merchant, townsman, labourer
title: Pork and beef
meet: dole_george_w

George Dole packed the first beef and pork in Chicago, three years ago, slaughtering the cattle that came in from the prairie and salting the meat in barrels to sell to the fort and ship east. People laughed at him. Who would buy beef from Chicago?

Now there are other packers, and Mr. Hubbard has a great warehouse for it, and the barrels go out on every schooner. Mark my words: one day this town will be known for its meat. The prairie grows grass, the grass grows cattle, and the cattle walk to Chicago. Go and find Mr. Dole. He will tell you exactly how many barrels he packed the first year. He remembers every one.

## s35-tm-butcher
roles: mechanic, merchant, labourer
title: The slaughterhouse on the North Branch
meet: clybourne_archibald

Archibald Clybourne keeps his slaughterhouse out on the North Branch, away from the town, thank heaven. He is from Virginia, and he has been here since before the town, driving cattle in from the south and butchering them for the fort and for the Indian payments.

When the wind is from the north you know exactly where his business is. But he is a good man, and an honest one, and his beef is the best in Illinois. Go and find him, if you have a strong stomach. He will tell you how to judge a steer by its walk.

## s35-tm-teamster
roles: labourer, townsman
title: Hauling on the prairie roads
meet: rc_bardwell_otis

A teamster in Chicago hauls everything: lumber from the wharves, goods to the stores, emigrants' baggage to the wagons going west. In spring the roads are mud to the axle, and in summer the dust gets into everything, your teeth, your bread, your sleep.

Otis Bardwell drives the best team of horses in town and he will tell you so. He has been stuck in the mud on every street in Chicago at least once and he knows every hole by name. If you want to see him lose his temper, ask him about the Clark Street hole. If you want to see him smile, ask about his horses.

## s35-tm-wages
roles: labourer, irish, townsman
title: A dollar a day

I came here because they said a labouring man could get a dollar a day in Chicago, which is twice what I got in New York. It is true. I dig foundations, I carry lumber, I unload schooners, I do whatever anybody pays for. A dollar a day.

But a room costs three dollars a week and a meal costs a quarter and flour costs twice what it does at home, so I am not as rich as I expected. Still, I have saved forty dollars since spring. When I have a hundred I am going to buy a lot. Everyone says the lots will double. Everyone cannot be wrong. Can they?

## s35-tm-emigrants
roles: townsman, labourer, mechanic, merchant
title: The wagons going west

Every week the wagons go through. Whole families, with everything they own piled up under a canvas cover, the father walking beside the oxen, the mother on the seat with the baby, the children running behind, the cow tied to the tail. They come off the schooners and buy what they need and go out across the prairie toward the Fox River and the Rock River and Iowa.

I watch them go and I think: that was me, a year ago. And I think: one day those farms will grow wheat and send it back here to be sold. Chicago does not grow anything itself. It is a door. Everything that goes west, and everything that comes back, goes through here.

## s35-tm-newcomer
roles: townsman, merchant, gentleman
title: The newcomer from New York
meet: ogden_w_b

A gentleman came from New York last month, William Ogden, to look after a great piece of land his brother-in-law bought here, sight unseen. When he walked out to see it, they say, he found it was mostly under water. He went quite pale.

He is a sharp one, though. I would not bet against him. He has been walking round the town for a month with a notebook, looking at everything, asking everybody questions. Find him and tell him what you know. He will listen as if you were the only man in Chicago. That is a rare gift.

## s35-tm-temperance
roles: townsman, merchant, gentleman, professional
title: Whiskey and the church
meet: carpenter_philo

This town has more taverns than churches by about twenty to one. Whiskey is cheap and water is dirty, so men drink. Some of them drink until they lie in the mud of Lake Street, and the pigs come and sniff at them.

Philo Carpenter keeps the drugstore, and he is the most religious man in Chicago. He started a Sunday school and he wants everybody to sign the pledge and give up drink. Some of us have. Some of us signed it in the morning and drank it away by the evening. Go and see Mr. Carpenter. He will be very kind to you, and he will hand you a pamphlet.

## s35-tm-printer
roles: mechanic, professional, clerk
title: Setting type by candlelight

I set type for the newspaper. Every letter of every word, picked out of the case by hand, backwards, and locked into the frame. A good compositor sets a thousand letters an hour. I am a good compositor.

On the night before the paper comes out we work until three in the morning by candlelight, inking and pulling the press, two hundred copies, four hundred, until our arms ache. Then I walk home in the dark with ink on my hands and the smell of it in my clothes. My landlady says I smell like a newspaper. I tell her I am one.

## s35-tm-innkeeper
roles: innkeeper
title: Every bed full

Every boat brings more people than this town has beds. I have twelve rooms and I put three men to a bed and six to a floor, and still I turn people away. They sleep in the barn, in the woodshed, on the porch. One man slept in a barrel last week.

I charge what I like, and they pay it. Some of them grumble. I tell them: go to the next house, and they come back an hour later because the next house is fuller than mine. This is the best business in Chicago, while it lasts. When the boom ends, the hotels will be the first to know.

## s35-tm-tavern-talk
roles: innkeeper, townsman
title: What you hear in a tavern

Keep a tavern long enough and you know everything about everybody. Who bought which lot, who sold it, who owes money to whom, who is courting whose daughter, who has the ague, who is drinking too much, who is going west.

I do not repeat any of it. That is the first rule of a tavern keeper. The second rule is to keep the whiskey and the fighting on different sides of the room. The third rule is never to lend money to a land speculator. I have broken the third rule more times than I can count.

## s35-tm-surveyor
roles: professional, mechanic
title: Drawing lines on the prairie

I am a surveyor. I walk out on the prairie with a chain and a compass and I draw lines where there are no lines. Here is a street, here is a lot, here is a corner. Then a man buys the lot, and builds on it, and my line becomes a real thing, a wall, a fence, a quarrel.

The town was first laid out on paper five years ago by a man named Thompson, for the canal commissioners. Every street in Chicago today is on his map. Some of them are still only on his map. The rest of the world is catching up with the paper, one lot at a time.

## s35-tm-fort-land
roles: townsman, canadien, merchant
title: Who owns the fort's land?
meet: beaubien_jean_baptiste

The land around the fort belongs to the government, everybody knows that. But Jean Baptiste Beaubien has lived on part of it for years, and this spring he went into the land office and bought the whole fort reservation, all of it, for less than a hundred dollars, under the law that lets a settler buy the land he lives on.

The government says he cannot do that. He says he has done it, and here is his receipt. It will go to the courts, and the lawyers will get rich, and in the meantime the soldiers drill on Beaubien's land. Go and ask the Colonel about it. He will laugh and show you the receipt.

## s35-tm-old-settler
roles: townsman, gentleman
title: I remember when there was nothing here

When I came here in 1829 there were a dozen houses and the fort. Everybody knew everybody. There were more Indians than whites and more French than Yankees. We had dances at the Kinzie house and the fort, and the only road was the Indian trail.

Now there are three thousand people and I do not know half the faces on Lake Street. Everybody is in a hurry. Everybody is buying and selling. Nobody has time for a dance. I am not complaining. I own four lots and they have made me richer than I ever dreamed. But I miss the old place, sometimes. I miss knowing everybody.

## s35-tm-gentleman-bank
roles: gentleman, merchant, professional
title: Money made of paper

There is not enough real money in this town. Gold and silver are scarce as diamonds. So everybody pays in paper: bank notes from Michigan and Ohio and Indiana banks, some of them good, some of them worth half, some of them printed by banks that do not exist.

A merchant keeps a little book that tells him which notes are good this week. A man can be rich on Monday and poor on Friday because a bank in Detroit has failed. General Jackson has made war on the national bank, so now every little bank prints its own money. It is madness. But it is profitable madness, so nobody complains.

## s35-tm-steamboat
roles: townsman, labourer, merchant
title: The steamboat whistle

When a steamboat comes in, the whole town knows it. You hear the whistle from far out on the lake, and then you see the smoke, and every boy in Chicago runs down to the river mouth to watch her come in past the piers.

Then the passengers come ashore, pale and seasick, with their trunks and their children and their dreams, and the hotel keepers fight over them like gulls over a fish. I watched it from the pier last week. A man stepped off the boat, looked around at the mud and the shanties, and asked the nearest fellow where the city was. The fellow said: you are standing in it.

## s35-tm-fort-neighbours
roles: townsman, labourer, mechanic
title: The fort at the end of the street

Fort Dearborn sits at the end of the town by the lake, its pickets and two blockhouses, and the flag over it. The soldiers come into town on their days off and spend their pay in the taverns, and the officers come to the dances.

There are old settlers who remember the first fort, the one that was burned in the war. They say it stood in the same place. They say the people who were killed are buried somewhere along the lake shore to the south, and nobody knows exactly where. When the wind blows in from the lake on a summer night and the sand moves, sometimes, they say, you find things.

## s35-tm-revival
roles: townsman, townswoman, gentleman, lady
title: Preaching in a warehouse

There was no church in Chicago until two years ago. Before that the preachers held meetings wherever they could: in a tavern, in a warehouse, in the fort's carpenter shop. Now there are a handful of congregations, Presbyterian, Baptist, Methodist, Episcopal and Catholic, all trying to build churches as fast as the taverns.

The Catholics built theirs in a few weeks, of light boards nailed together, and everybody said it would fall down. It did not. The Methodists preached on the street corner last Sunday and a man drove his cattle right through the congregation. The preacher did not stop. Neither did the cattle.
