# Tales told by the soldiers of Fort Dearborn's garrison and their officers, 1 July 1835.

## s35-so-boredom
roles: soldier
title: Nothing to fight

I joined the army to see a war. They sent me to Chicago, where the last war was twenty-three years ago and the next one is nowhere in sight. We drill in the morning, we mend the pickets in the afternoon, we stand guard at night, and in between we watch the town grow.

The town is more interesting than the fort. Every day there are new buildings, new people, new taverns. On our day off we go into Lake Street and spend a month's pay in an afternoon. The sergeant says the town is a worse enemy than the Indians ever were. He may be right.

## s35-so-old-fort
roles: soldier
title: The old fort's bones

This fort was built in 1816, after the first one was burned in the war. They built it on the same ground, with the same plan, the old men say. Two blockhouses, a palisade, barracks, a magazine. Some of the logs in the stable are supposed to be from the first fort, though nobody can prove it.

When I am on night watch in the south-east blockhouse I look down the shore toward where the massacre was. There is nothing there now but sand and grass. But the old French settlers say that when the wind is right, you can hear the drum. I have never heard it. I do not walk down there at night, all the same.

## s35-so-deserters
roles: soldier
title: Why men desert

Every month a man or two deserts. It is too easy. The town is right there, with its taverns and its land agents, and a man who can carry a hod or drive a team can earn more in a week in Chicago than the army pays him in a month.

So a fellow goes out on pass and does not come back, and the next we hear he is digging foundations on Lake Street under another name, or he has gone west with the wagons. The officers send out a sergeant and a corporal to find him, and they come back with nothing and a sore head from the Sauganash. I will not desert. But I understand the ones who do.

## s35-so-irish-recruit
roles: soldier, irish
title: The Irish in the ranks

Half the company is Irish. We came over to New York with nothing, and the recruiting sergeant found us on the docks and offered us food and a coat and five dollars a month to go and see the West. Many of us took it.

The Yankee soldiers call us Paddies and mock our Mass. The Irish soldiers call them psalm-singers and steal their tobacco. But when it comes to a fight in a tavern, the company stands together, Paddies and psalm-singers both, against the whole town. That is the army. You can hate a man in the morning and fight for him at night.

## s35-so-cholera-graves
roles: soldier
title: The cholera graves

Three years ago, in the Black Hawk war, General Scott's men came here by steamboat with the cholera on board. The fort became a hospital. Men died so fast they buried them in trenches on the lake shore, without coffins, in the night.

I was not here then. But the old sergeant was, and he showed me where the graves are, near the lake below the fort. He says when the lake is high it washes the sand away and bones come up. He says he has reburied more than one. He does not like to talk about it. He drinks a little more than he should. I do not blame him.

## s35-so-lighthouse
roles: soldier
title: The lighthouse

Beside the fort stands the lighthouse, a tall stone tower with a lamp at the top that the keeper lights every night for the boats on the lake. The first one fell down before it was finished, they say, because the builders were in a hurry. This one was built properly.

On a dark night, from the top of the blockhouse, you can see the lamp throwing its light out over the water, and sometimes far out a little point of light from a schooner coming in. It is a lonely sight. It makes you think about all the people out on that black water, trusting that little flame to bring them home.

## s35-so-major
roles: soldier
title: The major
meet: greene_john

Major Greene commands the fort now. He is a careful officer, an old soldier who has seen a lot of posts, and he does not like this one much. Too close to town, he says. Too many taverns. Too many land speculators trying to buy the fort out from under him.

He has the men drill twice a day so they will not have time to go into town. It does not work. But he is fair, and he does not flog a man for nothing, and when a soldier is sick he sends him to the doctor and not to the guardhouse. Go and see him if you like. He will probably ask you if you want to buy any land. He is joking. Probably.

## s35-so-indian-payment
roles: soldier
title: Guarding the payment

When the Potawatomi come in for their treaty payment, the soldiers guard the money. Two years ago at the great treaty there were thousands of them camped all round the town, and every trader in the Northwest came to collect what they said they were owed, and every whiskey seller came to take the rest.

We were told to keep order. You cannot keep order in a crowd of eight thousand people with forty soldiers. We kept the money safe, that is all. The rest of it, the whiskey and the fighting and the traders cheating, we watched. They say the people will come in again this year, for the last time, before they go west. I do not want to watch it again.

## s35-so-harbour-work
roles: soldier, labourer
title: Building the piers
meet: allen_lieut_james

The government is building piers out into the lake from the mouth of the river, so the channel will stay open and the sand will not close it again. Lieutenant Allen has had the charge of the works. Great cribs of timber, filled with stone, sunk one by one into the lake.

Some of the soldiers have been hired out to work on it, for extra pay. It is cold, wet, back-breaking work, standing in the lake up to your waist, but it pays. The lieutenant knows every timber and every stone. Go and find him. He will explain how the current carries the sand, and you will wish he had not.

## s35-so-garden
roles: soldier
title: The fort's garden

Every fort has a garden, and ours is the best in Chicago. Cabbages, turnips, potatoes, onions, beans, peas, corn. We grow enough to feed the garrison all winter, and then some, and the officers sell the rest to the town at a good price.

I was a farmer's son in Ohio, and I joined the army to get away from farming. Now I spend half my time hoeing cabbages. My father would laugh until he was sick. But I will tell you a secret: I like it. A man in a garden in the evening, with the lake behind him and the corn coming up, does not need much more.

## s35-so-fiddler
roles: soldier
title: The fiddler of the company
meet: beaubien_mark

There is a private in our company who plays the fiddle, and on Saturday nights he goes down to the Sauganash and plays with Mark Beaubien. Between the two of them they can keep a room dancing until three in the morning. Mark plays fast and loud and the private plays sweet and slow, and somehow it works.

The officers do not approve. A soldier should not play for money in a tavern. But they come down themselves to dance, so they do not say much. Go and find Mark Beaubien. Ask him which of them is the better fiddler. He will say himself. He is wrong.

## s35-so-pay
roles: soldier
title: When the paymaster comes

The paymaster comes every two months, if the roads are good, and every four months if they are not. When he comes, every soldier in the fort has his whole pay in his hand at once, and by the next morning half of them have none of it left.

The tavern keepers know when the paymaster is coming before we do. They lay in extra whiskey. The land agents come too, and sell lots to privates who have never owned anything bigger than a knapsack. I bought a lot once. It was under water. I still own it. One day, the agent says, it will be worth a fortune.

## s35-so-uniform
roles: soldier
title: A coat in summer

In July our coats are torture. Heavy blue wool, buttoned to the neck, with the collar up under your chin, and the sun beating down on the parade ground. Men faint at drill. The sergeant lets us unbutton the collar if the major is not looking.

In winter the same coat is not warm enough. The wind off the lake comes through it like it was made of cheese cloth. We wrap ourselves in blankets on guard and stamp our feet. Someone in Washington chose this coat. I would like to meet him. I would like to give him a month on the north-east blockhouse in January.

## s35-so-old-soldier
roles: soldier
title: Twenty years in the army

I have been a soldier for twenty years. I was a drummer boy in the last war with England, at Lundy's Lane, where I was so frightened I beat the wrong call and nobody noticed because everybody was too busy dying. I have served in Detroit, Green Bay, Fort Snelling, St. Louis. Now Chicago.

Every post is the same and every one is different. Detroit had the French. Green Bay had the snow. Fort Snelling had the Sioux. Chicago has the land agents. I will tell you which is the worst. The land agents. The Sioux at least tell you what they want.

## s35-so-cards
roles: soldier
title: Cards in the barracks

In the evening, when the drum beats tattoo and we are in barracks, we play cards. Euchre, mostly, and sometimes poker, for tobacco or for buttons, since gambling for money is forbidden. A good player can win a whole button collection in a week.

The champion of the company is a corporal from Kentucky who can remember every card that has been played. Some say he marks them. Nobody can prove it. He has more buttons than anyone else in the army. When he gets out, he says, he is going to sell them all and buy a farm. I think he is joking. With that man, you never know.

## s35-so-native-friends
roles: soldier
title: Watching them go

There is a Potawatomi man who comes to the fort gate every few days to sell venison. He is older than me, with grey in his hair, and he speaks a little English and I speak a few words of his language, and we have got to be friends of a kind.

Last week he told me that his people must go west across the Mississippi, by the treaty, within three years, and that most of them will go soon. He said it very calmly, as if he were talking about the weather. Then he said: you soldiers will go too, one day. Your fort will be empty, like ours. I did not know what to say to him.

## s35-so-guardhouse
roles: soldier
title: A night in the guardhouse

I spent last night in the guardhouse. I came back from town an hour after tattoo, singing, with a goose under my arm that I swear I bought honestly. The sergeant did not believe me about the goose.

The guardhouse is a log room with one window and a bench, and there were three of us in it, all for the same reason. We sang until dawn. The goose sang with us. In the morning the major looked at the three of us and the goose, and sighed, and gave us extra guard duty for a week. The goose he kept. I think he ate it.

## s35-so-sergeant
roles: soldier
title: The sergeant's advice

My sergeant has been in the army since before I was born, and he gives the same advice to every recruit. Keep your musket clean, your mouth shut, and your money in your boot. Never volunteer. Never lend money to an officer. Never buy land from a man in a tavern.

I have followed all of it except the last one. He told me I was a fool. I told him the lot would be worth a fortune. He said: son, every lot in Chicago is going to be worth a fortune, and that is exactly why none of them will be. I did not understand him. I think I am starting to.

## s35-off-officer-town
roles: officer, gentleman
title: An officer in a speculator's town

An officer at this post is a gentleman of some consequence in town. We are invited to every dinner and every dance. Every merchant wants our custom and every land agent wants our money.

I have been offered more lots than there are stars, on easier terms than a bank would give. Half my brother officers have bought. Some have doubled their money already. I have not bought anything. The army moves us every year or two, and I do not want to be at some post on the Arkansas when my lot turns into a fortune, or a lake. My colleagues call me a coward. I call myself a soldier.

## s35-off-treaty
roles: officer, gentleman
title: The treaty of 1833

I was here for the great treaty two years ago. Eight thousand Potawatomi and Ottawa and Chippewa camped on the prairie around the town, and for two weeks the government's commissioners sat with the chiefs in a great open tent and bargained for every acre they had left in Illinois.

In the end the chiefs signed. Five million acres, for money and goods and land across the Mississippi. The traders took a great part of the money to pay what they said they were owed. I have seen a great many things in the army. I have never seen so much grief and so much greed in one place at once. This February the Senate ratified it.
