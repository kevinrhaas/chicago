# Tales told by the Potawatomi, the Métis and French families, the Black residents and the
# Irish of the town, 1 July 1835. The Potawatomi tellings are reconstructions made with care:
# they stay with what the treaty of 1833 and its aftermath record, and stage no ceremony.

## s35-pm-last-summer
roles: potawatomi_man
title: The last summer here

This is the last summer my family will spend at Chicago. By the treaty our chiefs signed two years ago, all our land in this country is sold, and we must go west across the great river within three years. Some families have gone already. We will go soon.

I was born on the Des Plaines. My father is buried there, and his father. I will not see their graves again. The Americans say the new land is good, better than here. Maybe it is. A man does not love land because it is good. He loves it because his people are in it. You cannot carry that in a wagon.

## s35-pm-treaty
roles: potawatomi_man
title: The treaty tent

Two years ago in the fall, thousands of us came to Chicago for the great council. The commissioners from Washington sat in an open tent and our chiefs sat across from them, and they talked for two weeks. Every trader in the country came, and every whiskey seller, and the town was full of people from one end to the other.

The young men drank and the old men argued and in the end the chiefs signed. Some of them said it was the best they could get, because if they did not sign the Americans would take it anyway. Some of them said they had been tricked. I think both are true. That is the worst kind of treaty: the kind where everyone is right and everyone loses.

## s35-pm-traders-claims
roles: potawatomi_man
title: What the traders took

The money for our land was supposed to come to us. Much of it went to the traders instead, to pay what they said we owed them. Every trader in the Northwest came with a paper saying such and such a chief owed him so many hundreds of dollars for goods given on credit.

Some of the debts were true. Some were not. Nobody could tell the difference, and the commissioners did not try very hard. So the traders were paid first and we were paid what was left. I do not hate the traders. Some of them are our friends and our family. But I know who came out of that tent rich, and it was not us.

## s35-pm-whiskey-town
roles: potawatomi_man
title: The whiskey sellers

When we come into Chicago to trade now, the whiskey sellers are waiting at the edge of town. They sell from the back of a wagon or from a shanty by the river, and they do not care if a man is a chief or a boy. They take furs, blankets, guns, horses, anything.

I have seen men of my own family trade away everything they had for a few days of drinking, and then sit in the mud with nothing. The Americans say this is why we must go west: to get away from the whiskey. But the whiskey will follow us west. It always does. It goes wherever there is something left to take.

## s35-pm-robinson
roles: potawatomi_man, metis
title: The chief with the reservation
meet: robinson_alexander

Alexander Robinson, Che-che-pin-qua, is a chief now of the united bands, though his father was a Scotsman. By the treaties he has a reservation on the Des Plaines, two sections of land that are his own, and he will not have to go west if he does not want to.

Some of our people say he has done well for himself. Some say he has done well for himself at our expense. He keeps a house where travellers can stay, and he speaks for us to the Americans. Go and find him. Ask him what will happen to the people when they are gone. He will tell you the truth. That is why some people do not like him.

## s35-pm-caldwell
roles: potawatomi_man, metis
title: The Englishman
meet: caldwell_billy

Billy Caldwell, whom we call Sauganash, the Englishman, is our voice now with the Americans. His father was an Irish officer of the King. He fought with Tecumseh in the old war and he signed the treaty two years ago, and they say he will go west with the people when the time comes.

He is a hard man to read. He dresses like an American gentleman and speaks like one, and then he sits in council with the chiefs and speaks our language as well as any of them. Go and find him. He has seen everything that has happened to us in the last thirty years, from both sides. Ask him which side he is on. Watch how long it takes him to answer.

## s35-pm-hunting-gone
roles: potawatomi_man
title: The game is gone

When my father was young there were deer everywhere along the Des Plaines and elk on the prairie. Now there are farms. The deer are few and wild and the elk are gone. A man cannot feed his family by hunting here any more. He must buy flour from the stores like an American.

I have tried farming. My corn is as good as any Yankee's. But the land I farm is not mine any more, by the treaty. Next year a man from Ohio will come with a paper and tell me to get off it. I will not wait for him. I will go before he comes. A man should leave his field with his head up.

## s35-pm-sons-school
roles: potawatomi_man
title: My son learned to read

My son went to the mission school at Carey on the St. Joseph River for two winters, and he can read and write English now, better than some of the Americans in this town. He reads the newspaper to me. He reads me the notices of land for sale. He reads me the treaty.

I do not know if it was right to send him. He came back with short hair and a Bible and the Americans' songs. But he can read their papers, and they cannot fool him. In the west, he says, he will be a clerk for the agent, or an interpreter. Our people will need men who can read. That is what the old world has come to.

## s35-pm-council-words
roles: potawatomi_man, potawatomi_elder
title: An old man's words at the council

At the great council two years ago, one of our old men stood up and said to the commissioners: our fathers came to this country before yours, and our fathers' graves are here. You want us to go to a country we have never seen, among people we do not know. How would you feel, if we asked you to go?

The commissioner said the President was a father to all of us, and wanted only what was best. The old man said: then let the President come and live among us, and see what is best. Everybody laughed, Americans too. Then they went on with the treaty as if nothing had been said. That is how it always goes.

## s35-pm-horses-west
roles: potawatomi_man
title: Getting ready to go

I am getting my horses ready for the journey west. It is a long way, they say, across Illinois and the great river and into the country beyond, many weeks on the road. The government says it will give us food and wagons. Some of us remember what the government has said before.

So I am drying meat and corn, and mending the saddles, and trading for a good kettle and a second blanket for each of my children. I want to be ready for anything. A man who trusts the government's wagons, my uncle says, will walk west with nothing in his hands.

## s35-pm-trade-town
roles: potawatomi_man
title: I remember when this was ours

I came to Chicago as a boy, when there was the old fort and a few French houses and the Kinzie place, and we were the only people for a hundred miles. We camped by the river every summer, and the traders came to us. Now we come to the town, and stand in the mud on Lake Street, and the Americans walk past us without looking.

Yesterday a man in a fine coat stepped around me on the sidewalk as if I were a puddle. I wanted to say: I was here before your sidewalk. Before your street. Before your town. I did not say it. What would be the use? He would only have walked faster.

## s35-pm-shabbona
roles: potawatomi_man
title: The chief who saved the settlers

In the Black Hawk war three years ago, the Potawatomi chief Shabbona rode through the night to warn the American settlers that Black Hawk's warriors were coming. He saved many lives. The Americans called him a hero.

But Black Hawk's people called him a traitor, and some of our own young men did too. Shabbona says he did what was right. I believe him. But I have noticed that the Americans' gratitude has not saved the rest of us from going west. A man can save a hundred Americans and still lose his country.

## s35-pw-baskets
roles: potawatomi_woman
title: Selling baskets in town

I come into town twice a week with baskets I make from ash splints and sweet grass, and I sell them to the American women at their doors. They pay me in flour or salt or sometimes money. One lady on the North Side always buys two and gives me coffee in her kitchen.

She asked me with her hands where my people would go. I pointed west and made a long road with my fingers. She cried. I did not cry. I have done my crying. I gave her a little basket for her baby, a gift, and went out. I will miss her coffee. I will not miss the mud.

## s35-pw-graves
roles: potawatomi_woman
title: My mother's grave

My mother is buried on a little rise by the Des Plaines, under an oak tree, with a small house of bark built over her grave as we do. Every spring I go there and clear the grass away and leave food for her journey.

When we go west I cannot take her. A farmer will plough over that rise one day, and he will not know she is there. I have asked my daughter to remember the place, so that she can tell her children: there, under the oak, is your grandmother. But my daughter will be far away. And her children will never see the oak.

## s35-pw-sugar
roles: potawatomi_woman
title: The last sugar bush

This spring was the last time we made sugar at our old sugar bush on the Fox River. The maples there are older than my grandmother. We tapped them as we always have, and boiled the sap, and the children ate it on the snow, and it was a good spring.

But a man from Vermont has a paper for that land now. He came and watched us while we worked. He did not tell us to go. He only watched. I think he wanted to learn how we did it. Next spring he will tap the trees himself. I hope they give him sugar. I hope it is bitter.

## s35-pw-daughter
roles: potawatomi_woman
title: My daughter's husband

My daughter married a Frenchman, a trader's son, and they live in a house in the town, and their children go to the American school. By the treaty she may stay, because her husband is not one of us. Her children may stay. I must go.

She asked me to stay with her, to live in her house, to become a town woman. I could, I suppose. But I would be a stranger in her house, an old Potawatomi woman in an American kitchen. My people are going west. I will go with my people. She cried. I told her that daughters always cry and mothers always go. It is the way of the world.

## s35-pw-mats
roles: potawatomi_woman
title: Mats for the journey

I am weaving new mats from the marsh rushes, more than I have ever made, to cover our lodges on the journey west and in the new country. I do not know what grows there. Maybe there are no rushes. Maybe I will need these mats for the rest of my life.

My granddaughter helps me. She is learning to split the rushes and sew them with the bone needle. Her stitches are crooked and I make her pull them out and do them again, as my grandmother did with me. She asks why it matters when we are going away. I tell her: because wherever we go, we will still be ourselves. Our stitches must still be straight.

## s35-pch-going
roles: potawatomi_child
title: We are going on a long walk

My father says we are going on a long walk, across the whole country, to a new place where the sun goes down. It will take more days than I can count. We will take our horses and our dogs and our kettles.

I asked if there will be other children there. He said yes, many. I asked if there will be strawberries. He did not know. I asked if my friend from the town can come. He said no. I am taking my ball stick and my rabbit-skin blanket and the toy horse my grandfather carved. I am not afraid. I am a little afraid.

## s35-pch-town-boy
roles: potawatomi_child
title: The town boy and the bow

There is a boy in the town, with a red face and a loud voice, who wanted to learn to shoot my bow. I showed him and he shot the arrow straight into the ground. I laughed so hard I fell over.

Then I gave him the bow, because I can make another one, and he gave me a glass ball that has blue swirls inside it like the lake. I keep it in my pouch. When I look into it I can see the lake. I will take it west. There is no lake there, my father says. So I will bring one.

## s35-pch-mission
roles: potawatomi_child
title: My brother can read

My big brother went to the mission school and now he can read the Americans' words. He reads to me from a book about a man who was swallowed by a fish and spat out again. I do not believe it. My brother says it is true because it is in the book.

My grandmother says the old stories are true too, and they are not in any book. She says my brother has learned to read the Americans' words but has forgotten how to hear ours. My brother says that is not fair. I think they are both a little bit right. That is what my mother says about most arguments.

## s35-cd-voyageur
roles: canadien
title: The last of the voyageurs

When I came here as a young man, I paddled a canoe from Mackinac with the American Fur Company's goods, eight men to a canoe, singing all the way. In those days Chicago was a French place. The traders were French, the boatmen were French, the dances were French, the priests came from the French missions.

Now the Yankees have come, and the furs are gone, and the canoes are rotting on the bank. I work on the wharves, unloading schooners for a Yankee merchant who cannot pronounce my name. But on Saturday nights at the Sauganash, Mark Beaubien plays the old songs, and we sing them, and for an hour it is the old days again.

## s35-cd-st-mary
roles: canadien, irish
title: The little church of St. Mary

Two years ago the French and the Irish of Chicago signed a letter to the bishop in St. Louis asking for a priest. There were a hundred and twenty-eight of us, more or less, though some of us signed for our children. The bishop sent Father St. Cyr, a young Frenchman, and he said the first Mass in a log cabin.

Then we built a church, a little wooden building, in a few weeks, with the new kind of thin boards nailed together. Now on Sunday mornings it is full: French, Irish, a few Germans, some of the Potawatomi families who are Catholic. The Yankees have their churches. This one is ours.

## s35-cd-beaubien-family
roles: canadien, metis
title: The Beaubiens
meet: beaubien_jean_baptiste

There are Beaubiens everywhere in this town. Colonel Jean Baptiste Beaubien came from Detroit long ago, when Chicago was nothing, and he has married twice and has more children than a schoolroom. His brother Mark keeps the Sauganash. Their children and grandchildren are in every street.

The Colonel lives in the old house by the fort, and this spring he went into the government land office and bought the whole fort reservation for a few dollars, because he says he has lived on it longer than the soldiers. The government is furious. The Colonel just laughs. Go and see him. He is the most stubborn Frenchman in Illinois, and the kindest.

## s35-cd-dance
roles: canadien, townswoman
title: A French dance

A French dance is different from a Yankee dance. The Yankees dance in rows, very correct, bowing to each other. We dance in a ring, and we spin, and we stamp, and the fiddle goes faster and faster until somebody falls down, and then everybody laughs and the fiddler starts again.

At the New Year we go from house to house, singing and dancing, and at every house there is food and wine, and you kiss everybody. The Yankees think it is scandalous. The Yankee young men come anyway. By the end of the night they are dancing in a ring and stamping too.

## s35-cd-ouilmette
roles: canadien, metis
title: The old portage man

Old Antoine Ouilmette was here before the first fort, when Chicago was just a river and a few houses. He carried goods across the portage for the Kinzies, and in the old war, when the soldiers marched out and were killed on the beach, he stayed in his house with his Potawatomi wife and was not harmed.

His wife was given land up the lake shore at Gros Point by the treaty, and they live up there now. He comes into town sometimes and stands on Lake Street looking at all the stores and the people, shaking his head. He says he does not recognise the place. Nobody does, any more.

## s35-cd-cariole
roles: canadien
title: Racing in a cariole

In winter we French race our carioles on the frozen river, little sleighs pulled by a single horse, low and fast, with the driver standing up and the bells ringing. The Yankees have bigger sleighs, but ours are faster. Every Sunday afternoon after Mass, up and down the river from the forks to the fort.

Last winter I raced a Yankee lawyer with a fine new horse from Kentucky. He was ahead all the way until the bend by the fort, where the ice is rough. My little pony knows the ice. His big Kentucky horse did not. He went into a snowbank. I went home and bought everyone a drink.

## s35-cd-family-split
roles: canadien, metis
title: Family on both sides

My wife is half Potawatomi. Her mother's people are going west by the treaty. Her father's people, the French, are staying. My wife is staying with me and our children in Chicago. But her mother and her brothers and her cousins are going.

Every evening this summer my wife walks out to the camp by the river to sit with her mother. She comes back with red eyes and does not speak. What can I say to her? That it is the law? That it is progress? That the Americans want the land? She knows all that. What she does not know is how to say goodbye to her mother forever.

## s35-mt-two-worlds
roles: metis
title: Two peoples in one body

My father was a French trader and my mother is Potawatomi, and so I am both, and neither. The Americans call me a half-breed. My mother's people call me French. My father's people call me Indian. I call myself a Chicagoan, since I was born here before any of the Yankees came.

By the treaty, people like me were given land or money, because of our Indian blood. I took the money and bought a lot on Lake Street. Now I am a property owner, like a Yankee. The land my mother's people sold is paying for the land I bought. I do not know what to make of that. Nobody does.

## s35-mt-interpreting
roles: metis, canadien
title: Turning words into other words

I interpret for the agent and for the traders, between the Potawatomi and the English. It is harder than it sounds. A Potawatomi chief does not say I agree. He says the words are good and the sky is clear. An American commissioner does not say you must leave. He says your Great Father wishes you to remove for your own welfare.

So I must turn one kind of speech into another, and every time I choose a word I am choosing what the other side will hear. At the great treaty I was so tired at the end of each day I could not speak at all, in any language. A good interpreter is a bridge. Everybody walks on a bridge. Nobody thanks it.

## s35-br-free-papers
roles: black_resident
title: My free papers

I carry my free papers in an oilcloth packet next to my skin, day and night. Under the law of this state, a Black man must show a certificate of his freedom, and if he cannot, any white man can take him up as a runaway and the sheriff can hire him out. I have shown mine twice this year, once to a constable and once to a drunk who thought he could make money from me.

This is a free state, they say, and it is, compared to Kentucky, where I was born. But free is not the same as equal. I work as a barber on Lake Street. I am good at it. The lawyers and merchants come to me because I am the best. They talk in front of me as if I were a chair. I hear everything. I say nothing.

## s35-br-du-sable
roles: black_resident, canadien
title: The first man to live here

The old French people tell me that the first man to make a home at the mouth of this river, the very first who was not Potawatomi, was a Black man, Jean Baptiste Point du Sable. He came from the south, from the islands, and married a Potawatomi woman and built a house and a farm on the north bank, fifty years ago. The Kinzie house was his house.

The Yankees do not talk about him. They talk about the Kinzies as if they were the first. But the old French know, and the Potawatomi know. A Black man founded Chicago. I remind the Yankees of it sometimes, when they are in my chair and I have a razor at their throat. They do not argue.

## s35-br-steamboat-cook
roles: black_resident
title: A cook on the lake boats

I came here as a cook on a steamboat out of Buffalo. A good cook on a lake boat can name his wages, and the captains do not much care what colour the hands are that make the bread. I cooked for two seasons and saved my money and came ashore at Chicago.

Now I keep a little eating house near the river, for the sailors and the labourers. Fried fish, pork and beans, coffee strong enough to float a nail. Some of my customers are rough men. But a man who is hungry at five in the morning does not ask who cooked his breakfast. He asks for more.

## s35-br-family
roles: black_resident
title: Waiting for my family

My wife and children are still in Ohio. I came ahead to find work and a place for us, and I have found both. I work for a merchant on South Water Street, loading and unloading, and he pays me fairly, the same as the white men. I have rented two rooms.

Now I am saving for their passage. It is a long way and a hard one for a Black woman with three children, through places where people would sell her if they could. I have written to a minister in Cleveland who will see them onto the boat. I count the boats coming in every day. One day, one of them will be theirs.

## s35-br-church
roles: black_resident
title: Sitting in the back

On Sunday I go to the Methodist meeting. I sit at the back, where they want me to sit. The preacher says that all men are equal in the sight of God. Then after the meeting, the people walk past me as if I were not there.

But there is one old woman, a widow from New Hampshire, who always stops to shake my hand and ask after my health. She says that in New Hampshire she knew people who had stood up against slavery, and she means to stand up too, here, in her way. Her way is a handshake every Sunday. It is a small thing. It is not a small thing.

## s35-ir-boat-over
roles: irish
title: The boat from Liverpool

I came over from Liverpool in the hold of a timber ship, with two hundred others, for six weeks. We slept on boards and ate oatmeal and drank water that tasted of the barrel. A baby was born on the way, and an old man died, and they put him over the side with a prayer and a weight at his feet.

We landed at Quebec and walked and worked our way west, by boat and on foot, through Canada and into New York and onto the lakes. It took me a year to get to Chicago. I have a dollar a day here. I have a shovel and two good arms. I will never be that hungry again.

## s35-ir-canal-wait
roles: irish, labourer
title: Waiting for the canal

Everybody says the canal will start next year, and when it does there will be work for ten thousand men with shovels. I am going to be one of them. Until then I unload schooners and dig foundations and carry hods of brick.

My cousin is digging on a canal in Ohio and he writes that the work is hard and the fever is worse, and men die in the ditches every summer, and the contractors cheat on the pay. I know all that. But a canal pays every week, and when it is done there will be towns along it, and lots, and a man who was there at the start can get one. I am going to be there at the start.

## s35-ir-priest
roles: irish
title: Father St. Cyr

Father St. Cyr is a young Frenchman, and he speaks English like a Frenchman, and Irish not at all. But he is a priest, and he says Mass, and that is all that matters. Before he came there was no priest in Chicago, and a man could die without the last rites, and a baby could go unbaptized for a year.

Now we have a little church, St. Mary's, and every Sunday it is full. The French sit on one side and the Irish on the other, and the Potawatomi Catholics at the back, and Father St. Cyr preaches to all of us in his strange English. Nobody understands half of it. Everybody goes home happy.

## s35-ir-fight
roles: irish, labourer, soldier
title: A fight on Lake Street

Last Saturday there was a fight outside a tavern on Lake Street between some Irish lads and some Yankee carpenters. It started over a girl, or a lot, or a hat, nobody remembers. It ended with eleven men in the mud and two in the doctor's office and one Irish lad with a broken nose who was very proud of it.

On Monday they were all back at work together on the same building, carrying the same beams. The Yankee who broke the Irish lad's nose bought him a drink on Tuesday. That is how it is here. You fight on Saturday and work on Monday. Nobody has time to hold a grudge. There is too much building to do.

## s35-ir-pat
roles: irish, labourer
title: Patrick's luck
meet: rc_mahoney_patrick

Patrick Mahoney is the luckiest man in Chicago. He came off the boat last summer with nothing but the clothes he stood up in, and the first day he found a silver dollar in the mud on Clark Street. The second day a merchant hired him. The third day he fell off a roof and landed in a hay wagon.

He says it is because his mother gave him a holy medal when he left Ireland. He shows it to everybody. Go and find him. He will show it to you, and he will tell you the hay wagon story, and by the time he is done it will have been a much higher roof.
