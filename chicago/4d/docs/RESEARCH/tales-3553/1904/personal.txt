# Personal tales of 1904: told by one named person, in their own voice, from what is known
# of them. The telling is reconstructed.

## p04-glessner-house
for: glessner_john_j
title: The house that faces inward
meet: glessner_frances_m

When we asked Mr. Richardson to build this house, my wife and I wanted something that would turn its back on the noise and the dirt of the street and open toward a sunny court within. He gave us exactly that: granite walls on Prairie Avenue and Eighteenth Street, with small windows like a fortress, and all the light falling into the court behind.

The neighbours were horrified. Mr. Pullman, across the street, said he did not know what he had ever done to have that thing staring him in the face every time he came out of his door. Mr. Richardson never saw it finished. He died before the walls were up. We have lived in it seventeen years now, and I would not change a stone. Go and ask my wife. She chose every chair in it.

## p04-glessner-reapers
for: glessner_john_j
title: Reapers and mowers

I came to Chicago in 1870, a young man from Ohio, to run the western end of our firm, Warder, Bushnell and Glessner, that made reapers and mowers in Springfield. Every farmer from here to Dakota needed a machine to cut his grain, and we sold them by the thousand.

Two years ago our firm and McCormick's and Deering's and the others came together into one company, the International Harvester, and I am a vice president of it. People say we have made a trust. I say we have made a machine within a machine. When I came to Chicago, a man cut his wheat with a cradle, by hand, and his whole family bound the sheaves. Now one man and a team can harvest a field in a day. That is what I am proudest of.

## p04-frances-reading-class
for: glessner_frances_m
title: Monday mornings
meet: pa1904_lee_frances_glessner

Ten years ago I began a reading class for the ladies of my acquaintance, on Monday mornings in my library. We meet through the winter. A lady reads aloud, a novel or a history or a book of essays, while the others sew or knit, and then we talk about it, and then we have lunch.

It is the most pleasant morning of my week. Some of the ladies who come are very learned and some are very silly, and the silly ones often say the wisest things. I keep a journal, too, and have since my children were little: what we ate, who came, what was said. My daughter Fanny teases me about it. Go and find her. She will tell you that her mother writes down everything. It is true.

## p04-frances-thomas
for: glessner_frances_m
title: Our friend Mr. Thomas

Mr. Theodore Thomas has been our friend for many years. When he brought his orchestra here, we were among the people who promised to stand behind it, and for thirteen years we have heard every concert we could, on Friday afternoons and Saturday nights at the Auditorium.

He comes to dinner here, and sits by the fire and talks about Beethoven as if Beethoven were a neighbour he had just been speaking to. He is a hard man, people say, stubborn and proud. He is. He is also the kindest friend we have. This year the city is building him a hall of his own on Michigan Avenue. He has waited a long time for it. I pray he lives long to enjoy it.

## p04-fanny-college
for: pa1904_lee_frances_glessner
title: My brother went to Harvard
meet: pa1904_lee_blewett

My brother George went to Harvard. I wanted to go too, and I was not allowed. Young ladies of good family did not go to college, my parents thought, or not to Harvard, which does not take them in any case. So I was taught at home, in the schoolroom on the top floor of my father's house, with George, until he left for Cambridge and I stayed.

I have a husband now, Mr. Blewett Lee, and three children. I read everything I can. I am interested in how things work, how they are made, how a house is put together down to the last nail. My mother says that is not a subject for a lady. My husband is a lawyer. Go and find him. He has very decided views on what is a subject for a lady.

## p04-blewett-lee
for: pa1904_lee_blewett
title: The general's son

My father is General Stephen D. Lee, who commanded an army corps for the South in the war, and was at Vicksburg and Atlanta and Nashville. I grew up in Mississippi, in the years after the war, when we had nothing but our name. I studied law at Harvard and abroad, and came north to work for the Illinois Central Railroad, which runs from here all the way down to my father's country.

So I am a Southerner in a Northern city, married to a Northern lady, the daughter of a man who made machines for Northern farms. My father fought the men whose sons I dine with. None of us mention it. When the old soldiers march on Decoration Day, I stand on the sidewalk with my hat in my hand, like everyone else, and think of my father.

## p04-field-store
for: pa1904_field_marshall
title: Give the lady what she wants

I came to Chicago in 1856, a young man from the hills of Massachusetts, to clerk in a dry goods store on Lake Street. I slept in the store. I saved half of what I earned. I learned that a customer who is treated honestly will come back, and a customer who is cheated will tell twenty others.

Now the store on State Street is the largest in the world, perhaps, and we are building a new one beside it, bigger still. I am told I am the richest man in Chicago. I do not think about that. I think about whether the lady who came in this morning for a spool of thread got it quickly and was treated well. That is the business. The rest is just arithmetic.

## p04-field-fire
for: pa1904_field_marshall
title: The store burned
meet: pa1904_field_marshall_c1900_thomas_benson

In October of 1871 our store on State Street was the finest in the city, a marble palace. On the second night of the fire it burned to the ground with everything in it. We saved what goods we could, in wagons, and carried them to the lake shore. When it was over, I stood in the ashes with my partner and we looked at each other.

Two weeks later we opened again, in a horse car barn at State and Twentieth Street, with planks on barrels for counters. The ladies of Chicago came in their old clothes, because their good ones had burned, and bought new ones from us. We were rebuilt within two years. A fire can take a building. It cannot take a name. Ask my butler, Benson, what he thinks of fire. He has opinions.

## p04-pullman-harriet
for: pa1904_pullman_mrs_george_m
title: My husband's sleeping cars
meet: pa1904_lowden_mrs_frank_orren

My husband, George Pullman, built a railway car that a man could sleep in, with beds that folded out of the walls and linen and porters, so that a traveller could go to sleep in Chicago and wake up in New York. Before him, people sat up all night on wooden benches. He made a fortune from that idea, and he built a whole town south of the city, with brick houses and a church and a library, for the men who made his cars.

Then came the strike, ten years ago. The men he had built a town for turned against him. I do not think he ever understood why. He died three years later. My daughter Florence lives near me now with her husband and her children. Go and find her. She was always her father's favourite. The hotel in his town bears her name.

## p04-pullman-grave
for: pa1904_pullman_mrs_george_m
title: The grave at Graceland

When my husband died, seven years ago, there were people who hated him very much: men who had been in the strike, and their friends. We were afraid of what they might do. So he was buried at Graceland in a pit lined with concrete, in a lead coffin, and over the coffin they laid steel rails and more concrete, tons of it, so that no one could ever disturb him.

The newspapers said it was to keep the strikers away. It was. It was also because he was a man who always planned for everything, even this. I visit him on Sundays. There is a tall column there, very handsome. Nobody can tell from looking at it that he lies under a fortress of his own making.

## p04-florence-lowden
for: pa1904_lowden_mrs_frank_orren
title: The hotel named for me
meet: pa1904_lowden_frank_orren

When my father built his town at Pullman, he named the hotel there after me: the Hotel Florence. I was a girl of thirteen. I used to go down on his private car and walk through the halls and see my name on the menus. It was the proudest I have ever been.

My father thought of everything for that town except what the people in it wanted. I have thought about that a great deal since he died. I married a lawyer from a farm in Iowa, who taught school to pay for his own education, and who has never let me forget what it means to work for a living. Go and find him. He has just lost his first election, and he is taking it very well. Mostly.

## p04-frank-lowden
for: pa1904_lowden_frank_orren
title: Seventy-nine ballots

This spring I ran for governor of Illinois. The Republican convention met at Springfield in May, and there were seven of us wanting the nomination, and nobody had a majority, and nobody would give way. They voted, and voted again, and again, for three weeks. Every night we bargained in hotel rooms full of cigar smoke until two in the morning.

On the seventy-ninth ballot they gave it to Charles Deneen. I lost. I went to Deneen and shook his hand and promised to work for him, and I will. I grew up on a farm in Iowa and taught school and read law by lamplight. A man who has lost one fight has learned how the fight is fought. I am not done. Not by a long way.

## p04-armour-widow
for: pa1904_armour_mrs_philip_d
title: The million dollar sermon
meet: pa1904_armour_mrs_philip_d_c1900_henry_deneke

Fourteen years ago a young minister named Dr. Gunsaulus preached a sermon called: What I would do with a million dollars. He said he would build a school where the sons and daughters of working people could learn to be engineers and architects and chemists. My husband was in the church that morning. Afterwards he went up to the minister and said: if you will give five years of your life, I will give the million dollars.

So they built the Armour Institute, on the South Side, and there are hundreds of young people there now learning to build bridges and engines. My husband died three years ago, the year after our son Philip. He made his fortune in the stockyards, from meat. People say hard things about the yards, and some of them are true. But the Institute is true, too. Ask Henry Deneke, who looked after him. He will tell you what kind of man he was.

## p04-kimball-pianos
for: pa1904_kimball_william_w
title: A piano in every parlour
meet: pa1904_kimball_william_w_c1900_ernest_sibbald

I came to Chicago from Maine in 1857, with almost nothing, and started selling pianos and organs that other men made. People said nobody on the prairie would buy a piano. They were wrong. Every farm family that had a little money wanted an organ in the parlour, so that the daughters could play hymns on Sunday.

Then I started making them myself, organs first and then pianos, in a great factory on the West Side. The Kimball organ is in churches all over the country now. My wife and I built this house twelve years ago, like a French château, with a gallery for her paintings. Go and see Ernest Sibbald, who keeps our accounts. He will tell you how many pianos we sold last year. I would rather not boast.

## p04-fernando-jones
for: pa1904_jones_fernando
title: I came in 1835

I came to Chicago in 1835, a boy of fifteen, with my father. It was a town of a few hundred people, frame houses and log cabins in the mud along the river, and the old fort, and the Potawatomi camped on the prairie. That summer the great land craze was on, and every man in Chicago was buying and selling lots that were under water half the year.

I learned to speak a little of the Potawatomi language, trading with them. That autumn they gathered for the last time and went away west across the Mississippi, as the treaty said. I watched them go. Now I live on Prairie Avenue in a house of stone, on land that was open prairie when I was a boy, with wolves on it. I am one of the last who remember. When I am gone, nobody will.

## p04-shortall-books
for: pa1904_shortall_john_g
title: The night I saved the books
meet: pa1904_jones_fernando

On the night of the great fire, in October of 1871, every record of every piece of land in Chicago was in the courthouse, and the courthouse burned. Every deed, every mortgage, every title, gone. But my firm kept our own abstracts of title, copies of all the records, in our office. And the fire was coming.

I found a wagon in the street, and its driver did not want to go anywhere near the fire, and I persuaded him. Some say at the point of a pistol. I will not say. We loaded the books and drove them out ahead of the flames. Two other firms saved theirs too. Afterwards the legislature made our books the lawful record, and every title in Chicago rests on them today. Go and find Fernando Jones. He was in the same business. He will tell you whether I really had a pistol.

## p04-shortall-humane
for: pa1904_shortall_john_g
title: The Humane Society

For many years I have been president of the Illinois Humane Society. We began by protecting horses, because the horses of this city are beaten and starved and worked until they drop in the street. Then we began to protect children, because, God help us, children in this city are treated worse than the horses.

We have agents who go into the tenements and the stables and the factories. We have put drinking fountains for horses on the street corners. We take children away from fathers who beat them. Some people say it is none of our business. I say it is everybody's business. A city is judged by how it treats the creatures that cannot speak for themselves.

## p04-meeker-yards
for: pa1904_meeker_arthur
title: The packing business

I went to work for Mr. Philip Armour when I was a young man, and I have been in the packing business ever since. Every day the trains bring in cattle and hogs and sheep from the whole West, and every day we kill them and dress them and pack them and send them out in refrigerator cars to every city in the country, and to Europe.

People are horrified by the yards. I understand that. It is a hard business, and a bloody one, and the smell is terrible. But a working man in New York can eat beef for supper today because of what we do in Chicago. My daughters do not understand what I do. I do not take them to the yards. One day, perhaps, I will.

## p04-casey-wood
for: pa1904_wood_casey_a
title: The eye doctor

I am a doctor of the eye. I came to Chicago from Canada by way of Europe, where I studied in the great eye hospitals of London and Vienna and Berlin. Every day in my office men and women come to me afraid of going blind, and some of them I can help, and some I cannot.

The eye is the most beautiful thing in the body, I think. When you look into it with the ophthalmoscope, with the little light, you see the blood vessels branching like the roots of a tree, and the nerve where the world comes in. When I am not in my office, I am watching birds. Every creature sees the world differently. I would like to know how.

## p04-mattie-glessner-kitchen
for: glessner_williamson_martha_l
title: Mrs. Glessner's kitchen

I cook for Mr. and Mrs. Glessner, in the kitchen on the north side of the court. Mrs. Glessner is particular. She plans the meals with me every morning, and she writes things down in her book: who came to dinner, and what was said. I think she remembers my dinners better than I do.

When Mr. Thomas the musician comes, I make his favourite things. When the reading class ladies come on Monday, I make the lunch, light, because ladies who have been sitting all morning do not want a heavy lunch. Go and find Mrs. Glessner in the house. If you ask her who came to dinner a year ago, she can tell you. It is all in the book.
