# Tales told by the people who keep the houses of Prairie Avenue running, 1 July 1904:
# maids, cooks, laundresses, coachmen, grooms, butlers, valets, nurses.

## s04-md-thursday
roles: maid
title: Thursday afternoon

Every maid on Prairie Avenue lives for Thursday afternoon. That is our afternoon off, from two o'clock until ten, and every Sunday after the breakfast dishes, every second week. On Thursday the streetcars going north are full of girls in their best hats, going to see their sisters and their sweethearts and the shops on State Street.

I go to my cousin's in the Swedish quarter on the North Side, and we drink coffee and eat cardamom bread and speak our own language for six hours, and nobody rings a bell for me. At half past nine I am on the car again, and at ten I am in my attic room with my uniform laid out for six o'clock. Six hours of being myself. It is enough to last the week. Nearly.

## s04-md-bells
roles: maid, manservant
title: The bells

In the back hall of this house there is a board with forty little bells on springs, one for every room, each with a name painted underneath: Library, Parlour, Mrs. G's Room, Front Door, Guest Room North. When someone in the family presses a button in a room, the bell for that room shakes and rings.

You learn to sleep through every bell but your own. The cook does not hear the library bell. The butler does not hear the nursery. But I hear Mrs. G's room bell in my sleep, even on my afternoon off, even when I am miles away. When I am an old woman and have a house of my own, I am going to have no bells in it at all.

## s04-md-irish
roles: maid
title: From County Mayo
meet: pa1904_field_marshall_c1900_maggie_fitzsimmons

I came from County Mayo when I was seventeen, with my passage paid by my aunt who was in service in Chicago already. She met me at the station and the next day took me round the houses on Prairie Avenue, ringing at the back doors, until a housekeeper took me on as a kitchen maid.

Now I am a house maid in a great house, and I send four dollars home every month to my mother, and my little brother is coming over next spring on money I saved. Half the girls on this street are Irish, and the other half are Swedish or Norwegian or German. Go and find Maggie Fitzsimmons at Mr. Field's. She is from the next county to mine, and she knows every Irish girl on the Avenue.

## s04-md-swedish
roles: maid, cook
title: Swedish girls

The ladies of Prairie Avenue all want a Swedish girl. They say we are clean and honest and do not drink and do not argue. I do not drink. I am honest. I am clean. I argue a great deal, but only in Swedish, so the ladies do not know.

I came from Småland with my sister. She is a cook on Calumet Avenue and I am a parlour maid here. On Sundays we go to the Swedish church and sing the old hymns and eat with people from home. Then we come back to our employers' houses and become quiet and clean and honest again until Thursday.

## s04-md-fire-iroquois
roles: maid, cook, nurse, laundress
title: The fire at the Iroquois

Last December, just after Christmas, the new Iroquois Theatre downtown caught fire during the matinee. It was full of women and children on their holidays, two thousand people, watching Eddie Foy in Mr. Bluebeard. A light set fire to the curtain, and the fire curtain that was supposed to come down stuck halfway, and the doors opened inward, and some of them were locked.

Six hundred people died in a quarter of an hour. Children, mostly, and their mothers and nurses and maids who had taken them. I knew a girl who was a nursemaid in a house on Michigan Avenue. She took the two children. None of them came home. I still cannot pass a theatre without counting the doors.

## s04-md-stairs
roles: maid
title: The back stairs

There are two staircases in this house. The family uses the front stairs, wide and carved and carpeted, with a window of coloured glass at the turn. We use the back stairs, narrow and steep and bare, that go from the kitchen to the attic without ever passing through a room where the family might see us.

I go up and down those stairs forty times a day with coal, hot water, trays, linen, slops. My legs are like a soldier's. The family hardly ever sees us working. That is the idea. A well-run house, the housekeeper says, should seem to run itself. As if the coal walked up the stairs on its own.

## s04-md-wages
roles: maid
title: Four dollars a week

I am paid four dollars a week and my keep, and that is a good wage for a house maid. In a factory I would get more, maybe six, but I would have to pay for my room and my food, and work in the noise and the dirt, and walk home through the streets at night.

Here I have a room of my own in the attic, with a bed and a washstand and a window that looks out over the roofs to the lake. I eat what the family eats, the next day. I have a uniform. I save almost everything. In five years I will have enough to marry, or to open a little shop, or to go home and buy my father's farm. I have not decided which.

## s04-md-silver
roles: maid, manservant
title: Polishing the silver

Every Monday the silver is polished, every piece, whether it was used or not. Forks, spoons, knives, ladles, the tea service, the candlesticks, the great centrepiece shaped like a ship. It takes two of us all morning, with rouge and chamois and soft brushes for the engraving.

The butler counts every piece before and after. He has a book with every item listed and its weight. Once a fish fork went missing for three days and the whole household was in an uproar. It turned up in the garbage, where a kitchen girl had thrown it out with the bones. She nearly lost her place. The fork was worth less than a day of her wages.

## s04-md-ladies-maid
roles: maid
title: A ladies' maid
meet: pa1904_armour_mrs_philip_d_c1900_frances_jordan

A ladies' maid is the highest of the maids. I dress my mistress, do her hair, mend her clothes, pack her trunks, and travel with her when she goes away. I know every dress she owns and which gloves go with it. I know her moods before she does.

It is a lonely job. The other servants think I am too grand for them, and the family thinks I am a servant. I sit with neither at dinner. But I have been to New York and to Paris with my mistress, and I have seen the sea, and the Alps from a train window. Go and find Frances Jordan at Mrs. Armour's. She is a ladies' maid too. We are the only two on the Avenue who understand each other.

## s04-md-telephone
roles: maid, manservant, housekeeper
title: The telephone

There is a telephone in this house, on the wall in the back hall, and I am the one who answers it. You lift the ear piece and turn the crank and say: Residence of so-and-so, and then a voice comes out of the wall from somewhere miles away.

The first time it rang I nearly dropped the tray I was carrying. Now I am used to it. I take the messages and write them down and bring them to the mistress on a little silver plate. My mother in Norway has never seen a telephone. When I wrote and told her about it she wrote back that I should go to church more.

## s04-md-ice
roles: maid, cook
title: The ice man

The ice man comes three times a week in summer with his wagon full of great blocks cut from the lakes up north in the winter and stored in sawdust. He carries them in on his back with tongs, a hundred pounds at a time, and puts them in the ice box in the pantry.

He is a big Bohemian with a moustache, and every girl on the Avenue knows his name, and he knows all of ours. He winks at the kitchen maids and slips the little children chips of ice to suck. Cook says he is a scoundrel. Cook gives him a cup of coffee and a slice of cake every time he comes.

## s04-md-wedding
roles: maid, young_lady
title: A society wedding

When a daughter of one of the great houses is married, the whole Avenue knows it weeks ahead. The dressmakers come and go, the florists, the caterers. On the day, the carriages line the street from Sixteenth to Twenty-second, and the police come to keep the crowds back.

The maids from every house watch from the back windows. We see the bride come out in her white satin and her veil, and we see the guests in their silks, and we make our comments, which are not polite. Then we go back to work. The next morning we hear everything from the girls who served at the wedding breakfast: who drank too much, who cried, who was not invited.

## s04-md-sweetheart
roles: maid
title: My sweetheart drives a wagon

My sweetheart drives a delivery wagon for a grocer on Cottage Grove, and he comes to the back door of this house three times a week with the order. We have exactly as long as it takes him to carry in the boxes to talk. Two minutes. Sometimes three, if Cook is in a good mood.

We have been walking out for a year, on my Thursdays. He is saving for a little flat on the West Side. When he has enough, we will marry, and I will leave service. The mistress will be cross. She says she cannot find good girls any more. I say, then she should not let them meet the grocer's boy.

## s04-md-dust
roles: maid, laundress
title: The soot

Chicago is the dirtiest city in the world. The soot from the railroads and the factories and the furnaces of every house comes down like black snow, all year round. You dust a room in the morning and by evening there is a grey film on every table.

The windows have to be washed every week, inside and out. The lace curtains have to be taken down and washed every month. The white collars of the gentlemen go grey in an afternoon. The lake wind helps a little. When the wind is from the west, from the stockyards, you shut every window in the house and pray.

## s04-md-attic
roles: maid
title: My room under the roof

My room is on the top floor, under the roof, with the other maids. It is hot in summer and cold in winter, and the roof slopes down so that I can only stand up straight in the middle. There is a bed, a chair, a washstand, a hook for my clothes, and a little window.

From the window I can see the lake. In the morning, before the bell, I watch the sun come up over the water, all pink and gold, with the ore boats and the schooners going out. Nobody else in the house sees it. They are all asleep. It is the only thing in this house that is entirely mine.

## s04-ck-kitchen
roles: cook
title: The kitchen at five in the morning

I am in the kitchen at five, before anybody. I shake down the range and get the fire roaring, and set the kettle on, and start the bread. By six the kitchen maid comes down and starts on the vegetables. By seven the servants' breakfast is on the table and the family's is on the dumbwaiter.

Then it is lunch, and then tea, and then dinner, seven courses if there are guests, and the dishes, and the next day's bread set to rise. I go to bed at ten. I have been a cook for twenty-two years. My feet hurt every minute of every day. But I make the best clear soup in Chicago, and every lady on this street knows it.

## s04-ck-dinner-party
roles: cook, maid, manservant
title: A dinner for twenty-four

Last month we gave a dinner for twenty-four. Oysters, clear turtle soup, a whole salmon on ice, saddle of mutton, roast duck, an ice in the middle to clear the mouth, a salad, a cold bombe, cheese, fruit, coffee. Nine wines. Every plate changed at every course. Forty-eight hands to serve and clear.

We were up until three in the morning washing up. The next day the mistress came down to the kitchen and told Cook it was the finest dinner she had given in years. Cook said thank you, ma'am, very calm. When the mistress went upstairs, Cook sat down at the kitchen table and cried for a minute. Then she got up and started the bread.

## s04-ck-market
roles: cook
title: Going to the market

On Tuesdays and Fridays I go to the South Water Street market with the coachman to choose the meat and the fish and the vegetables myself. I do not trust the grocer's boy. He would bring me a fish that died last week and tell me it was this morning's.

South Water Street is the busiest place in Chicago at six in the morning: wagons from every farm in Illinois, crates of chickens, barrels of apples, men shouting in ten languages. I walk through it all like a general, pinching the peaches and smelling the fish, and the commission men know me by name and give me the best. They know if they cheat me I will tell every cook on Prairie Avenue.

## s04-ck-german
roles: cook
title: A German cook

I learned to cook in Bavaria, in a hotel kitchen, when I was fourteen. I came to Chicago twenty years ago and I have cooked for three families on this street. They want French food for their dinners and German food for their own lunches, though they would never say so.

So I make them a sauce béarnaise and a soufflé for the guests, and for the family on Sunday a pot roast with dumplings and red cabbage, and the master eats three helpings and says it is the best thing he has eaten all week. The French chefs downtown think they are the only cooks in Chicago. They should taste my strudel.

## s04-ck-summer-cook
roles: cook, maid
title: When the family goes away

In the summer the family goes away, to the lake shore in Wisconsin or to the East, for July and August, and the house is shut up with the furniture under sheets. Most of the servants go too. One or two of us stay, to keep the house aired and the burglars away.

I stayed last summer. It was the strangest feeling, to have the great house all to myself. I sat in the master's chair in the library one evening, just to see what it felt like. It felt like nothing. It is only a chair. I went back to the kitchen. That is where I am comfortable.

## s04-ck-stockyards
roles: cook, manservant, coachman
title: Meat from the yards

Every piece of meat in this house comes from the stockyards, four miles south and west, where they kill more cattle and hogs in a day than there are people in some towns. When the wind is from the west you can smell it here on Prairie Avenue, a thick sweet smell that gets into the curtains.

The men who own those yards live on this street: Armour, and the others. They made their fortunes from that smell. I have a cousin who works on the killing floor at Armour's. He works ten hours a day standing in blood, for a dollar seventy-five. Then he comes to visit me, and I give him a piece of the roast that his own hands killed. He says it tastes better here.

## s04-ck-typhoid
roles: cook, laundress, nurse
title: Boiling the water

Two summers ago the typhoid came through the city and many people died, mostly in the poor wards on the West Side, but some here too. The doctors said it was in the water. Ever since, I boil every drop that is drunk in this house, and I make the maids boil the water for the babies' bottles too.

They reversed the river four years ago, so the sewage would go down the canal toward St. Louis instead of out into the lake where the city drinks. It helps. St. Louis is very angry about it. I do not care what St. Louis thinks. I care that nobody in this house gets the typhoid.

## s04-ld-washday
roles: laundress
title: Washday

Monday is washday, every week of my life. I start at five with the copper boiler on the laundry stove in the basement. Sheets, tablecloths, napkins, shirts, collars, petticoats, the children's things, the servants' aprons. I boil them, scrub them on the board, rinse them twice, blue them, starch them, wring them through the mangle, and hang them in the drying yard or, if it rains, in the drying room by the furnace.

Tuesday I iron. Wednesday I mend. Thursday I do the fine things, the laces and the silks, by hand. Friday I start getting ready for Monday. My hands are red and cracked from the lye and the hot water, and they will be until I die. But my sheets are the whitest on Prairie Avenue. Ask anybody.

## s04-ld-starch
roles: laundress
title: The gentleman's collars

A gentleman on this street changes his collar twice a day and his shirt once, and every collar must be starched so stiff it could stand up by itself, and ironed until it shines like porcelain. A collar with a crease in it is a disgrace, the butler says, to the whole household.

I iron forty collars a week. I can do one in a minute and a half. My iron is heated on the stove, three of them, so one is always hot while I use the other. I have burned my fingers so many times they no longer feel it. The gentleman has no idea who irons his collars. He has never once come down to the laundry.

## s04-ld-norwegian
roles: laundress, maid
title: The laundry in the basement
meet: pa1904_field_marshall_c1900_karolina_hall

The laundry is in the basement, under the kitchen, where the family never goes. There is a window up high, at the level of the yard, and through it I can see the boots of the coachmen going by, and the wheels of the carriages, and the dogs. That is my view of Prairie Avenue: the ankles of the rich.

But I sing while I work, the songs from home, and the cook upstairs says she can hear me through the floor and it makes the bread rise. Go and find Karolina Hall at Mr. Field's. She is a laundress too, and she sings better than I do. Do not tell her I said so.

## s04-cm-horses
roles: coachman
title: The horses come first

A coachman's day begins in the stable, before light. The horses must be fed, watered, groomed, their feet picked out, their coats brushed until they shine. Then the harness, every buckle polished, every strap oiled. Then the carriage, washed, the brass rubbed, the wheels greased. Then, and only then, my own breakfast.

The family thinks a coachman's job is to sit up on the box in his livery and drive. That is the least of it. A coachman lives for his horses. I know mine better than I know my own children. I know when they are off their feed, when they are lame, when they are in a mood. A good horse is a good friend. A bad horse is a bad master.

## s04-cm-automobile
roles: coachman, gentleman
title: The automobile

A gentleman down the Avenue has bought an automobile, and a man to drive it. It makes a noise like a threshing machine and smells like a lamp and frightens every horse on the Avenue. My mares shied at it last week and nearly put me in the gutter.

The young gentlemen think it is the future. They say in ten years there will be no horses in Chicago at all. I do not believe it. A machine cannot know its way home in the fog. A machine cannot tell when the ice is bad. A machine does not come when you whistle. But I have been teaching myself how the engine works, from a book. Just in case.

## s04-cm-livery
roles: coachman
title: The livery

My livery is dark green, with brass buttons that have the family's initial on them, a high collar, white breeches, black boots to the knee, and a silk hat with a cockade. In winter there is a great caped coat over it. I look like a general.

I hate it. It is hot in summer and the boots pinch and the hat blows off in the lake wind. But I wear it, and I sit up straight on the box, because when the carriage goes down Michigan Avenue on a Sunday afternoon, everybody looks, and they see the family's horses and the family's carriage and the family's coachman, and they know whose they are. That is what the livery is for.

## s04-cm-stable-life
roles: coachman
title: Living over the stable

The coachman and the groom live over the stable, in rooms above the carriage house at the back of the lot, off the alley. It smells of horse, and hay, and harness oil. I would not trade it for the finest room in the house.

Out here nobody rings a bell for me. I can smoke my pipe, and play my concertina, and have a glass of beer with the coachman from next door. We sit on the bench by the stable door in the evening and talk about horses and the families and the price of oats. It is a whole other street, the alley. The rich live on the front, and we live on the back.

## s04-cm-groom
roles: coachman
title: The stable boy
meet: pa1904_pullman_mrs_george_m_c1900_samuel_whatter_jr

I started as a stable boy when I was twelve, mucking out and carrying water and sleeping in the hay loft. I am a groom now. One day I will be a coachman, with my own livery and my own horses to drive, and a stable boy of my own to shout at.

There is a groom at the Pullman house, Sam Whatter, who is the best I know with a difficult horse. He can calm a bolting mare just by talking to her. Go and find him at the Pullman stable. Ask him about the grey mare that nobody could ride. He will tell you how he did it. It took him three months and a great many apples.

## s04-cm-park-drive
roles: coachman
title: A drive in the park

On fine afternoons I drive the mistress out to Washington Park or down the boulevards. The boulevards are wide and smooth, with trees on either side, and every fine carriage in Chicago is out on them, the ladies in their hats and the gentlemen nodding to each other, and the coachmen sizing each other up.

We coachmen have our own races. Not real races, of course, the ladies would faint. But if you can pass another carriage on the boulevard without seeming to try, with your horses stepping high and not breaking stride, that is a victory, and every coachman on the boulevard knows it. I passed the Kimball carriage last Tuesday. I have not stopped smiling since.

## s04-cm-fire-1871
roles: coachman, gentleman, lady, manservant
title: The night of the Great Fire

I was a boy of fourteen in October of 1871, working in a livery stable on the West Side, when the fire started in a barn not far from us. By midnight the whole sky was red. The wind carried the fire across the river and through the business district and up into the North Side, and it burned for two days.

We let all the horses out of the stable and drove them ahead of the fire like cattle, through the streets full of people running with their bundles and their children. I saw a man pushing a piano down the street. I saw a woman carrying a birdcage with no bird in it. When it was over, the heart of the city was gone. And they built it all back, bigger, in a few years. That is Chicago.

## s04-cm-carbarn
roles: coachman, manservant, gentleman
title: The car barn bandits

Last summer four young men robbed the streetcar company's barn on the South Side and shot two men dead for the payroll. They were hardly older than boys. They hid in a dugout in the sand dunes of Indiana, and when the police came for them there was a gun battle in the dunes.

They hanged three of them this April, at the jail on the North Side. I went to see the crowd outside, though I did not go in. Thousands of people, all quiet, all waiting. A woman next to me said they were only boys. A man said they were murderers. They were both right. I came home and fed the horses and could not eat my own supper.

## s04-ms-butler
roles: manservant
title: The butler's pantry
meet: pa1904_field_marshall_c1900_thomas_benson

The butler is the head of the household below stairs, and I rule it from the butler's pantry, with the silver safe and the wine cellar keys on my chain. I hire and dismiss the footmen. I decant the wine. I answer the door to callers. I know who is at home and who is not at home, which is not the same as who is in the house.

A good butler is invisible and indispensable. The family should never have to ask for anything, because it should be there before they think of it. Go and see Thomas Benson at Mr. Field's. He is the finest butler on Prairie Avenue. He will be very polite to you and tell you absolutely nothing.

## s04-ms-valet
roles: manservant
title: A gentleman's valet
meet: pa1904_armour_mrs_philip_d_c1900_henry_deneke

A valet dresses his gentleman, shaves him, lays out his clothes, packs for his journeys, keeps his wardrobe in order and his boots polished. I know my gentleman's collar size, his hat size, his shoe size, his opinions on everything, and the name of every man he has quarrelled with.

Henry Deneke was valet in Mr. Armour's own house, before the old gentleman died three years ago. Go and find him at Mrs. Armour's. He will tell you about Mr. Armour, who was one of the richest men in America, and was up before five and at his desk at the yards by seven, every day of his life.

## s04-ms-footman
roles: manservant
title: The footman

A footman's job is to be tall, to stand still, to open doors, to carry trays, and to ride on the back of the carriage looking as if he were made of wood. I am six feet two inches, which is why I was hired. Height in a footman is worth two dollars a week.

When there is a dinner, I stand behind a chair for three hours without moving, without smiling, without hearing a word anybody says. Afterwards in the servants' hall we tell each other everything we heard. The family thinks we are furniture. Furniture has ears.

## s04-ms-steward
roles: manservant
title: The steward's books
meet: pa1904_kimball_william_w_c1900_ernest_sibbald

In a great house, somebody has to keep the accounts: the grocer's bill, the butcher's, the coal, the ice, the wages, the repairs. That is the steward. I sit in my little office off the back hall with my ledgers and I know what everything in this house costs, down to the last candle.

Ernest Sibbald keeps the books at Mr. Kimball's across the street. We compare notes sometimes. Go and find him. He will tell you exactly how much a piano company owner spends on asparagus in a year. It is a great deal. More than I earn.

## s04-ms-coal
roles: manservant, coachman
title: Feeding the furnace

In the cellar of this house there is a furnace as big as a locomotive, and in winter somebody has to feed it, every four hours, day and night, with coal shovelled from the coal room where the wagons tip it down a chute from the alley. That somebody is me.

The heat goes up through pipes to every room in the house, and the family is warm, and has no idea there is a furnace at all. On a January night, at two in the morning, shovelling coal by the light of a lantern with the wind howling outside, I sometimes think I am the most important man in the house. Nobody else thinks so.

## s04-hk-keys
roles: housekeeper
title: The housekeeper's keys
meet: pa1904_pullman_mrs_george_m_c1900_emma_scott

I carry the keys of the house on a chain at my waist: the linen room, the china closet, the store room, the wine, the guest rooms, the attic. Nothing goes in or out of this house without my knowledge. The maids answer to me. The cook pretends she does not, and does.

I have been in service for thirty years, starting as a scullery girl in Ireland. Now I run a house with eleven servants. The mistress consults me on everything, though she would never admit it to her friends. Go and see Emma Scott at the Pullman house. She keeps the biggest house on the Avenue. If anyone has more keys than I do, it is she.

## s04-hk-new-girl
roles: housekeeper, maid
title: The new girl

A new girl came to us last month, from Galway, sixteen years old, straight off the train. She had never seen an electric light, or a flush toilet, or a gas range, or a staircase with a carpet. She stood in the front hall with her mouth open, and then she burst into tears.

I took her down to the kitchen and gave her tea and told her I was just the same, thirty years ago, in a big house in Dublin. Now she is the quickest girl I have. She laughs all day and sings in the scullery and the cook adores her. In ten years she will be a housekeeper herself. Or married. They always get married.

## s04-nu-nursery
roles: nurse
title: The nursery floor

The children of this house live on the nursery floor, with me. They sleep there, they eat there, they have their lessons there, and they see their mother for an hour in the afternoon, washed and brushed and dressed in their best, in the drawing room.

So I am the one who knows them. I know which one is afraid of the dark and which one bites his nails and which one is going to be a great lady one day. I taught them to walk and to say their prayers. When they are grown, I suppose, they will forget me. Children always do. But I will not forget them.

## s04-nu-park
roles: nurse
title: Walking out with the children

Every fine morning I take the children out, the baby in the perambulator and the older ones walking beside, down the Avenue to the lake front or over to the park. All the nurses on the Avenue do the same, so it is like a parade: six nursemaids, twelve children, three dogs.

We nurses sit on the benches and talk while the children play. We know everything about every family on the street. Who is quarrelling, who is ill, who is going abroad, who has lost money. The ladies would be horrified to know how much we know. Children talk, you see. Children tell their nurses everything.

## s04-nu-governess
roles: nurse
title: The governess

I am a governess. I teach the girls of this house French, music, drawing, deportment, and a little arithmetic, though not too much, because their mother thinks too much arithmetic is unladylike. I was educated at a school in Boston. My father was a minister. We had no money.

So I am neither a lady nor a servant. I eat with the family when there are no guests, and on a tray in the schoolroom when there are. The servants think I am grand. The family think I am useful. I think I am lonely. But the youngest girl drew me a picture last week of the two of us, holding hands, under a tree. I have it in my drawer.

## s04-nu-illness
roles: nurse, lady
title: The scarlet fever

When the little boy had scarlet fever last winter, the nursery was shut off from the rest of the house with a sheet soaked in carbolic hung over the door, and I was shut in with him for six weeks. Nobody else could come in. His mother stood outside the sheet every evening and talked to him through it.

The doctor came every day. There was a night when we thought he would die. I sat by his bed and held his hand and sang him every song I knew, twice. In the morning his fever broke. When I came out, after six weeks, the mistress put her arms around me. She has never done that before or since.
