# Tales told by the families of Prairie Avenue and their grown sons and daughters,
# 1 July 1904.

## s04-gm-fire-spared
roles: gentleman, lady
title: Why we live on Prairie Avenue

When the great fire came in October of 1871, it started on the West Side and the wind drove it north and east, across the river, through the whole business district and the North Side, where most of the fine houses were. It never came this far south. Prairie Avenue was spared.

So afterwards, everybody who could afford it came here. Within ten years this was the finest street in Chicago, stone and brick houses side by side for six blocks, and every man in them had made a fortune or was making one. The old families still say they live on the Avenue as if there were no other. There are others now. But for thirty years, there were not.

## s04-gm-north-side
roles: gentleman, lady
title: Everyone is moving north

Since Mr. Potter Palmer built his castle on the Lake Shore Drive, the fashionable people have been going north, one after another. The young couples do not want a house on Prairie Avenue. They want one on the Drive, or in Lake Forest, away from the railroad.

The Illinois Central runs along the lake behind us, and the trains go by day and night, and the smoke comes over the gardens. And to the west and south of us, the town is creeping up: boarding houses, saloons, the Levee district on Twenty-second Street, which no lady will mention by name. My father built this house to last a hundred years. I do not think his grandchildren will live in it.

## s04-gm-fair
roles: gentleman, lady, young_man, young_lady
title: The White City

Eleven years ago, in the summer of 1893, Chicago held the World's Columbian Exposition in Jackson Park, and the whole world came to see it. Great white palaces around a lagoon, lit at night by electricity, more light than anybody had ever seen in one place. And Mr. Ferris's wheel, taller than any building in the city, turning slowly with a whole street car full of people in every car.

I went forty times. Everybody did. People came from every country in the world and stood in the Court of Honour at night with their mouths open. They called it the White City. Then it ended, and that winter the palaces stood empty, and the next summer most of them burned. It was like a dream that everybody had at once.

## s04-gm-mayor
roles: gentleman
title: The last night of the fair

On the last week of the fair, Mayor Carter Harrison gave a speech to the visiting mayors of America, and said Chicago had shown the world what it could do. That evening a young man came to his front door on Ashland Avenue, asked to see him, and shot him three times.

The fair's closing ceremonies were cancelled. Instead of fireworks, there was a funeral. I had shaken the mayor's hand a week before. He was a big, loud, generous man who wore a slouch hat and rode about the city on horseback and knew every saloon keeper in the city by name, and half the respectable people hated him, and they all went to his funeral.

## s04-gm-haymarket
roles: gentleman
title: The bomb in the Haymarket

In May of 1886 the workingmen of Chicago struck for an eight hour day. There was a meeting in the Haymarket on Randolph Street, anarchists speaking from a wagon. When the police marched in to break it up, someone threw a bomb into their ranks. Seven policemen died of it, and more people in the shooting afterwards.

Nobody ever proved who threw the bomb. They tried eight anarchists for it and hanged four. Some men on this street said it was justice. Some said it was not. I have never been sure. When the governor pardoned the three who were left, eleven years ago, half the city called him a traitor. The other half called him brave.

## s04-gm-pullman-strike
roles: gentleman, lady
title: The summer of the strike

Ten summers ago the workers at Mr. Pullman's car works went on strike, after he cut their wages and would not lower their rents in his town. The railway union refused to handle Pullman cars, and every railroad in the West stopped. No trains went in or out of Chicago.

President Cleveland sent the army. There were soldiers camped on the lake front and fires in the railroad yards and mobs in the streets, and the men on Prairie Avenue sat up at night with pistols in their desks. In the end the strike was broken and the union man Debs went to jail. Mr. Pullman was never the same afterwards, and neither were the people of his town.

## s04-gm-board-of-trade
roles: gentleman, young_man
title: The wheat corner

Seven years ago a young man named Joe Leiter, son of old Levi Leiter, tried to corner the wheat market at the Board of Trade. He bought every bushel of wheat he could find, and for months he drove the price up and up, and everybody said he was a genius.

But old Phil Armour was selling against him, and Armour sent ships into the ice of Lake Superior with tugs to break a channel and bring down the wheat from the Northwest in the dead of winter. When spring came there was wheat everywhere, and the price collapsed, and young Leiter lost millions. His father paid. I watched it all from the gallery. That is the Board of Trade. Fortunes in an afternoon, and gone by the next.

## s04-gm-skyscraper
roles: gentleman, young_man
title: Buildings that touch the sky

When I was a young man, a building of five storeys was tall. Now there are buildings downtown of sixteen, twenty, twenty-two storeys, built on frames of steel, with the walls hung on them like curtains, and elevators to carry you to the top in a minute.

The Masonic Temple has a roof garden twenty storeys up, where you can look out over the whole city and the lake. The architects of this city have invented a new kind of building, and New York has copied it. My office is on the fourteenth floor. In the morning, when the smoke is low, I look down on it like a man in a balloon. I can see the ships coming in thirty miles off.

## s04-gm-commute
roles: gentleman
title: Going downtown

Every morning at half past eight my carriage takes me downtown to my office on LaSalle Street, a mile and a half, and every evening at half past five it brings me home. Some of my neighbours take the cable car, which runs up State Street, or the elevated railway, which runs down the alley west of State Street.

My father walked to work, along the lake shore, with his dinner in a pail. I could walk if I liked. I do not like. The streets downtown are so crowded with wagons and cars and carriages that it can take half an hour to cross State Street. A gentleman's time is worth more than that. So is his collar.

## s04-gm-civil-war
roles: gentleman
title: When I was a soldier

I was nineteen when the war began, and I went with an Illinois regiment, and I was at Shiloh and at Vicksburg. I have not talked about it much since. Most of the men on this street my age did not go. They hired substitutes, or they were too busy making money. That was allowed.

On Decoration Day I go out to the cemetery with the old soldiers of my post and we lay flowers on the graves. There are fewer of us every year. Last month there were eleven. When the young men talk about war, about Cuba and the Philippines, I listen and say nothing. They had a short war. Ours was four years.

## s04-gm-spanish-war
roles: gentleman, young_man
title: The war with Spain

Six years ago, when the Maine was blown up in Havana harbour, every young man in Chicago wanted to go and fight Spain. The First Illinois went to Cuba and were at the siege of Santiago, and more of them died of the fever than of the Spanish bullets.

My son went. He came home thin and yellow with the malaria, and he has never been quite strong since. He says it was worth it. They freed Cuba, he says, and they took the Philippines, and the flag goes around the world now. Then he coughs. I am proud of him. I wish he had stayed at home.

## s04-gm-river
roles: gentleman, lady
title: Turning the river backwards

Four years ago, Chicago did something no city had ever done. It made its river run backwards. For years the river had carried all the sewage of the city out into the lake, where the city drinks. When the rains were heavy, the typhoid and the cholera came.

So they dug a great canal from the South Branch to the Des Plaines River, twenty-eight miles through rock, deeper and wider than any canal in the world. In January of 1900 they opened the gates, and the river turned around and began to flow away from the lake, toward the Mississippi and St. Louis. St. Louis sued. Chicago does not care. Chicago never cares what anybody thinks.

## s04-gm-convention
roles: gentleman, young_man
title: The convention last week

Last week the Republican party held its national convention at the Coliseum on Wabash Avenue and nominated President Roosevelt for another term. The city was full of delegates and bands and banners. Every hotel was booked, every saloon was full, and you could not get a cab for love or money.

I went to two sessions. It was very hot and very loud and nothing was in doubt, since everybody knew it would be Roosevelt. But it was a fine show. They say the Democrats meet in St. Louis next week, where the World's Fair is open. If the Republicans can win Illinois with Roosevelt, they can win the country. I think they can. Most of this street thinks so too.

## s04-gm-university
roles: gentleman, young_man, young_lady
title: The new university

Twelve years ago a new university opened on the Midway, beside where the fair would be, built on Mr. Rockefeller's millions. Now it has grey stone towers like Oxford, and professors from all over the world, and students, men and women both, studying side by side. President Harper says it will be the greatest university in America.

The old families send their sons east to Yale and Harvard, as they always have. But the new university is changing Chicago. It has brought scholars to a city that only had merchants. A man on this street used to be ashamed to read a book in public. Now there is a professor at every dinner party, and the ladies hang on every word.

## s04-gm-philanthropy
roles: gentleman, lady
title: What a fortune is for

The men on this street have given Chicago a great deal. A museum, an art institute, a library, a university, hospitals, an orchestra. Some of them did it because they believed in it, and some of them did it so that people would forget how they made their money.

My father used to say: a man who dies rich dies disgraced. That was Mr. Carnegie's saying, and my father repeated it often. He did not die poor, mind you. But he gave the money for a hospital ward and a school, and the nurses in that ward still put flowers by his portrait on his birthday. That is a better monument than this house.

## s04-gm-trusts
roles: gentleman
title: The trust

Two years ago the five biggest makers of reaping machines in the country joined together into one great company, the International Harvester. Mr. Morgan's bankers arranged it. The newspapers call it a trust and say it will crush the farmer.

I say the farmer has never had better machines at lower prices. But the President is in a fighting mood against the trusts, and his attorney general is going after the railroad combinations in the courts. Everything is getting bigger. Companies are getting bigger, cities are getting bigger, fortunes are getting bigger. I sometimes wonder where it stops.

## s04-ld-calling
roles: lady
title: Calling day

Every lady on this street has an at home day, when she receives callers in the afternoon. Mine is Tuesday. From three until six, ladies come in their carriages, leave their cards on the silver tray in the hall, come in for a quarter of an hour, take a cup of tea, talk about nothing, and leave. Then they go and call on somebody else.

It sounds very silly, and it is. But it is also how everything on this street is done. A marriage, a charity, a quarrel, a reconciliation: all begin with a card on a tray. If a lady leaves a card with the corner turned down, she came herself. If she does not come back, something is wrong. You can read the whole history of Prairie Avenue in the cards on my hall table.

## s04-ld-dress
roles: lady, young_lady
title: Dressing for the day

A lady on this street changes her clothes four or five times a day. A morning dress for breakfast and the household. A walking dress to go out. A visiting dress for calls. A tea gown for the afternoon at home. A dinner dress at night, cut low, with jewels. Each with its own hat and gloves and shoes.

My maid spends half her day dressing me and undressing me and putting my things away. I spend a great deal of money at Field's and at the dressmaker's. My husband says I could clothe an army on what I spend in a year. I tell him an army does not dine with the Fields.

## s04-ld-hull-house
roles: lady, young_lady
title: Miss Addams on Halsted Street

Fifteen years ago Miss Jane Addams took an old house on Halsted Street, in the middle of the poorest ward on the West Side, and went to live there among the immigrants: Italians, Greeks, Russian Jews, Bohemians. She calls it Hull House. She runs a kindergarten, a night school, a bath house, a theatre, a public kitchen, and a club for working girls.

Some of the ladies of this street give her money. A few of us go and teach. I have a class of Italian girls on Thursday nights, sewing and English. They work ten hours a day in the garment shops, and then they come and sit in my class and learn, and laugh. My husband thinks I go to a church sewing circle. In a way, I do.

## s04-ld-iroquois
roles: lady
title: My friend at the Iroquois

My friend took her two little girls to the Iroquois Theatre on the thirtieth of December, for the holiday matinee, with their nurse. They had seats in the balcony. When the fire started on the stage, the people in the balcony rushed for the doors, and the doors were locked, or opened inward, and the crowd pressed against them.

They found my friend afterwards in the stairway, with her arms around both her girls. The nurse lived. She was below them on the stairs and was pulled out by a fireman. She comes to see me sometimes, and we sit together and do not say much. The theatre had been open five weeks. They advertised it as absolutely fireproof.

## s04-ld-servants
roles: lady
title: The servant problem

Every lady on this street complains about servants. A good cook is impossible to find. A good parlour maid stays a year and then marries the grocer's boy. The Irish girls are clever but quick tempered. The Swedish girls are steady but they leave to go to the factories, where they have their evenings free.

I have had the same cook for eleven years. I pay her more than any cook on the Avenue, and I give her two afternoons a week instead of one, and I have never once gone into her kitchen without knocking. The other ladies think I spoil her. My dinners are the best on the street. I have no servant problem at all.

## s04-ld-women-vote
roles: lady, young_lady
title: The vote

There are women in Chicago now who want the vote. They hold meetings, they march, they write to the newspapers. My husband says it is ridiculous. A woman's place is in the home, he says, and her influence is through her husband and her sons.

Women can vote in Colorado, and Wyoming, and Utah, and Idaho, and nothing terrible has happened there. Here in Illinois we can vote for school officers and the trustees of the state university, and nothing else. I do not march. But I have started to read the newspapers that my husband leaves on the breakfast table, all of them, the political pages too. When the time comes, I want to know what I am voting for.

## s04-ld-orchestra
roles: lady, gentleman
title: Mr. Thomas's orchestra
meet: glessner_frances_m

Thirteen years ago some of the men of Chicago persuaded Mr. Theodore Thomas to come from New York and build an orchestra here. He has given us Beethoven and Brahms and Wagner every week in the season, and he has fought with everybody, and he has never once given the public a cheap programme to please them.

The orchestra has always played in the Auditorium, which is too big for it. So this year the city raised the money to build him a hall of his own, on Michigan Avenue, and they are building it now. Mrs. Glessner has been one of his truest friends. Go and see her. She will tell you about the evenings Mr. Thomas spent at her table, talking music until midnight.

## s04-ld-bicycle
roles: lady, young_lady
title: The bicycle

Ten years ago, every lady in Chicago learned to ride a bicycle. It was a madness. We wore divided skirts and shirtwaists and straw hats, and rode out along the boulevards in parties, twenty or thirty of us, ringing our bells. Some of the gentlemen were scandalised. Some of the clergy preached against it.

I learned at forty-two, on the gravel paths in Washington Park, with my coachman running alongside to catch me. I fell off eleven times. The twelfth time I did not fall, and I rode all the way to the lake and back. I had never been anywhere by myself before, without a carriage or a maid or a husband. It was the most free I have ever felt.

## s04-ld-mourning
roles: lady
title: In mourning

When my husband died, I wore black crêpe for a year, and black silk for a year after that, and grey and lavender for six months more. For the first year I did not go out in society at all, and I received only family. My writing paper had a black border an inch wide.

It is the custom, and I followed it. But I will tell you something. In that first year, alone in this house, I read every book in my husband's library, which he never let me touch. I learned to keep the household accounts and I found out that our man of business had been cheating us for ten years. I dismissed him. Nobody on this street knows that. They think I sat in the dark and wept.

## s04-ld-summer
roles: lady
title: Going away for the summer

In two weeks we close the house and go away for the summer: to the house on the lake in Wisconsin, or to the White Mountains, or to the sea at Bar Harbor. Everybody on the Avenue goes. By the end of July, the street is empty, the blinds are drawn, and the furniture is under dust sheets.

We take most of the servants and forty trunks and the horses go by special car. It is like an army moving. Then we come back in September, browner and fatter, and the season begins again. Chicago in August is impossible: hot, stinking, full of flies. I do not know how the people who have to stay here bear it. I suppose they do not have a choice.

## s04-ld-gardener
roles: lady
title: My garden

I have a little garden behind this house, between the back porch and the stable, and it is mine. I planted the roses myself, and the lilies, and the peonies that come up every June, as big as cabbages. My gardener does the digging. I do the rest.

The soot is terrible for roses, and the lake wind is worse. I wash the leaves by hand. My friends think I am eccentric. In the evenings, when the trains have gone by and the dust settles, I sit out there with a book and the smell of the roses, and for a quarter of an hour I could be anywhere. That is enough. That is what a garden is for.

## s04-ld-telephone
roles: lady
title: Talking to my sister in New York

Last month, for the first time, I talked to my sister in New York by long distance telephone. The operator called me back at three o'clock, and there was her voice, very small and crackling, from a thousand miles away. We had not heard each other's voice in four years.

We talked for three minutes, which cost my husband a fortune. Neither of us could think of anything to say. We just said: is that really you? And: how are the children? And: it is very hot here. Then the operator said time, and it was over. I sat down and cried. My husband thought something had happened. Something had.

## s04-ym-yale
roles: young_man
title: Home from college

I have just come home from my third year at Yale, and I am bored to death. In New Haven there was football and rowing and the clubs and the night before the game against Harvard when the whole town went mad. Here there is my mother's at home day and dinners with old men who talk about wheat.

My father wants me to come into the firm when I graduate. He started as a clerk at sixteen and built the business with his own hands, and he does not understand why I want to do anything else. I want to be an architect. I want to build things, like Mr. Burnham and Mr. Sullivan. My father says architects do not get rich. I say neither do clerks, mostly.

## s04-ym-automobile
roles: young_man
title: My automobile

I bought an automobile in the spring, a little Winton with a two cylinder engine and a brass horn. It goes twenty miles an hour on a good road. The roads around Chicago are mostly not good. I have been stuck in the mud on the way to Lake Forest four times and been pulled out by a farmer's horse each time. The farmers laugh at me.

My father refuses to ride in it. My mother rode once, in a veil and goggles, and said never again. The coachman hates it, because it frightens his horses. But I tell you, in twenty years there will not be a horse left on this street. Everybody will have one of these. Everybody will laugh about the time they laughed at me.

## s04-ym-football
roles: young_man, child
title: The big game

Last Thanksgiving I went to see the University of Chicago play Michigan at Marshall Field. Not the man, the field, named for him because he gave the land. Thousands of people in the stands, with flags and horns, in the cold. Michigan had not lost a game in three years.

Mr. Stagg's boys fought them for every yard. It was the hardest football I have ever seen, with men carried off the field one after another. In the end Michigan beat us badly, and Chicago's men walked off the field crying like babies. I lost my voice for a week. My mother says football should be outlawed. Half the country agrees with her.

## s04-ym-settlement
roles: young_man, young_lady
title: A summer at the settlement

This summer I am living at the university's settlement near the stockyards, back of the yards, as they call it. Miss McDowell runs it. I teach English to Polish and Lithuanian boys in the evening, and I see how the people live who work in the packing houses.

Six families in a frame house built for one. No water but a pump in the yard. The smell of the yards over everything. Men who work ten hours on the killing floor and come home with their hands cut to pieces. My father's money comes from shares in a packing company. I have not told him where I am spending my summer. I do not know what I am going to say when he asks.

## s04-ym-clerk
roles: young_man
title: My first job

I started work in my father's bank on the first of June, as a junior clerk, at twelve dollars a week. My father said I must learn the business from the bottom, as he did. So I sit at a high desk in a back room on LaSalle Street and copy figures into ledgers for nine hours a day.

The other clerks know who my father is. They are very polite to me and they do not invite me to lunch. I eat a sandwich at my desk and look out of the window at the street. Someday I will have my father's office, with the big window and the leather chair. Today I have a stool and a pen that leaks.

## s04-ym-st-louis
roles: young_man, young_lady
title: Going to the fair at St. Louis

Next week my brother and I are going down to St. Louis on the train to see the World's Fair, the Louisiana Purchase Exposition. They say it is bigger than our fair in 1893. They say there are palaces of electricity and machinery and agriculture, and a great wheel, and villages of people from all over the world, the Philippines and Africa and Japan, and Mr. Ferris's own wheel, carried down from Chicago and set up again.

My father says nothing will ever be as fine as the White City. He says St. Louis is only copying Chicago. Maybe. But I was only a boy in 1893, and I remember the lights and the wheel and nothing else. This time I am going to see everything. I have a list. It is four pages long.

## s04-ym-car-barn
roles: young_man, gentleman
title: The boys who robbed the car barn

Last August four young men robbed the car barn of the Chicago City Railway, out at Sixty-first Street, and shot two clerks dead for two thousand dollars of the payroll. They called them the car barn bandits. They were about my age. They had been to school, some of them. One of them had a mother who took in sewing.

The police chased them all over the country until the winter, and at last they found them in a dugout in the Indiana dunes. They shot a detective dead there before they were taken. Three of them were hanged this spring. I followed the trial in every newspaper. I keep asking myself what turned them. Nobody can tell me.

## s04-yl-coming-out
roles: young_lady
title: My coming out

Last winter I came out, which means I was presented to society at a ball my mother gave for me at the Auditorium, with four hundred guests and an orchestra and banks of white roses. I wore white satin from Paris, and I danced every dance, and I did not sleep for two days.

Since then I have been to forty dances, and twenty dinners, and a hundred teas. I have been proposed to twice, by a boy I danced with once and by a man of forty with a moustache. I said no to both. My mother says I must say yes to somebody by the end of next season. I would like to say yes to somebody I can talk to. That seems to be very difficult to arrange.

## s04-yl-college
roles: young_lady
title: I want to go to college

My brother is at Harvard. My cousin is at Yale. I wanted to go to Vassar, or Smith, or to the new university here, where women study alongside the men. My father said no. A girl does not need college, he said. A girl needs a husband and a household.

So I am taking lessons at home. French, music, drawing, a little history from a tutor who comes on Tuesdays. I read every book my brother leaves in the house when he comes home for the summer, and when he goes back to Harvard, I know more than he does. He knows it, too. He does not say so to our father.

## s04-yl-art-institute
roles: young_lady
title: Lessons at the Art Institute

I take classes at the Art Institute three mornings a week, in the school behind the galleries. We draw from plaster casts, and from life. Not from the nude, of course, not the ladies' class. From a man in a suit, or a woman in a dress, sitting very still.

My teacher says I have a real talent. He says I could exhibit, if I worked hard. My mother says a lady may paint as a pastime but not as a profession. So I paint as a pastime, five hours a day, and I have a portfolio under my bed of two hundred drawings. Some day I am going to show them to someone who will say yes.

## s04-yl-hat
roles: young_lady
title: The hat

I bought a hat yesterday at Field's. It is the largest hat on Prairie Avenue. It has a brim a foot and a half across, a whole stuffed bird on it with its wings spread, ostrich plumes, a bow of violet velvet, and a veil. It cost forty dollars. My mother nearly fainted.

I wore it to church this morning and three ladies behind me could not see the minister. One of them said something to me afterwards that I shall not repeat. I do not care. It is a magnificent hat. The bird is a tern. I named him Henry.

## s04-yl-tennis
roles: young_lady, young_man
title: Tennis on the lawn

On summer afternoons we play lawn tennis on the court behind the Keiths' house, or at the club. The girls play in long white skirts and shirtwaists with sleeves to the wrist, and straw hats tied on with veils. It is very hot. We play anyway.

I am the best player on the Avenue, man or woman. The young men do not like it. They play hard against me for a set and then say they have a headache. I beat my brother six games to love last week, and he has not spoken to me since. My mother says a young lady should let the gentleman win. I say then she should find me a gentleman who can.

## s04-yl-engagement
roles: young_lady
title: My engagement

I am engaged. He is a lawyer, twenty-eight years old, from Boston, with a moustache and a good income and very kind eyes. We met at a dinner last autumn and he wrote to me every week all winter. In May he asked my father, and my father said yes, and then he asked me, and I said yes.

We are to be married in October, at Second Presbyterian, and we will live on the North Side near the lake. My mother is planning the wedding like a military campaign. I would be happy with a small wedding in the parlour. Nobody asks what I would like. That is all right. In October, I will be the one who gets to decide.

## s04-yl-diary
roles: young_lady
title: My diary

I have kept a diary since I was ten years old. Every night before bed I write in it: what I did, who I saw, what they said, what I thought about it. I have nine volumes now, bound in red leather, in a locked drawer in my desk.

My diary knows more about this street than anybody. Who is in love with whom. Who is quarrelling. Which family has had losses at the Board of Trade and is selling the horses. In a hundred years, somebody may read it and know what it was like to be a girl on Prairie Avenue. They will think we were very silly. Some of us were. Not all.

## s04-ch-pony
roles: child
title: My pony

I have a pony named Biscuit, who lives in our stable with the carriage horses. He is fat and brown and very naughty, and he bites the coachman when nobody is looking. The coachman says he is the devil in a pony's skin.

On fine mornings the groom puts the little saddle on him and leads me up and down the alley, and then out on the street, very slow, past all the houses. All the other children wave. One day the groom let go of the rope and Biscuit ran all the way to the corner with me holding on to his mane, and I did not fall off, and the groom turned white. I was not scared at all. Not much.

## s04-ch-fourth
roles: child
title: The Fourth of July

In three days it will be the Fourth of July, and we are going to have firecrackers. My father bought a whole box of them, and Roman candles, and pinwheels, and sparklers, and at night we will set them off in the yard behind the house, and everybody on the street will do the same.

Last year a boy on the next street blew off the end of his thumb with a cannon cracker. My mother says I may only have the little crackers and the sparklers. I am saving my pocket money for a cannon cracker anyway. I will set it off very carefully, behind the stable, where my mother will not see. Do not tell anybody.

## s04-ch-schoolroom
roles: child
title: The schoolroom on the top floor

I do not go to school. School comes to me. There is a schoolroom on the top floor of our house, with a blackboard and a globe and a map of the world, and a governess who comes every morning at nine and stays until one.

I learn reading and writing and sums and geography and French and the piano. I am very good at geography and very bad at the piano. My governess says I have no ear. I can name every river in Europe and every capital in South America. When I am grown up I am going to go and see all of them. The piano I am going to leave at home.

## s04-ch-dancing-school
roles: child
title: Dancing school

On Saturday mornings I go to dancing school with all the other children of the street, in a hall downtown, with a master in a tail coat who plays the violin and counts out loud. One, two, three. One, two, three. Point your toe.

The boys have to wear white gloves and bow to the girls and ask them to dance. The girls have to curtsy and say yes. You cannot say no, even to the boy who steps on your feet. There is a boy who always steps on mine. He says he does it on purpose because he likes me. I said that was a very stupid way to show it.

## s04-ch-lake
roles: child
title: Down at the lake

Behind our house, across the railroad tracks, is the lake. We are not supposed to go there because of the trains. But the nurse takes us over the bridge across the tracks sometimes, and we go down to the shore and throw stones and watch the ships.

The lake is so big you cannot see the other side. It looks like the ocean. In a storm the waves come right over the breakwater and spray all the way to the tracks. My big brother says there are ships at the bottom of the lake, hundreds of them, with drowned sailors still inside. I do not believe him. I look anyway, every time.

## s04-ch-circus
roles: child
title: The circus came to town

In May the circus came to Chicago, with three rings at once and a parade down Michigan Avenue with elephants and camels and a lion in a cage on wheels, and a steam calliope playing tunes that you could hear for a mile.

We went on a Saturday afternoon, all the children and both nurses. I saw a lady standing on the back of a galloping horse, and a man put his head in a lion's mouth, and twelve clowns came out of one little carriage. When I am grown I am going to join the circus. I am going to be the man with the lion. My nurse says the lion will eat me. I say not if I am very polite.

## s04-ch-zoo
roles: child
title: The bear at Lincoln Park

Last Sunday we drove all the way up to Lincoln Park, on the North Side, to see the animals. There are buffaloes and deer and eagles and monkeys, and a big brown bear in a pit who stands up on his back legs and begs for peanuts.

I threw him a peanut and he caught it in his mouth. Then I threw him another one and it hit him on the nose and he looked at me very sadly. I felt so bad I gave him all my peanuts. My father said the bear was the cleverest beggar in Chicago. He said there were men on LaSalle Street who could learn from him.

## s04-ch-electric
roles: child
title: The electric light

In our house, when you turn the little brass key on the wall, the lights come on. Electric light, my father says, from wires that come all the way from a power house downtown. My grandmother still does not trust it. She keeps a candle and a box of matches on her nightstand, just in case.

Once there was a thunderstorm and all the lights went out, all over the street, and everybody had to light the old gas lamps. My grandmother sat in her room with her candle and said: I told you so. I told you so. I think she was pleased.

## s04-ch-new-baby
roles: child
title: The new baby

There is a new baby in our house. She came in the night, and in the morning my nurse took me in to see her. She is very small and very red and she does not do anything but sleep and cry. Everybody says she is beautiful. I do not think she is beautiful at all.

But yesterday she held my finger very tight, with her whole hand, and looked at me. My nurse says babies cannot see very well yet. I think she saw me. I am going to teach her to ride my pony when she is bigger. And to climb trees. And not to cry so much.

## s04-ch-ice-cream
roles: child
title: The ice cream

On Sunday, after church, the cook makes ice cream in the freezer in the yard, with ice and salt and a handle you have to turn. My brother and I take turns turning it. It takes a whole hour and your arm feels like it will fall off.

Then the cook opens it and there it is, vanilla, with real cream, so cold it hurts your teeth. And the cook gives us the dasher to lick, the paddle from inside, which has the most ice cream on it of anything. That is the best part of the whole week. Better than church. Do not tell the minister.

## s04-ch-toddler-ball
roles: toddler
title: My ball

I have a ball. It is red. It goes bounce. It went under the big chair and Nanny got it out.

I threw it at the dog. The dog ran away. Then the dog came back and I threw it again. Nanny says that is enough. It is not enough.

## s04-ch-toddler-horse
roles: toddler
title: The big horses

The big horses live in the stable. They say huff, huff. They have big feet.

The man in the hat lifted me up to touch the brown one. It was warm. Its nose was soft, like my blanket. It blew on my face. I laughed. I want to see the horses again.

## s04-ch-toddler-lake
roles: toddler
title: The big water

We went to the big water. It goes swish, swish. It is cold. It got my shoes wet.

Nanny said no, no. I found a stone. It is white and smooth. It is in my pocket. You can hold it. Give it back.

## s04-ch-toddler-train
roles: toddler
title: Choo choo

The train goes choo choo, past the garden. It is very loud. It makes the windows shake.

I wave at the train. The man on the train waved back. He did. He waved at me. Tomorrow I will wave again.
