# Tales told in the streets of Chicago on Sunday afternoon, 8 October 1871 (T-3690): the city's
# reconstructed people, every one carded, none named by a source. Reconstruction, liberty
# L-tales-3553. Nothing here is said that the city could not know that afternoon: the dry summer,
# the fires of the week, Saturday night's fire on the West Side, the new tunnels and the Water
# Works, the canal's deep cut, the horse cars, the Palmer House two weeks open.

## s71-gm-palmer
roles: gentleman, tradesman
title: How State Street was moved

Ten years ago nobody who mattered did business on State Street. Lake Street was the street: the wholesale houses, the dry goods, the banks a step away. State was a narrow muddy lane of frame shops and boarding houses.

Then Potter Palmer bought it. Not all of it, but near a mile of frontage, and he widened it, and he built a marble store at Washington and let it to Field and Leiter, and he has just opened a hotel at Monroe that he says is the finest in the West. Two weeks it has been open. Every merchant on Lake Street laughed at him and every one of them is looking for a lease on State now. One man moved a street. That is Chicago.

## s71-gm-dry
roles: gentleman, tradesman, workman, driver
title: Not a drop since July

Do you know when it last rained properly? The fourth of July. An inch since, maybe, and it has been hot and blowing out of the southwest for weeks. The leaves came down dry in September before they had turned. The pine sidewalks curl at the ends, the roofs are tar and shingle, and there is a lumber yard on every bend of the river.

There were twenty fire alarms last week, the papers say, and one of them burned four blocks on the West Side last night. The engines are worn out and so are the men. I am not a nervous man. But I have put the deed box by the door.

## s71-gm-tunnel
roles: gentleman, tradesman, youth
title: Under the river

Have you been through the LaSalle Street tunnel? It opened this summer. You go down a ramp on the South Side, under the river, and up on the North Side, and the bridge can swing for a schooner all day and you never wait. There is a walk for foot passengers beside the carriage way, lit by gas.

The Washington Street one has been open two years on the West Side. It leaked, and the papers made fun of it, but it works. In the old days half your life in this city was spent standing at a swung bridge watching the masts go by. Now the river is a thing you go under.

## s71-gm-grade
roles: gentleman, tradesman, workman
title: The year they lifted the city

When I came here the streets were at the lake's level and every spring they were a swamp. So the council raised the grade, four feet, eight feet, ten in places, and every man had to raise his house to meet it or live in a pit.

They jacked up the Tremont House, brick and all, with the guests still in it, and nobody spilled his coffee. That is why you will see a walk on one block a step higher than the next, and stairs up and down to the doors. Half the city is still not level with the other half. Mind your feet on the planks.

## s71-gm-waterworks
roles: gentleman, tradesman, lady
title: Clean water from two miles out

You remember the water? We used to get fish in it. Little ones, out of the tap. The intake was at the shore where the river came out into the lake, and you can guess what the river carries.

So they dug a tunnel two miles under the lake to a crib out in clean water and built the pumping house and that tower on the North Side, with the standpipe inside it. The tower looks like a castle out of a picture book. The water comes in clear now. The children think it was always so.

## s71-gm-canal
roles: gentleman, tradesman, workman
title: The river runs backwards

This summer they finished the deep cut on the canal and the river turned round. It used to run into the lake and take every slaughterhouse's leavings with it, out to where we drank. Now the canal pulls it the other way, down toward the Illinois.

I did not believe it until I stood on the Rush Street bridge and watched a stick float west. The smell on the South Branch is still bad, mind. They say it is better than it was. Everyone who says so lives on the North Side.

## s71-lady-sunday
roles: lady
title: The Sunday promenade

After dinner on a fine Sunday everybody walks. Michigan Avenue if you have a new bonnet, Wabash if you want to be seen without seeming to want it, the lake front if the children must run.

I wore the purple today. My husband says it is the colour of a bruise. My dressmaker says it is the colour of Paris. I have walked from Monroe to Twelfth and back and counted three other ladies in it, which my husband says proves his point and I say proves mine.

## s71-lady-field
roles: lady, needlewoman
title: The marble palace

Field and Leiter's store at State and Washington is six storeys of white marble with plate glass you could walk through if you were not careful. The clerks are young men in black coats who call you madam and bring the silks down for you one bolt at a time.

It is shut today, of course. But I walked past it after church to look in the windows, and so did half of the South Side. On a weekday you cannot get to the counter. My mother used to buy her calico from a man with a cart.

## s71-lady-church
roles: lady, gentleman
title: Second Presbyterian

Our church on Wabash Avenue is built of stone with black flecks in it, bitumen they call it, and people call it the Spotted Church. The sermon this morning was on the parable of the talents and went on for an hour and ten minutes.

The minister prays for the city every Sunday. This morning he prayed for rain. Everyone in the pews said amen louder than I have heard them say anything.

## s71-ts-horsecar
roles: tradesman, youth, workman, woman
title: The State Street cars

I ride the State Street horse car to the shop every morning. Five cents, and you stand on the back platform with the smokers if there is no seat. The car runs on iron rails laid in the street, and two horses can pull forty people in it where they could not pull ten in a wagon through the mud.

The driver stands out on the front platform in all weathers with the reins and a brake handle. In January you see the frost on his whiskers. They run them on Sunday too, which some of the ministers do not like. Everybody else does.

## s71-ts-saturday
roles: tradesman, workman, driver
title: Last night's fire

Did you see it last night? The West Side, by Canal Street, a planing mill went up about eleven and took four blocks of lumber and houses with it, all the way from Van Buren to Adams. I went over the bridge to watch. Half the city did.

Every engine in the city was there, and they fought it all night with the wind pushing it north. The firemen were black to the eyes this morning, asleep on the hose carts. Some of the engines were damaged and the hose is burnt. I hope there is nothing tonight. They need a day.

## s71-ts-clerk
roles: tradesman, youth
title: A clerk's wages

I am a clerk in a commission house on South Water Street: grain, flour, pork. Twelve dollars a week, which is good, and I sleep in a boarding house on Wabash with four other clerks in two rooms.

On Sunday we walk. You go out on the lake shore by the Water Works, or up to Lincoln Park where they are taking up the old cemetery, or you ride the car to the end of the line and back for ten cents and look at the houses. A man can see the whole city on a Sunday for a dime.

## s71-wm-lumber
roles: workman, irish, german, scandinavian
title: Shoving lumber

I shove lumber on the South Branch. The schooners come in from Michigan and Wisconsin loaded to the gunwales with pine boards, and we carry it off on our shoulders and pile it in the yards, a board at a time, from six in the morning till dark.

There is more lumber in this city than in any city in the world, they say, and I believe it, because I have carried most of it. The yards run along the river for miles. On a dry day like this, when you light your pipe out there, the foreman watches you like a hawk.

## s71-wm-stockyards
roles: workman, irish, german
title: The yards

My brother works at the Union Stock Yards, out south past the city limits. He rides out on the cars before light. A hundred acres of pens, he says, and the cattle and the hogs come in on every railroad there is, and the packing houses take them in at one end and send out barrels of pork at the other.

He comes home smelling of it. My wife makes him leave his boots on the step. But he earns two dollars a day and he is never laid off in the winter, when the packing season comes. There are worse things to smell of than money.

## s71-wm-sunday
roles: workman, driver
title: A working man's Sunday

Six days I work, and on the seventh I sit on my own step with my pipe and nobody tells me to move. That is my Sunday. Mass first, if the wife has her way, and then dinner, and then the step.

The children go off down to the river to see the boats, or out to the prairie at the end of the street to fly their kites. There is a wind today for kites. There has been a wind for a week.

## s71-ir-kilgubbin
roles: irish, workman, woman
title: Kilgubbin

The respectable people call our part of the North Side by the river Kilgubbin, after a place in Cork, and they do not mean it kindly. Shanties and pigs and geese, they say. Well, the geese are good eating and the shanties were built by men who also built their houses.

My father came over in forty-nine with nothing but the clothes on him. Now I have a house of my own on a lot I pay for by the month. It is a small house. But it is mine and it is in America.

## s71-ir-hollyfamily
roles: irish, woman, servant
title: Holy Family

The church of the Holy Family on Twelfth Street is the biggest church in the city, they say, built by the Jesuits for the Irish of the West Side out of our pennies. The priest says it holds four thousand and on a Sunday morning it is full twice over.

There is a school beside it now. My children go. They learn to read in English and to say their prayers in Latin, and they come home and tell me what everything means, as if I did not know.

## s71-de-oleary
roles: irish, woman, child, workman
title: The cows on DeKoven Street

Down our street there are houses with a barn behind and a cow in it, and the women sell the milk to the neighbours. You pay a few cents and take your own can. It is cheaper than the milk wagon and you can see the cow you are getting it from.

The barns are full of hay for the winter now, and the sheds of wood and coal, everything bone dry. My husband says a match in one of those alleys and the whole street would go. He says it every Sunday. Then he lights his pipe.

## s71-ge-northside
roles: german, tradesman, workman, woman
title: The North Side

On the North Side you can live your whole life in German: the church, the bakery, the newspaper, the Turnverein, the beer garden on a Sunday with the band playing and the children running about under the trees. The Yankees think a Sunday afternoon with music and a glass of lager is the road to ruin.

My father was a cabinet maker in Baden and I am a cabinet maker on Clark Street, and I make more in a week here than he made in a month. I have a vote and a lot of my own. On Sunday I sit in the garden with my family and nobody tells me I am going to hell for it except the people across the river.

## s71-ge-brewery
roles: german, driver, workman
title: The brewery wagon

I drive for a brewery on the North Side, and Sunday is our busy day, which some people find scandalous. The beer gardens want their kegs before three, when the band starts. The horses know the way better than I do.

More breweries in this city than I can count on two hands. Lager is a German drink and twenty years ago you could not buy it here. Now the Irish drink it, the Yankees drink it behind the curtains, and the temperance ladies stand on the corner and pray for me as I go by. I always tip my hat.

## s71-sc-milwaukee
roles: scandinavian, workman, woman
title: On Milwaukee Avenue

We came from Norway in sixty-six, on a sailing ship to Quebec and then by the lakes. My husband had a cousin on Milwaukee Avenue and that was all we knew of America: one cousin and the name of a street.

Now there are thousands of us out there, Norwegians and Swedes and Danes, with our own churches and our own newspaper. The men work in the planing mills and on the ships. I keep house and take in two boarders, which pays the rent. In winter it is like home. In summer it is hotter than any place God meant people to live.

## s71-bk-bohemia
roles: bohemian, workman, woman
title: Pilsen on the West Side

The Bohemians live south of the Irish on the West Side, by the lumber yards where the men work. We have our own church, St. Wenceslaus, and a hall for the singing society, and the old women still wear their headscarves to market.

My husband shoves lumber like the Irish and gets paid the same, which is not much. But in Bohemia we would never have owned a house. Here we have two rooms, a cellar and a garden with cabbages in it, and the cabbages are doing well in all this sun, if nothing else is.

## s71-bl-quinn
roles: black_resident, workman, woman
title: Quinn Chapel

Our church is Quinn Chapel, the African Methodist church. Before the war it was a station on the underground road: people hid in the basement on the way to Canada, and the congregation fed them and sent them on. Some of those people stayed and they are in the pews today.

There are not many of us in the city, a few thousand, most on the South Side by Clark Street. The men work as waiters and porters in the hotels and on the railroads, as barbers and coachmen. It is not a free country yet, not all the way. But it is freer than where my mother was born.

## s71-sv-dayout
roles: servant
title: My afternoon out

Every second Sunday I have from three o'clock until supper, and I walk. I go to see my sister, who is in service on Michigan Avenue, and we walk on the lake front and look at the ladies' bonnets and decide which ones we would buy.

The mistress is kind enough. I get three dollars a week and my keep, which is more than I would get in a lifetime at home, and I send half of it back to my mother in Galway. I have a new hat. I bought it myself. It has a ribbon.

## s71-yo-roof
roles: youth, child
title: From the roof

If you climb up on our roof you can see the Court House cupola, and if you climb up the Court House, which they let you do if you ask the bellman nicely, you can see the whole city to the prairie and out over the lake.

The man up there watches for fires. When he sees smoke he rings the bell and taps out the district on a telegraph, and the engines go. He has been busy all week. He told me he has never seen it so dry. He said it like it was interesting, not frightening.

## s71-ch-kites
roles: child
title: Kites on the prairie

At the end of our street the houses stop and it is all prairie, and when the wind is up we fly our kites there. Mine is red paper on two sticks with a tail of rags.

Today the wind is so strong it nearly pulled me over. My brother let his go and it went over the houses and we ran after it but it was gone. Mother says it is in Indiana by now.

## s71-ch-engine
roles: child, youth
title: The steam engine

When the fire bell rings we run to see the engine come out. The firemen jump on and the horses come out of the stalls by themselves and stand under the harness and it drops on them, and then they go, with the smoke coming out of the chimney on the engine and sparks and the bell going.

The one I like best is the Little Giant, on Maxwell Street. I am going to be a fireman. Mother says I am going to be a clerk.

## s71-nw-dress
roles: needlewoman, woman
title: Dressmaking

I make dresses in a room over a shop on Clark Street. Every lady wants the new shape this year, with all the fullness drawn back and a bustle under it, and an overskirt looped up over the skirt, and three flounces at the hem, and trimming on all of it. Thirty yards of silk in one dress, some of them.

I work from seven to nine six days a week, with two girls under me. On Sunday I do not touch a needle. I sit in the window and watch the ladies go by in my dresses and see which of them are wearing them properly. Most are not.

## s71-dr-express
roles: driver, workman
title: Meeting the trains

I drive an express wagon. On Sunday the trains still come in, and somebody has to take the trunks from the depot to the hotels, and that is me. The Sherman House, the Tremont, the new Palmer House.

There are six railroad depots in this city and no two of them are in the same place, so a man going from New York to California has to cross Chicago in a hack and his trunk has to cross in my wagon. I am in favour of it. I would be out of work if they ever built one big depot.

## s71-dr-milk
roles: driver
title: The milk round

I am up at three. Down to the dairy, load the cans, and out by five, every day God sends, Sundays included, because a baby does not know what day it is.

The horse knows every stop. He stops at the door before I tell him and waits while I fill the jug and goes on again by himself. I could sleep on the seat and he would bring me home. Some mornings I think I have.
