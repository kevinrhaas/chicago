# Tales told by the men of Heald's company and the Chicago militia, 14 August 1812.
# Format: see ../README.md. Every tale is a reconstruction told in the teller's voice.

## s12-sol-the-letter
roles: soldier
title: The letter from Detroit
meet: winnemeg

Five days ago a chief called Winnemeg walked in at the gate with a letter from General Hull, and the whole post went quiet. We are to leave the fort. Give up Fort Dearborn, hand out the goods, and march for Fort Wayne. Some of the old hands said it plain: the safest walls between here and Detroit are the ones we are standing behind.

Winnemeg told Mr. Kinzie we should go at once, before the camps fill, or else stay and hold the place. We did neither. We waited, and every day more lodges went up on the prairie. If you want to know what he truly thinks, find him. He came a long way to carry bad news, and he does not look like a man who enjoyed it.

## s12-sol-river-of-grog
roles: soldier, militia
title: The night the river tasted of grog

Last night we carried the barrels out of the sally port in the dark and stove in their heads at the river's edge. Every drop of the post's whiskey went into the Chicago River. The captain will not hand liquor to men who may be at our backs on the march, and I cannot fault him for that.

But you could smell it on the water this morning, and the men from the camps smelled it too. They came down to the bank and stood looking at the river as if it had been robbed. Then we heard the powder and the spare muskets went down the well. They had been promised those goods. A promise broken in the dark is worse than one refused in daylight. I would not want to be the man who explains it to them.

## s12-sol-gill
roles: soldier
title: A gill a day
meet: ronan_george

Ensign Ronan signed an order last November that each man was to have his gill of whiskey. One gill, mind, measured out by the sergeant with a face like a church deacon. The ensign is the youngest officer in the fort, not long out of West Point, and some of the men laughed at him behind his back for being so careful with his signature.

I stopped laughing when I saw him at drill. He is earnest. He reads his manual by candle and he knows every man's name. If we have to fight our way down the shore, I would rather have a boy who reads the book than a man who has forgotten it. Go and find him if you like. He will tell you he is ready, and he will mean it.

## s12-sol-five-years
roles: soldier
title: Five years for five dollars

I put my mark on the paper for five years and five dollars a month, and I was told I would see the country. I have seen it. I have seen a sandbar, a river the colour of tea, and a prairie so wide that a man walking toward you takes an hour to arrive.

The pay comes late when it comes at all. We grow our own corn and cabbages in the garden by the river, and we cut our own firewood, and in winter we break the ice for water. A soldier here is half a farmer and half a carpenter, and he is a soldier only when the drum says so. Now the drum says march. I find I do not want to leave the cabbages.

## s12-sol-fifer
roles: soldier
title: The fifer's tunes
meet: burnett_george

Listen at dawn tomorrow and you will hear George Burnett's fife before you hear the birds. He has been on the rolls since 1806 and he can play every call the army ever wrote, and a few it never did. He plays reveille as if he is angry at the sun for being late.

In the winter, when the snow comes up to the pickets and there is nothing to do but mend boots, he plays jigs in the barracks and the men dance with each other because there is nobody else to dance with. I have seen a sergeant waltz a corporal round the stove. If you want to hear him, ask nicely. He pretends to be shy and then plays until you beg him to stop.

## s12-sol-drummers
roles: soldier
title: The drums tell the day
meet: hamilton_john

Everything here runs to the drum. The drum wakes you, the drum calls you to roll, the drum sends you to dinner and to bed. John Hamilton has beaten it since 1808, and Hugh McPherson beats the other one. Between them they have more say over my life than the President.

John told me once that a drummer learns to read a man's face on the parade, because he sees the whole company at once while every other man sees only the back of the head in front. He says this week the faces have changed. Men look at the gate, and then at the lodges, and then at the lake. He keeps his beat steady anyway. He says that is what a drum is for.

## s12-sol-hays-kindness
roles: soldier
title: The sergeant who made friends
meet: hays_otho

Sergeant Otho Hays is the bravest man in the company, and the strangest. He goes out among the lodges when he is off duty and comes back with smoked fish and a new word or two. There is a chief from the Calumet village, Nau-non-gee, whom he has helped more times than I can count, with a mended gun lock, a blanket in a hard winter, a kind word when nobody else had one.

The men tease him that he will marry into the tribe. He only says that a man who lives among neighbours ought to know them. I think about that now, with the camps grown so large. Go and ask him what Nau-non-gee told him this summer. He will not answer me. Perhaps he will answer you.

## s12-sol-kelso-run
roles: soldier
title: The run from Lee's farm
meet: kelso_john

In April, John Kelso was out at Lee's farm on the South Branch, a free man then with his time served. Just before sunset a party of strangers who had come in friendly turned on the farm, and Liberty White and the Canadian Cardin were killed. Kelso and a boy ran. They ran the whole way down the river with the light going, and they came to the gate shouting.

That night every family in the settlement came into the fort. Four weeks later Kelso signed on again. He said he had done his running and now he would rather have a musket and a wall. He does not talk about it much. If you find him, do not ask him about the run. Ask him about the boy. He is proud of the boy.

## s12-sol-barracks-wives
roles: soldier
title: Wives in the barracks
meet: corbin_fielding

Fielding Corbin did his five years, went out, and came back again, and this time he brought a wife. She is the steadiest person in the fort. She does the washing for half the company, she knows which of us drinks and which of us lies, and she has said to anyone who will listen that she will never be taken alive by anyone.

She is expecting a child. Corbin walks about like a man carrying a full bucket, careful of every step. He has asked to march beside her wagon tomorrow and the sergeant said yes before he had finished asking. If you want to see a soldier who is truly afraid, find Corbin. It is not for himself.

## s12-sol-old-mott
roles: soldier
title: Old Mott
meet: mortt_august

The oldest man in the company is August Mortt, a German we call Old Mott. He has been a soldier since before some of our drummers were born. He fought somewhere in Europe, he says, though which war and on which side changes depending on the day.

What he will tell you, always the same, is that an army is safe while it stands still behind a wall and in danger the moment it walks out of one. He has said it every night this week. The young men tell him to hush. I do not. Old men who have lived through marches have earned the right to be gloomy. Go and find him if you want to hear about Europe. Bring tobacco.

## s12-sol-father-son
roles: soldier
title: A father and son in one company
meet: smith_john_jr

There are two John Smiths in this company, and they are father and son. The young one is a fifer. The old one is a private who pretends he does not watch the boy at drill, and who watches him at drill.

They enlisted three years apart, and they sleep in the same barracks room, and when the young one plays a sour note the old one coughs. That is the whole of their arguing. I asked the father once why he brought his son to the end of the earth. He said he did not bring him, the boy followed. Find the young Smith and ask him. He will roll his eyes and say his father followed him.

## s12-sol-wagon
roles: soldier
title: The wagon detail
meet: mills_elias

Elias Mills and William Moffett have the fort's wagon, and between the two of them they have the patience of a whole church. The oxen here are as stubborn as the officers, and the sand along the lake shore takes a wheel up to the hub.

Tomorrow the wagons will carry the children and the sick. Mills has been greasing axles since noon and checking every pin twice. He says a wagon that breaks on a march is a coffin with wheels. He means it as a joke, but nobody laughs. Find him at the wagon, if you like. He will show you how to hitch an ox, and he will not let you leave until you have done it properly.

## s12-sol-wells-arrives
roles: soldier, militia
title: Captain Wells and his face
meet: wells_william

Yesterday Captain William Wells rode in from Fort Wayne with Miami men behind him. Wells was taken by the Miami as a boy and grew up among them, and married a daughter of Little Turtle himself. He has fought on both sides of every war in this country, and both sides say he is the best scout alive.

He is Mrs. Heald's uncle. He came to bring us home safe. I watched him talk to the captain in the yard, and I watched his face, which is a hard face to read. When he turned away from the captain he looked at the prairie for a long time. Find him if you can. Ask him what he thinks of tomorrow. I did not have the courage.

## s12-sol-council
roles: soldier
title: The council and the cannon

Two days ago the captain went out to hold a council with the chiefs on the esplanade. The other officers would not go. They stayed in the blockhouse, and I heard one of them say the guns were trained on the council the whole time, in case of trouble.

I do not know if that is true. I know the chiefs could see the ports of the blockhouse, and a man can tell when he is being watched over a barrel. The captain promised them the goods of the factory, the guns and powder and the liquor, and they agreed to let us pass. Now the powder is in the well and the liquor is in the river. I keep thinking about how that council looked from the other side.

## s12-sol-letters-home
roles: soldier
title: A letter I cannot send

I wrote to my mother in May. It went east with the express rider, if he ever got there. I wrote to her again in July, but by then the war had come and nobody would carry it. I have it here inside my coat.

I told her about the lake, which is so big you would swear it was the sea, except that you can drink it. I told her about the cranes on the marsh, taller than a boy, that dance in the spring. I did not tell her that the post is surrounded or that the British took Mackinac in July without a shot. A letter is for the things you want your mother to keep. The rest you keep yourself.

## s12-sol-mackinac
roles: soldier, militia
title: The fall of Mackinac

The worst news this summer did not come with an arrow. It came in a sentence. The British took Fort Mackinac on the seventeenth of July, and the American garrison there gave up without firing a shot because they did not even know there was a war.

Think on that. A fort on an island, in the middle of the straits, and it fell in a morning. If Mackinac can fall, so can Detroit, and if Detroit falls there is nothing between us and the whole British army but trees. The men from the camps heard the news before we did. That is how you know who your neighbours have been talking to.

## s12-sol-the-belt
roles: soldier, militia
title: The red belt
meet: main_poc_runner

This morning a runner came in to the camps carrying a big red belt. The interpreters went pale. A red belt is a call to war, and they say he came from Main Poc, the war leader who has gone over to the British and hates every American he has ever met.

The runner's name is Fox Running. He brought news of British victories in the north, and the young men gathered round him like flies on honey. I watched from the bastion. He did not look at the fort once, the whole time he was talking. That is how I knew he was talking about us. Find him if you dare. He will not lie to you about what he carries.

## s12-sol-sick-list
roles: soldier
title: The sick list
meet: van_voorhis_isaac

Every soldier here has had the ague. You shake until your teeth knock, then you burn, then you shake again. Doctor Van Voorhis doses us with bark and calomel and tells us it is the marsh air. He is a young man from Fishkill on the Hudson, and he writes long letters home about how this country will be rich one day.

He is right, I think. But he will not be here to see it, and neither will I. The sick are to ride in the wagons tomorrow. The doctor has been up two nights counting blankets and bandages. If you find him, tell him to sleep. He listens to no one, but he might listen to a stranger.

## s12-sol-crozier
roles: soldier
title: An old friend from the north
meet: crozier_john

Sergeant Crozier has been here since 1808, and in that time he has made friends you would not expect. There is a Chippewa man from up the lake who comes to see him every summer, and the two of them sit by the river and smoke and say almost nothing for an afternoon.

I asked him once what they talk about. He said they talk about fish, and weather, and how foolish young men are, which is the same talk old soldiers have everywhere. I believe a friend like that is worth more than a cannon in a bad week. Find the sergeant and ask him if his friend has come this year. Watch his face when he answers.

## s12-sol-griffith-birds
roles: soldier, militia
title: The linden birds
meet: griffith_william

William Griffith was a settler until April. After the killings at Lee's farm he joined the army right here at Chicago and he is a sergeant already, because he can read and write and does not panic.

He told me something I cannot get out of my head. One of the chiefs, a friend to the fort, told him that the linden birds had been singing in his ears. Griffith did not understand it at first. Then the interpreter explained. It means the chief has heard things he cannot say out loud, warnings carried on the wind. Griffith asked him what the birds said. The chief only looked at him. Find Griffith and ask him who the chief was.

## s12-sol-nightwatch
roles: soldier
title: On the night watch

I had the watch on the north-west blockhouse two nights ago. You can see the river from there and the Kinzie house beyond it, with its poplars, and past that the dark of the prairie. That night the prairie was not dark. There were fires out there, more than I could count, all the way to the trees.

You hear things on a night watch. A dog. A drum, far off. Singing, sometimes, that rises and falls and stops all at once. I am not a superstitious man, but I said a prayer up there that I have not said since I was a boy. When the relief came up the ladder I nearly shot him.

## s12-sol-who-stays
roles: soldier, militia
title: The man who will not go
meet: ouilmette_antoine

Antoine Ouilmette was here before the fort was built, or so he says, and his wife is Potawatomi. He works the portage for Mr. Kinzie, carrying packs across the mud to the Des Plaines. He never joined the militia and he is not marching tomorrow.

He told me he is going to stay home. Just that. Stay home and mind his garden. The officers think he is a fool or a traitor. I think he is the one man in Chicago who knows exactly where he belongs. If you want to know how a man can be at home in a place like this, ask him. He will offer you a pipe and talk about the portage until you fall asleep.

## s12-sol-musket
roles: soldier
title: Cleaning a musket

Watch a soldier clean his musket on a day like this and you will learn everything you need to know about him. One man scours the barrel until it shines like a spoon. One man does it in three strokes and goes to find his dinner. One man takes the lock apart and puts it back together four times, because his hands need something to do.

I am the third kind today. Tomorrow we carry twenty-five rounds each. The rest of the powder went into the well. The captain says we must not leave anything for our enemies. The men from the camps say the captain promised it to them. Both of them are right, and that is the trouble.

## s12-sol-garden
roles: soldier
title: The fort garden

We planted the garden in May, when we were all living inside the walls after the killings in April. Beans, cabbages, turnips, a row of potatoes, and corn the way the Potawatomi women showed us, in hills. The women across the river laughed at our hills. They said we planted like men who had never been hungry.

The corn is coming in now. Fine ears, tall as a man. And we are leaving it. Some fellow from the camps will eat my turnips this winter, and I hope they choke him, and I hope they feed his children. Both at once. That is how a man feels when he leaves his garden.

## s12-sol-boy-soldier
roles: soldier
title: Why I enlisted

I enlisted at seventeen because a recruiting sergeant in Pennsylvania bought me supper and told me about the West. He did not mention the mosquitoes. He did not mention the fevers, or the winters, or the way the wind comes off the lake in January and goes straight through a wool coat as if it were paper.

But he did not lie about the West. I have seen buffalo on the far prairie, a dark smudge moving like a cloud shadow. I have seen the lake turn green and gold and grey in a single afternoon. If I get home I will tell my brothers about it, and they will not believe a word.

## s12-sol-coming-out
roles: soldier
title: The order of march

The order of march came down this afternoon. Captain Wells and some of his Miami in front. Then the regulars. Then the wagons with the women and children and the sick, and the militia with them. Then the rest of the Miami at the back. We go down the beach, along the lake, toward Fort Wayne, a hundred and fifty miles.

Somebody asked the sergeant what music we would march out to. He said the band will play what the captain orders. Somebody else said they had heard it would be the Dead March. The sergeant told him to hold his tongue. Nobody spoke for a long time after that.

## s12-sol-bake-oven
roles: soldier
title: Bread for the road

The bakers have been at the oven since before dawn, making hard bread for the march. Fifty-odd soldiers, a dozen militia, the women and the children, all to be fed for a hundred and fifty miles, and the factory's flour is mostly gone to the camps.

I carried sacks for them this morning. The baker is a sour man who has never said a kind word to me, but he slipped me a soft roll still hot from the oven and told me to eat it quick before the captain saw. I did. It was the best thing I have eaten at this post. Funny, what you remember.

## s12-sol-wives-ride
roles: soldier
title: The women will ride
meet: holt_isaac

Sergeant Holt has given his wife his own horse for the march, and he has been teaching her to ride it astride, like a man, so she can stay on in the sand. She is not a natural horsewoman. Twice today I saw her slide off and twice she got back on, red in the face, and told him to stop laughing.

He was not laughing. He was standing with his hand on the bridle, very still. Find Sergeant Holt if you like. He will tell you she rides better than he does. That is a lie, but it is a good one.

## s12-mil-twelve
roles: militia
title: The twelve of the militia
meet: burns_thomas

After the killings in April, the captain made soldiers of every grown man in the settlement who was not already one. Twelve of us. Thomas Burns is our sergeant, an old soldier himself who stayed on here when his time was up and married a widow with a houseful of children.

We drill on the parade with the regulars, and they laugh at us, because we march like farmers going to market, which is what we are. But every one of us has a family inside these walls. A man fights differently when the wagon behind him holds his own children. Find Burns. He will tell you we are getting better. That is very nearly true.

## s12-mil-burns-bed
roles: militia, settler_man
title: Carried on her bed
meet: burns_mrs

The night we all came into the fort in April, Mrs. Burns had given birth only the day before. She could not walk. So Mr. Kinzie and his men carried her down to the boat on her bed, mattress and blankets and baby and all, and rowed her across the river with the newborn in her arms.

I helped hold the lantern. She did not make a sound the whole way except to tell the boatmen to mind the baby's head. The baby is fat now and has a voice like a bugle. If you see Mrs. Burns, tell her I said the baby takes after her. She will know who it was.

## s12-mil-lees-son
roles: militia, settler_man
title: Lee's boy
meet: lee_charles

Charles Lee feeds the fort. His farm out on the South Branch grows the wheat and the beef, or it did until April, when the farm was attacked and his men killed. His boy got away that day, running down the river to the fort with the alarm.

Lee has not been back to the farm since. He drills with the militia and he looks at the South Branch every evening from the walls. His boy marches beside him. They are very alike, both quiet, both stubborn. Ask Lee about the farm and he will tell you about next year's wheat as if there will be a next year. I hope he is right.
