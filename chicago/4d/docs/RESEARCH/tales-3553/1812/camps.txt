# More tales of the gathering on the prairie, told by the Potawatomi and the Miami, 14 August 1812.

## s12-pm-canoe-lake
roles: potawatomi_man
title: The lake took my brother's canoe

Two springs ago my brother and I went out on the big lake in a bark canoe to set nets for whitefish. The lake was flat as a dish when we left. By noon it was a wall of grey water, higher than a lodge, and the wind came out of the north-east like a fist.

We turned for the shore and the waves threw us onto the sandbar at the river mouth. The canoe broke in two. My brother could not swim well, and I held him by the hair until our feet found sand. We walked home soaked, with no fish and no canoe, and our mother laughed until she cried and then cried until she laughed. The lake is not a river. It does not care who you are. Never trust it on a calm morning.

## s12-pm-sauk-trade
roles: potawatomi_man
title: Trading with the Sauk

Every few summers I go west to the Rock River to trade with the Sauk. They have horses from the far plains and lead from the mines near the Mississippi, and we have goods from the traders here at the lake. One of their war leaders there is a hard man called Black Hawk, who hates the Americans almost as much as Main Poc does.

He told me the Americans took the Sauk land along the Mississippi with a treaty that his people never agreed to, signed by men who were drunk and had no right. He says he will never forgive it. I believe him. I have never met a man who held a grudge so carefully, like a coal kept alive in a shell. One day, I think, that coal will set the prairie burning.

## s12-pm-names
roles: potawatomi_man
title: How I got my name

When I was a baby my grandfather gave me my name. He had a dream, and in the dream he saw what my name should be, and he stood up at the naming feast and said it out loud four times so that the spirits would hear it. Then everyone said it with him, and I was a person.

The soldiers cannot say my name. They call me by some word that sounds a little like it, or by the colour of my blanket, or they do not call me anything. A man without a name to his neighbours is a stranger to them. It is easy to fear a stranger. It is easy to hurt one.

## s12-pm-kinzie-silver
roles: potawatomi_man
title: Forty brooches for a winter
meet: kinzie_john_elder

Last spring I came in from the winter grounds with a good pack: forty muskrat, twelve otter, a bear hide, a bale of deer skins. I took it all to the trader with the big house. I paid my debt for the powder and the blanket, and I had enough left for forty silver brooches for my wife.

The trader made them himself at his bench while I watched, beating the silver thin and cutting the holes with a little chisel. He makes them better than anyone. He asked after my wife by name. That is why we trade with him and not the government store. A man who knows your wife's name is not only a trader. Find him if you want to see the silver.

## s12-pm-river-names
roles: potawatomi_man
title: The river of the wild onion

The river here is called after the wild onion, or the skunk, depending on who tells it: the plant that grows thick in the wet ground along the banks and smells strong when you crush it. The French wrote the name down as best they could, and the Americans write it the way the French did.

It is a poor little river. Slow and muddy and almost nothing in summer. But it is the door between the great lake and the great river to the west, and so every people who ever wanted to go anywhere has come through it. That is why there is a fort here. That is why there are so many of us here this week. Doors are where people quarrel.

## s12-pm-elk
roles: potawatomi_man
title: The last elk on the prairie

When I was a boy there were elk on the prairie west of here, big herds of them, and buffalo too, some years. I saw my first buffalo when I was eight, a whole dark river of them flowing across the grass at the far edge of the sky.

Now the elk are few and the buffalo do not come this far east any more. The hunters say the guns have frightened them away, or the fires, or the fort. My son has never seen a buffalo. I have told him what they look like. He thinks I am making it up, the way old men do.

## s12-pm-son-fort
roles: potawatomi_man
title: My son wants to see the soldiers

My son is ten and he wants to go to the fort every day to watch the soldiers march. He stands at the edge of the parade with his friends and copies them, stiff-legged, with a stick on his shoulder, and the soldiers laugh and the sergeant shouts at them to stop laughing.

This week I have forbidden him to go. He asked me why, and I did not have a good answer. I told him the soldiers are busy. He said the soldiers are always busy. He is right. I am a coward in front of my own son. How do you tell a boy that his friends may be his enemies tomorrow?

## s12-pm-tecumseh
roles: potawatomi_man
title: I heard Tecumseh speak

Two summers ago Tecumseh came to our village and spoke at the council fire. He is not a tall man, but when he speaks you cannot look anywhere else. He said the red people are one people, from the lakes to the gulf, and that the land belongs to all of us together, and that no chief may sell any of it without the consent of all.

He said: the white people are like a flood. One drop at a time it does not seem like much. But if every nation stands alone, the flood will cover us one by one. Stand together and it cannot. I have thought about those words every day since. I think about them tonight more than ever.

## s12-pm-gun-mend
roles: potawatomi_man
title: The man who mends guns
meet: hays_otho

My gun is an English trade gun, the lock gone loose from a bad fall on the ice. In the spring I took it to the fort, because there is a sergeant there named Hays who is good with locks, and kind. He sat on the bank with me for an hour with his little tools and fixed it, and would take nothing for it but a pipe of tobacco.

I have thought about that sergeant all week. He did not need to help me. He did it because he is the kind of man who helps. In every people there are men like that, and they are always the ones who get hurt. If you see him, tell him the lock still holds.

## s12-pm-wife-council
roles: potawatomi_man
title: My wife sits in council too

The soldiers think only men decide things among us. They are wrong. The women decide who will be chief, among some of our families, and the women decide whether there will be food for a war party, and the women decide whether a captive will be adopted into a family or not.

Last night my wife sat with the other women of our lodges for a long time, and when she came back she told me what they had decided. I will not tell you what it was. But I will tell you that I listened. A man who does not listen to his wife in a week like this is a man who will be sorry.

## s12-pm-wabash-men
roles: potawatomi_man
title: The men from the Wabash

Some of the men who have come in this week are from the Wabash, from the Prophet's people, and they are different from us. They do not trade with Kinzie. They do not know the soldiers by name. They have no friends inside those walls, only enemies.

When they speak at the fires, the young men of our own villages listen to them like children to a storyteller. Our chiefs say, these soldiers are our neighbours. The Wabash men say, these soldiers are the ones who burned our town. Both are telling the truth. It is the second truth that is louder.

## s12-pm-goods
roles: potawatomi_man
title: The goods from the store

Today the soldiers gave out the goods from the government store: cloth, blankets, kettles, paint, tobacco. People carried them away to their lodges. I took a blanket for my mother, a good red one.

But the guns were not given out, or the powder, or the whiskey. The captain promised them at the council two days ago, I was there and heard it. And now they say the guns are broken and in the well and the whiskey is in the river. My mother says a blanket is a blanket and to be glad of it. My mother is wiser than the young men. But I am one of the young men.

## s12-pm-milwaukee
roles: potawatomi_man
title: The road from Milwaukee

I came down from Milwaukee three days ago with a party of twenty, along the lake shore trail. At every creek we met others going the same way, from the north and the west, families with their horses and dogs and kettles, all walking toward Chicago.

It was like the whole country was moving, like birds before a storm. Nobody had told us to come. We came because everybody was coming. That is how great things happen, I think. Not because a chief says so. Because everybody starts walking in the same direction at once and nobody can stop.

## s12-pm-lalime-friend
roles: potawatomi_man
title: The interpreter who was killed

The interpreter, Lalime, was a quiet man and a fair one. When we went to the fort to speak to the captain, he put our words into English carefully, without adding or taking away. Not every interpreter does that.

Then in June the trader Kinzie killed him at the gate, and the soldiers did nothing. Kinzie went away for a while and came back, and now he sits on his porch looking at Lalime's grave. I am not saying Kinzie was in the wrong. Nobody knows what happened between them. I am saying that a fort where a man can kill the interpreter and walk free is not a place that keeps its own laws. Why would it keep its word to us?

## s12-pm-elders-advice
roles: potawatomi_man
title: My uncle's advice

My uncle took me aside last night. He is an old man and he has fought in more wars than I have years. He said: when it starts, and it will start, do not be the first to run forward, and do not be the last to stay. The first are killed and the last are disgraced. Be in the middle.

Then he said: and when it is over, remember that you will have to live with what you did for the rest of your life. Do only what you can live with. Then he went to sleep. I have not slept at all.

## s12-pm-muskrat
roles: potawatomi_man
title: A winter of muskrats

Muskrats live in the marshes in little houses of reeds and mud, like tiny lodges. In winter, when the ice is thick, you walk out on the marsh and break into their houses with a spear, very quietly, and catch them inside. Their fur is soft and the traders like it, and their meat is good to eat.

Last winter I took two hundred. My fingers froze so badly I could not close my hand for a week. My wife rubbed them with fat and scolded me for being greedy. I said I was not greedy, I was in debt. She said those were the same thing with the traders. She is right about most things.

## s12-pm-dog
roles: potawatomi_man
title: My dog knows

My dog has been strange all week. She growls at the soldiers when they come near the river, which she never did before. She will not leave my side at night. She looks toward the fort and whines.

The old people say dogs can see what is coming before men can. I do not know if that is true. But I know my dog is not a fool, and she has decided something. I scratched her ears tonight and told her she was a good dog. She looked at me as if she wanted to tell me something, and could not. I know the feeling.

## s12-pm-black-partridge-village
roles: potawatomi_man
title: From Black Partridge's village
meet: black_partridge

I am from Black Partridge's village on the Illinois River, across from the head of Peoria Lake. He is a good chief. He has kept the peace with the Americans for seventeen years and he has kept us out of every war that came near.

This week he has been sitting with the other chiefs from morning until night, and when he comes back to our fire he does not speak. Last night I saw him take off his American medal and hold it in his hand for a long time, looking at it. Then he put it back on. Go and see him if you can. You will understand what kind of man he is when you look at his face.

## s12-pm-wells-scout
roles: potawatomi_man
title: The scout's reputation
meet: wells_william

Everyone in this camp knows the name of Wells. When the Americans' armies came into our country twenty years ago, Wells was scouting for them. He knew where our villages were, and how we fought, because he had been one of the Miami himself. Many of our people died because of what he knew.

So when he rode in yesterday with his Miami, some of the young men spat on the ground. Some of the older men nodded to him, because they remember him from before, when he was Little Turtle's son-in-law and the Americans' enemy. A man with two lives has two kinds of enemies. Wells has more than anyone.

## s12-pm-ball-stick
roles: potawatomi_man
title: My ball stick

This is my ball stick. I made it from a hickory sapling three summers ago, bent while it was green and tied until it set, with a net of rawhide at the end. It has played in nine great games. I have won six.

I will not play with it this summer. There is a different game coming, and it is played with different sticks. But I am going to keep it beside me tonight anyway, for luck. A man needs something from the old days in his hand on a night like this.

## s12-pw-new-dress
roles: potawatomi_woman
title: A dress for my daughter

I have been sewing a dress for my daughter all summer, of red stroud from the trader, with ribbon at the hem and twenty brooches across the shoulders. She will wear it at the dances in the fall when the people come in from the hunting grounds, and the young men will look at her.

Tonight I finished the last brooch. She tried it on by the fire and turned round and round, and the brooches chimed. She is so beautiful. I did not tell her what I was afraid of. I told her the young men had better watch out. She laughed. I want to remember that laugh for the rest of my life.

## s12-pw-wild-rice
roles: potawatomi_woman
title: Wild rice in the north

My mother came from the north, near the Menominee country, where the wild rice grows in the shallow lakes. In the fall the women go out in canoes, two to a boat, one pushing with a pole and one bending the stalks over and knocking the grains in with sticks. Then you parch it over a fire and dance on it to loosen the husks.

There is no wild rice here at the river. It is too muddy, and the water is wrong. My mother missed it every fall. She used to say a woman without wild rice in her stores was a poor woman, however rich her husband. I never ate it until I was grown. Now I would give anything for one bowl.

## s12-pw-kinzie-wife
roles: potawatomi_woman
title: The trader's wife
meet: kinzie_eleanor

The trader's wife, Mrs. Kinzie, speaks some of the languages of the eastern nations, because she lived among the Senecas when she was a little girl. They took her from her family and adopted her, and she loved them, and when her people came to take her back she cried.

So she is not like the other American women. She looks at us as if we are people. She has eaten in my lodge, and I have eaten in her house, and her daughter Margaret used to run in and out of our camp when she was small. Find her if you can. She will treat you kindly, whoever you are. That is how she is.

## s12-pw-horses-grass
roles: potawatomi_woman
title: Too many horses

There are so many horses in this camp now that the grass is eaten down to the dirt for a mile in every direction. My boys take ours farther out each day to find grazing, and come back later each night. I worry about them out on the prairie in the dark.

Everything is too much this week. Too many people, too many horses, too many fires, too much talking. Not enough food, not enough grass, not enough sleep. When a place has too much of the wrong things and not enough of the right ones, something has to break. I only hope it breaks gently.

## s12-pw-baby-board
roles: potawatomi_woman
title: The cradle board

My baby rides on my back in a cradle board, laced in snug with moss under him to keep him dry. His grandfather carved the board and the hoop over his face, so if I drop him the hoop takes the blow. From the hoop hang little things to watch: a shell, a carved bird, a string of beads.

He watches the whole world from up there, over my shoulder, and never cries. The soldiers' wives think it is cruel to lace a baby so tight. I think it is cruel to let a baby kick and scream on the floor, as they do. Each people thinks the other people raises children wrong. Every mother is sure.

## s12-pw-sister-fort
roles: potawatomi_woman
title: My sister married a Frenchman in the fort

My sister married one of the Frenchmen who works for the trader, years ago, and she lives in his log house on the north bank with their children. When the settlers ran into the fort in April, she went with them, because her husband did.

So now my sister is inside the walls and I am outside them. We stood on either side of the river this morning and waved to each other. She held up her youngest so I could see how big he has grown. I wanted to shout across to her, come out, come home. I did not. She has made her choice. I pray it is the right one.

## s12-pw-elder-woman
roles: potawatomi_woman, potawatomi_elder
title: An old woman's work

I am old, and old women have a special work: we remember. We remember who is related to whom, and which family owes which, and who was born in which winter. When the young people forget, they come to us.

And we have another work, which nobody speaks of. When there is fighting, it is the old women who decide what happens afterwards. Who is adopted. Who is spared. Who is sent home. The men think they make the war. They do. But we make the peace that comes after. Remember that, if anyone tells you the women have no power here.

## s12-pw-quillwork
roles: potawatomi_woman
title: Quills before ribbons

Before the traders brought ribbon, my grandmother decorated everything with porcupine quills. You soften them in your mouth, flatten them with your teeth, dye them with roots and berries, and sew them down in patterns so fine you would think they were painted.

I still do quillwork on moccasins for my family, though it takes ten times as long as ribbon. My daughter says why bother, when the trader has ribbon in every colour. I tell her: because your grandmother's hands taught mine, and mine will teach yours, and when you sew quills you are holding her hands. She rolls her eyes. One day she will understand.

## s12-pch-race
roles: potawatomi_child
title: I won the race

Today the boys of our camp raced the boys from the Milwaukee camp, all the way from the big oak to the river and back. I won. I ran so fast my feet hardly touched the grass, and the Milwaukee boy behind me fell in a muskrat hole.

My grandfather saw it and gave me a piece of dried meat as a prize, and said that when he was young he could run all day without stopping, from sunrise to sunset. My grandmother said he could not, he could only talk all day without stopping. Everybody laughed. It was the first time anybody laughed all week.

## s12-pch-dogs
roles: potawatomi_child
title: The puppies

Our dog had five puppies in the spring, under the edge of the lodge. I got to name them all. One is called Little Thunder because he barks so loud, and one is called Mud because he is always muddy, and one is called Sleepy.

I wanted to give one to the boy in the fort, the one with the yellow hair. My mother said no, not this week. I asked her when. She said maybe in the fall. I am saving Sleepy for him. Sleepy is the nicest one.

## s12-pch-grandmother-story
roles: potawatomi_child
title: My grandmother's summer story

In summer my grandmother will not tell the real stories, only made-up ones. Tonight she told one about a raccoon who wanted to wash everything in the world, even the moon, and he waited by the lake every night until the moon came down to the water, and then he tried to wash it, and it broke into a hundred pieces.

Then he cried, because he thought he had broken the moon. But the next night it was whole again, up in the sky. Grandmother said: some things that look broken are only resting. Then she sent us to sleep. My little sister fell asleep right away. I lay awake thinking about the moon.

## s12-pch-sister
roles: potawatomi_child
sex: f
title: My mother's dress

My mother finished my new dress today, red, with twenty silver brooches on the shoulders. When I turn round it rings like little bells. I am going to wear it at the dances in the fall.

I asked if I could wear it tomorrow, to show the other girls. My mother said no, very fast, in a strange voice. Then she hugged me too tight. I do not know why grown-ups are so strange this week. I put my dress away carefully with the brooches inside so they will not get scratched.

## s12-pch-boy-bow
roles: potawatomi_child
sex: m
title: My first bird

Today I shot my first bird with my little bow, a blackbird in the reeds by the river. My father made the bow from ash and the arrows from cane, and he has been teaching me since the spring snow.

I brought it to my mother and she cooked it in the fire and gave the first piece to my grandfather, because that is how it is done with a boy's first game. My grandfather ate it slowly and said it was the best meal of his life. I know it was only a blackbird. But I felt very tall.

## s12-mia-kekionga
roles: miami
title: Our town at the three rivers

Our town is at Kekionga, where the St. Joseph and the St. Marys rivers join to make the Maumee. It was the greatest town of the Miami, with the cornfields stretching for miles. The Americans built Fort Wayne right beside it, after General Wayne beat us all at Fallen Timbers.

So we live beside a fort, as the Potawatomi do here. We know what it is like, to have soldiers for neighbours. Some days they are good neighbours and some days they are not. It depends on the captain, and on the news from the east, and on the whiskey. Mostly on the whiskey.

## s12-mia-wells-wife
roles: miami
title: Sweet Breeze
meet: wells_william

Wells married Little Turtle's daughter, Sweet Breeze, when he was still a Miami warrior. She bore him children, and she died young, years ago. Her children are Miami and they are American, both, like their father.

Wells sent his children to be schooled in Kentucky, but he keeps them close in his heart. On the road here he told me that his daughter had written him a letter in English with every word spelled wrong, and that he kept it in his coat. He showed it to me. I could not read it. But I could see how he held it.

## s12-mia-tired
roles: miami
title: We are a tired people

The Miami fought the Americans for twenty years and won great battles, and lost, and lost again. Then at Greenville we made peace and promised to keep it, and we have. Our chief Little Turtle kept it even when the young men wanted war, even when Tecumseh came to our town and called him a coward.

Now Little Turtle is dead, a month ago, and the young men are restless again. I do not want war. I have buried a father and two brothers. I want to go home and plant corn and grow old. That is what I am riding here for: to bring these soldiers home so that maybe, just maybe, there will be one less war.

## s12-mia-road-home
roles: miami
title: The road along the lake

Tomorrow we go back the way we came, along the lake shore, then inland through the woods to Fort Wayne. It is a long road, through the sand and then through the swamps, and with wagons and women and children it will take many days.

Wells has put some of us in front of the column and some of us behind. I am in front. I would rather be in front. If something is going to happen, I want to see it coming. A man at the back hears it before he sees it, and that is worse.
