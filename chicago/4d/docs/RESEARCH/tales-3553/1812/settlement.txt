# Tales told by the families of the fort and the settlement, 14 August 1812.

## s12-wom-midwife
roles: woman
title: No midwife at the end of the world
meet: heald_rebekah

There is no midwife here. Not one, between this fort and Fort Wayne. When a woman's time comes she has the doctor, who is a boy, and the other wives, who do their best, and the Potawatomi women across the river, who know more than all of us together.

In May the captain's wife lost her first child, a son born dead. Rebekah Heald is only twenty-two and she rode all the way here from Kentucky on horseback to be married at the edge of the map. She does not speak of the baby. She rides out every afternoon instead, very fast, along the shore. Find her if you like. Do not mention it. Ask her about her horse.

## s12-wom-washing
roles: woman
title: The washing on the river

I do the washing for six of the soldiers and my own family. That is a fire, a kettle, a tub, a paddle, and a whole morning on my knees at the river bank with lye soap that takes the skin off your hands.

The Potawatomi women come down to the same bank with their children, and we have learned to nod to each other, and then to talk with our hands, and then, a few words. One of them showed me how to bleach linen in the sun on the grass. One of them laughed at my bonnet until she cried. This week they did not come down to the river. I washed alone. That is how I knew.

## s12-wom-into-the-fort
roles: woman, child
title: The night we ran into the fort

In April, when word came of the killings at Lee's farm, every family in the settlement left their houses in the dark. Nobody took anything but the children and a blanket. I carried my youngest on my hip and dragged the other by the hand, and the soldiers held the gate open with lanterns until the last of us was in.

We have lived in the fort ever since. Four months, crowded into the officers' quarters and the storerooms with the soldiers' wives. My own house is just over there, a few hundred yards, with the door shut and the garden gone to weeds. I can see it from the walls. I will not see it again after tomorrow.

## s12-wom-what-to-carry
roles: woman
title: What will fit in a wagon

The captain says each family may take what will fit in the wagons, and the wagons are already full of the sick and the children. So I have been deciding all day what to leave.

My mother's pewter. My husband's good coat. The cradle. A Bible with all our names written in the front, back to my grandfather. I put the Bible in my apron pocket in the end, and the cradle stays. The little one is too big for it anyway, though I told myself she was not. A woman packs the past on a day like this. The past is heavier than you think.

## s12-wom-kinzie-boat
roles: woman, settler_woman
title: Mrs. Kinzie's boat
meet: kinzie_eleanor

The Kinzies will not march in the column tomorrow. Mrs. Kinzie and the children will go by water, in their own boat, with the boatmen and the nurse, and meet us down the lake. Mr. Kinzie will walk with the soldiers, because he says he will not be seen to hide.

Mrs. Kinzie was taken herself as a girl, they say, carried off from Pennsylvania by the Senecas and raised among them, and she knows the people in the camps better than any officer here. She has said nothing about tomorrow, only gone on packing in that quiet way of hers. Find her and ask her. She will tell you more with one look than the captain tells in a speech.

## s12-wom-margaret
roles: woman
title: The lieutenant's young wife
meet: helm_margaret

Margaret Helm is seventeen and she married the lieutenant last year, and she is the merriest creature in this whole sad fort. She is Mrs. Kinzie's daughter by her first husband, a British officer, so her late father wore the King's coat and her husband wears the President's.

She can ride, and she can shoot, and she has told us all she is not afraid of anything. I think it is mostly true. She is going to ride with the column tomorrow instead of going in her mother's boat, because she will not leave her husband. Find her, and ask her why. She will laugh and tell you, as if it were the simplest thing in the world.

## s12-wom-corn
roles: woman
title: Corn in hills

When I first came here I planted corn the way my father did in Ohio, in straight rows, and the Potawatomi women laughed at me. They showed me how they do it: a hill of earth, three or four kernels, beans to climb the stalk, and squash below to keep the weeds down. Three sisters, they called it, through the interpreter.

My corn was better that year than it had ever been. I tried to thank the woman who showed me and she shrugged, as if anyone could have known that. I wonder whose corn will feed whose children this winter. I would like to think it will be hers.

## s12-wom-cooking
roles: woman
title: Bread, pork and nothing

You would think a fort would eat well. You would be wrong. Salt pork, hard bread, beans, and when the boats are late, nothing at all. The fresh meat is whatever the hunters bring, and the hunters this summer have stopped bringing it.

Mr. Kinzie's people have the best table in the place, with venison and wild duck and fish from the lake, and maple sugar in a bark box that they put on everything. Last Christmas Mrs. Kinzie sent us a whole wild turkey. I have never forgotten it. That is the thing about a small place. One kindness is enough to keep you warm all year.

## s12-wom-letters
roles: woman
title: A sister in Kentucky

My sister writes from Kentucky that she cannot imagine how I live. No church, no shop, no doctor, no neighbours but soldiers and savages, she says. I wrote back and told her she has the neighbours wrong.

The woman across the river who brings us fish has three children and a husband who is away hunting half the year, just like me. Her baby cut his first tooth the same week as mine. We do not speak each other's language and we both know what a tooth does to a night's sleep. That is not a savage. That is a neighbour. My sister has not written back.

## s12-wom-healds-horse
roles: woman
title: The captain's wife rides

Rebekah Heald came here in 1811 on horseback, all the way from Louisville, a bride of twenty, with her husband and her servant Cicely and Cicely's baby. She rode the whole way. The officers' wives in Detroit, they say, would have gone by boat and been carried ashore.

She is the niece of Captain Wells, the scout, and she rides like him, low on the horse's neck, fast. The officers say she is a fine shot as well. Tomorrow she rides with the column. I have seen her checking her saddle girth twice an hour all afternoon. She is not as calm as she looks.

## s12-wom-simmons
roles: woman
title: A baby born in the fort
meet: simmons_susan_millhouse

Susan Simmons has a baby girl not two months old, and a boy of six, David, who follows the soldiers everywhere and salutes the captain. Her husband is a private. She is from Pennsylvania and she says the winters here are no worse than home, which is how you know she is a liar and a brave one.

She has wrapped the baby in so many shawls for the march that the child looks like a loaf of bread. I asked her if she was afraid. She said a woman with a baby does not have time to be afraid, and that is the truest thing anyone has said to me all week. Go and see her. The baby is very pretty, under all those shawls.

## s12-wom-singing
roles: woman
title: Singing at night

The voyageurs at the Kinzie house sing in the evenings, songs from Canada about a girl and a river and a rose. I do not understand the words, but I hear them across the water every night, and they make the fort feel less alone.

This week they have stopped singing. I asked one of them why and he said it was not a week for songs. Then he laughed and sang one line, very low, and stopped. I do not know what the line meant. I asked the interpreter later and he said it was something about going home.

## s12-set-trader-silver
roles: settler_man
title: Silver for the trade
meet: kinzie_john_elder

Mr. Kinzie was a silversmith before he was a trader, and he still makes the silver himself: brooches, ear-bobs, arm bands, crosses, little round gorgets with holes for the thread. The Potawatomi wear it sewn on their clothes in rows, so that a man in his best coat shines like a lake in the sun.

That is how Kinzie built the trade here. Not with guns or whiskey, though he sells those too, but with silver that people wanted to wear. He knows every chief's name and every chief's wife's name, and the names of their children. If you want to understand Chicago, find him. He is the town, more than the fort is.

## s12-set-lalime
roles: settler_man, soldier, woman
title: The quarrel at the gate
meet: kinzie_john_elder

Two months ago, at sunset on the seventeenth of June, Mr. Kinzie and the government interpreter, Jean Lalime, met just outside the gate as it was being shut. Words were said. Nobody agrees what they were about. Some say the trade, some say an old grudge, some say Lalime had been writing letters to Fort Wayne about Kinzie.

They fought, and when it was over Kinzie had a wound in his neck and Lalime lay dead. A friend took Kinzie off to Milwaukee that night, until it was safe to come back. Lalime is buried in front of the Kinzie house, inside a little picket fence, where Kinzie sees the grave every morning from his porch. I do not know how a man drinks his coffee looking at that.

## s12-set-portage
roles: settler_man, engage
title: The portage in a wet spring
meet: ouilmette_antoine

The Chicago portage is the gate of the whole West. Paddle up the South Branch, cross the mud at Mud Lake, and you are in the Des Plaines and on your way to the Illinois and the Mississippi. In a wet spring you can float a canoe the whole way. In a dry summer you carry everything on your back through mud to your knees.

Antoine Ouilmette has done that carrying for Mr. Kinzie for years. Packs of ninety pounds, two at a time, on a strap across his forehead. He says the mosquitoes on the portage are the size of sparrows. He is exaggerating, but not by as much as you would hope. Find him and ask him to show you his strap.

## s12-set-lee-farm
roles: settler_man
title: Hardscrabble
meet: lee_charles

Lee's farm on the South Branch fed this fort. Wheat, cattle, hogs, the first proper farm on this river. People call it Hardscrabble, because that is what it is: a hard scrabble on wet land.

Since April it has stood empty. The two men who worked it are buried, the cattle are scattered, and the fields have gone to grass and wild flowers. Charles Lee rode out once in July with a guard of soldiers and came back without a word. They say he wants to sell. I do not know who would buy. Ask him yourself. He is a proud man, and it may do him good to talk.

## s12-set-burns-house
roles: settler_man, militia
title: An old soldier's house
meet: burns_thomas

Thomas Burns served his time in this same fort and then stayed, and built a house on the north bank, and married the widow Cooper with all her children. He says a soldier who finishes his five years here has two choices: go home and tell lies about the West, or stay and find out which of the lies are true.

He found out. He grew corn and kept a cow and made friends among the camps. Now he is our sergeant of militia, and his house stands empty across the river with the cow inside it. He says the cow is the only one of us with any sense.

## s12-set-fettle
roles: settler_man
title: The French on the north bank
meet: fettle_louis

There were Frenchmen here before there was a fort, before there was a United States in this country at all. Louis Fettle lives in one of the old log houses on the north bank with his Indian wife. He was here when the soldiers came in 1803 to build the fort, and he watched them cut the logs and laughed at how they did it.

The French came to trade, and they stayed, and they married into the families of the camps, and their children speak three languages before they can walk. When the Americans talk about the frontier, the French smile. To them it is not a frontier. It is home, and it has been for a hundred years.

## s12-set-factory
roles: settler_man, soldier
title: The government store

Across from the fort stands the government's trading house, the factory. The idea was that the government would sell goods to the Potawatomi at a fair price, cheaper than the British traders, and so win their friendship. Mr. Irwin, the factor, ran it for three years, and in all that time he complained that the Indians liked Kinzie's goods better than his.

He left in July and gave the keys to Doctor Van Voorhis. Now the store is being emptied out to the camps, as the captain promised, bolts of cloth, kettles, blankets, paint. The people take them and look at the fort and do not say thank you. You cannot buy a friend in a week with what you would not sell him for three years.

## s12-set-clerk
roles: settler_man, engage
title: The clerk who speaks to everyone
meet: chandonnai_jean_baptiste

Jean Baptiste Chandonnai is Mr. Kinzie's clerk, and he is the only man in Chicago who is welcome in every house and every lodge. He is French and Potawatomi both, and young, and he moves between the trading house and the camps like a fish between rocks.

He keeps the books, and he translates, and he carries messages. This week he has been carrying a great many messages. Everyone wants to know what everyone else is thinking, and he is the only one who can ask. If you find him, he will greet you kindly. He is very good at greeting people kindly and telling them nothing.

## s12-set-peresh
roles: settler_man, engage
title: The young man who brought the war
meet: leclerc_pierre

Pierre Le Clerc lives a few yards from the fort and works for Mr. Kinzie. He is young and quick and French and Indian, and they say it was he who first brought word to Chicago in July that the war had begun.

He will march with the soldiers tomorrow, he says, and fight for them if it comes to that. Some of the officers do not trust him, because he has cousins in the camps. I think a man with cousins on both sides is the one most worth trusting, because he has the most to lose either way. Find Peresh. Ask him what his cousins say.

## s12-eng-lyed-corn
roles: engage
title: A quart of corn and two ounces of tallow

An engagé of Mr. Kinzie gets a quart of lyed corn a day and two ounces of tallow to cook it with, and that is all, and that is enough. You boil the corn in lye until the skins come off, wash it, boil it again, and eat it with the fat. Every day. All winter.

In the fall we go out to the wintering grounds with the trade goods, and in spring we come back with the packs of furs, beaver and muskrat and otter, and then we sing and drink and fight and spend everything, and in the fall we go out again. That is a voyageur's life. It is a good life. It is the only one I know.

## s12-eng-songs
roles: engage
title: Songs for the oars

We sing because a song keeps the paddles together. Forty strokes a minute, all day, from dawn until dark, and a good song can get you another league before the steersman lets you rest. The bowman starts it, the rest answer.

There is one about a girl who drops her ring in the river, and one about a duck, and one about a soldier who comes home to find his sweetheart married. The Americans think they are all happy songs because we sing them so loud. Most of them are sad. That is why we sing them loud.

## s12-eng-wintering
roles: engage
title: A winter on the Kankakee

Last winter I was sent with the goods to a wintering post on the Kankakee River, two of us and a Potawatomi family who traded with us. For four months I saw no other white face. The snow came up to the roof of our hut and the wolves sat in a ring around it at night and sang.

The family took us in when our flour ran out. The old woman gave me a pair of moccasins lined with rabbit fur, and I wore them until they fell to pieces. When I came back to Chicago in the spring I had forgotten how to talk to Americans. I have not entirely remembered.

## s12-eng-boat
roles: engage
title: The family boat
meet: kinzie_engage_1812

Tomorrow I am at the oars of the Kinzie boat. We will take the family and the nurse and the servants down the river and out the mouth, and wait there for the column to pass, and then follow along the shore.

My friend who rows with me was born on the Saint Lawrence and his father was a voyageur before him. He says a boat is the safest place in a storm and the worst place in a battle, because you cannot hide in it and you cannot run. I told him there will be no battle. He looked at me the way you look at a child who has said something sweet and foolish.

## s12-eng-beaver
roles: engage, settler_man
title: Beaver in the old days

The old men say that when the French first came here, the beaver were so thick on the rivers you could walk across their dams. Then the trade began, and every man wanted a beaver hat, from Paris to New York, and every beaver in the country went to make them.

Now we take muskrat mostly, and deer hides, and raccoon. The beaver are far off to the north and west. The trade follows them like a wolf follows a herd. One day there will be none left anywhere, and then what will the traders do? Farm, I suppose. Heaven help them.

## s12-eng-lodging
roles: engage
title: The lodging house for the Frenchmen

Mr. Kinzie keeps a lodging house beside his own for us, his hired men. Bunks, a fire, a table, a fiddle on the wall that nobody can play properly. In the evening we sit there and smoke and argue about which of us has the worst back.

Tonight nobody is arguing. Everybody is mending something, or sharpening something, or packing something. Old Baptiste, who is the best storyteller among us, started a story about a werewolf in Quebec and stopped halfway through, and nobody asked him to finish. That never happens.

## s12-srv-cicely
roles: household_servant
sex: f
title: Cicely's road from Louisville
meet: cicely

The captain's household came up from Louisville last year, on horseback, a month on the road through the woods. Mrs. Heald rode, and the captain rode, and Cicely rode with her baby boy held in front of her. Cicely is enslaved to the Healds. The Ordinance says there shall be no slavery in this country; they brought her anyway, and nobody here has said a word.

She runs the captain's quarters. She cooks, she mends, she knows the captain's moods before he does. She has a son and no other family within a thousand miles. If you speak to her, call her by her name. Ask about her boy. That is the one subject on which she will smile for you.

## s12-srv-jim-axe
roles: household_servant
sex: m
title: The man in the bow
meet: black_jim

Black Jim works for the Kinzies. Whether he is free or held, nobody here has ever written down, and he does not say. He is strong and quiet and he does the heavy work of the house, the wood, the water, the boats.

Tomorrow he will be in the bow of the Kinzie boat with the family. I saw him this evening checking the boat over plank by plank, and he set an axe under the bow seat where he could reach it. When he saw me looking he only nodded. A man who checks his boat that carefully is not expecting a pleasant trip.

## s12-srv-nurse
roles: household_servant
sex: f
title: The children's nurse
meet: laframboise_josette_1812

Josette LaFramboise is nurse to the Kinzie children. She is young, French and Ottawa, from a family of traders up the lake, and she speaks to the children in three languages so that they answer in whichever one they feel like.

She will be in the boat tomorrow with the little ones. She has sewn pockets inside the children's clothes and filled them with dried corn and maple sugar, in case. She did not tell anyone she was doing it. I found out because the baby was chewing on his pocket. Find Josette and ask her about the pockets. She will say she has no idea what you mean.

## s12-srv-unnamed
roles: household_servant
title: Nobody writes down my name

I work in the Kinzie house. I fetch, I carry, I scrub, I mind the fire. When the gentlemen write their letters they will say Mr. Kinzie and his family and two servants. That is me. One of the two servants.

But I am a person all the same, and I have my own fears tonight, and my own prayers. I have friends in the camps and friends in the fort. I know how the master takes his tea and how the mistress hides her worry. If anybody ever asks who was in that boat, I hope somebody remembers there were two of us, and that we were brave.
