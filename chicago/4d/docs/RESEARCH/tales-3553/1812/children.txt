# Tales told by the children of the fort and the settlement, 14 August 1812.

## s12-ch-drum
roles: child
title: I can beat the drum
meet: mcpherson_hugh

Hugh McPherson the drummer let me hold his sticks once, and I beat the call for dinner on an upturned bucket, and the whole company came running and found nothing in the kettle. I was in trouble for a week. It was worth it.

He says a drummer must count in his head all day, even when he is asleep, and that is why drummers are strange. He showed me the long roll, which goes brrrrrrrr, and he says it is only for very serious things. I asked him if he would play it tomorrow. He said he hoped not. Go and find him. If he is in a good mood he will show you the long roll too.

## s12-ch-cranes
roles: child
title: The dancing birds

In the spring the big grey cranes come to the marsh behind the fort and dance. They jump up in the air and flap and bow to each other, like ladies at a ball, and they make a noise like a wooden rattle. My father says they are courting.

I sneaked out to watch them one morning in May, though we were not allowed past the gate after the killings at the farm. A soldier on the wall saw me and shouted, and the cranes flew away, and I got a whipping. But I saw them first. Nobody can take that away.

## s12-ch-kinzie-sugar
roles: child
title: Maple sugar in a bark box
meet: kinzie_john_h_1812

John Kinzie, the big one, has a son called John too, who is nine, and he gets maple sugar whenever he wants it, in a box made of birch bark with a picture scratched on the lid. The Potawatomi women make it in the spring in the woods, boiling the sap for days.

John gave me a piece once. It tastes like the smell of a fire. He says his father is going to take them all away in the boat tomorrow and he is not frightened at all, and then he went very quiet. Find him and ask him for some sugar. He might give you a bit if you say please and do not tell his mother.

## s12-ch-soldiers-drill
roles: child
title: Watching the drill

Every morning the soldiers march up and down the parade and the sergeant shouts at them, and they all turn at once like a fence falling over. I practise in the yard with a stick for a musket. I can do shoulder arms and present arms and right face. I cannot do left face, I always turn the wrong way.

When I am big I am going to be a sergeant, so I can shout. My mother says I already shout enough. Today the soldiers drilled for a long time, longer than ever, and nobody was laughing at all. I stopped practising and just watched.

## s12-ch-lodges
roles: child
title: My friend in the camp

There is a boy in the camp across the river who is the same size as me. We cannot talk to each other but we can race. He always wins. He can swim like an otter and he can shoot a bird with a little bow, and he laughed when I fell in the river.

I gave him a brass button off my old coat and he gave me a feather with a red tip. I have it in my hat. This week my mother says I may not go near the river at all. I saw him on the other bank yesterday and waved, and he waved, and then a man called him away. I hope he keeps the button.

## s12-ch-whiskey-river
roles: child
title: The river smells funny

Last night the soldiers rolled all the barrels down to the river and broke them open. This morning the whole river smells like my father's breath on a holiday. The fish must be very confused.

The grown-ups are cross and quiet. A lady told me the men in the camps were angry because the whiskey was meant for them. I asked why soldiers would pour away something people wanted, and nobody answered me. I think when grown-ups do not answer it means the answer is frightening.

## s12-ch-wagon-ride
roles: child
title: We are going in the wagon

Tomorrow we are all going away to Fort Wayne, and the little children and the sick soldiers get to ride in the wagons. I am not little but my mother says I am to ride anyway. I wanted to walk with the soldiers. She said absolutely not in her not-arguing voice.

I have packed my feather and my knife and my wooden horse. My sister has packed her doll and three acorns. Mama has packed our Bible in her apron. We are going to walk along the lake shore all the way, she says, so I will see the lake all day. I like the lake. It is the biggest thing I know.

## s12-ch-nurse
roles: child
title: Josette's stories
meet: laframboise_josette_1812

Josette who looks after the Kinzie children knows the best stories in the world. She tells one about a giant who walks across the lake in winter, so big that the ice cracks under him and that is why the ice makes noises. And one about a little bird that tricked a bear.

She says the old people only tell the real stories in winter when there is snow on the ground, because the spirits are sleeping then and will not be offended. In summer she tells made-up ones instead. Find her and ask for a summer story. She will pretend to be too busy and then tell you one anyway.

## s12-ch-born-here
roles: child
title: I was born in Chicago

I was born here, in the fort, when the snow was on the ground. My mother says I was the first baby she ever saw come into the world in a blockhouse. I do not remember being born, obviously.

I have never seen a town. I have never seen a church, or a shop, or a brick, or a road that goes anywhere. My father says that in Ohio there are houses side by side for miles and they all have glass windows. I do not believe him. I think he is teasing me again.

## s12-ch-crying-baby
roles: child
title: The fattest baby in the fort
meet: burns_mrs

Mrs. Burns had a baby in April, the night before we all ran into the fort, and they carried her across the river in her bed. Now the baby is the fattest baby in Chicago and it cries louder than the morning gun.

I help mind it sometimes. If you jiggle it and sing Yankee Doodle it stops crying for exactly as long as the song. Then it starts again. Mrs. Burns says I am very useful. Go and see the baby. It is very ugly and very nice.

## s12-ch-isabella
roles: child
sex: f
title: The bravest girl
meet: cooper_isabella

Isabella Cooper is nine and she is not afraid of anything. She climbed up the flagpole halfway once and the sergeant had to get her down with a ladder. She can carry a full bucket from the river without spilling. She fights the boys and wins.

Her mother is Mrs. Burns, and her new father is the sergeant of the militia. Tonight Isabella told me that if anybody tries to hurt her mother tomorrow she will bite them. She means it. Go and find her. Do not get in a fight with her.

## s12-pch-ballgame
roles: potawatomi_child
title: The ball game

When the men play the ball game it goes on all afternoon, a hundred on each side, running across the prairie with their sticks, and the goals are so far apart you cannot see one from the other. The women bet their best things on it, kettles and blankets and silver, and shout louder than the players.

We boys play with little sticks and a ball of deer hair. My uncle made my stick from a hickory sapling, bent and tied while it was green, with a pocket of rawhide. I am the fastest in my lodge. This summer the men have not played. My uncle says there are other games coming. He did not smile when he said it.

## s12-pch-soldier-boy
roles: potawatomi_child
title: The boy in the fort

There is a boy in the fort who is my size, with hair the colour of dry grass. We race on the bank. I always win. He gave me a button, a shining one with a bird on it, and I gave him a feather.

My mother says I must not cross the river now. The grown-ups talk late into the night around the fire, and they send us children away so we cannot hear. I hear anyway. I keep the button in my pouch. I do not know if that is right or wrong now. I keep it anyway.

## s12-pch-sugar
roles: potawatomi_child
title: The sugar camp

In the spring when the snow starts to go, we go to the maple woods, all the families together. The women cut the trees and set bark pans under them, and the sap drips all day, and we children carry it to the big kettles. When it boils down thick my grandmother pours some on the snow and it goes hard and we eat it.

That is the best time of the year. Everybody is happy and tired and sticky. My little brother ate so much he was sick, and my grandmother laughed and said that was how she knew he was a real boy. I would like it to be spring again.

## s12-pch-canoe
roles: potawatomi_child
title: My first canoe

My father is making me a canoe of my own, a little one, from a single log burned and scraped hollow. He lets me scrape the burned part out with a shell. It is very slow. My father says patience is the first thing a canoe teaches you, before it teaches you to paddle.

When it is finished I will paddle it on the river and catch fish. I will paddle it out to the lake if my mother lets me, which she will not. My father says it will be ready before the leaves turn. Then he looked at the fort and said, if we are still here.

## s12-pch-drum
roles: potawatomi_child
title: Their drum and our drum

The soldiers have a drum that goes rat-a-tat, very fast, and it makes them all walk the same way like geese. Our drum is different. It is big and deep and you feel it in your chest, and it makes people want to dance, each their own way.

My sister and I copy the soldiers' drum on a hollow log and march up and down like them, very stiff, and the old men laugh until they cough. Last night a woman told us to stop. She said it was not a good week for laughing at the soldiers. I asked her why. She told me to go to bed.

## s12-pch-traders-silver
roles: potawatomi_child
title: Silver brooches

My mother has a dress with forty silver brooches sewn on it in rows, and when she walks it chimes like rain on a kettle. The trader across the river made them, the one with the big house and the tall trees. My father traded a whole winter of furs for them.

She wears it only on great days. When I am a woman I will have a dress like that, she says, with sixty brooches. I asked her if the trader would still be here then. She took a long time to answer. Then she said someone will always be making silver.

## s12-pm-tippecanoe
roles: potawatomi_man
title: The night at Tippecanoe

Last autumn I went south with the young men to the Prophet's town on the Wabash. The Prophet, Tecumseh's brother, said we must give up the white men's goods and their whiskey and live as our grandfathers did, and that if we were strong the Americans would go back over the mountains.

Before dawn on a cold day in November we attacked the army that General Harrison had brought up to the town. I will not tell you about the fighting. When the sun came up we had lost, and the Americans burned the town. The Prophet's medicine had not stopped their bullets. Some men stopped believing in him that day. Some men only started hating the Americans more. I was one of the second.

## s12-pm-main-poc
roles: potawatomi_man
title: Main Poc's messenger
meet: main_poc_runner

Main Poc is the greatest war leader of our people. He was born without fingers on one hand, and he says the spirits did that so he would know he was marked for them. He has gone over to the British at Amherstburg, and he will not take the Americans' presents, though they have offered him many.

This morning his runner came into the camp with a red belt. Fox Running, they call him. He says the British have won in the north, and that Mackinac is theirs and Detroit will be next, and that every warrior who wants to be a man should come with him. I listened all morning. Go and find him. He will tell you, too. He tells everyone.

## s12-pm-medal
roles: potawatomi_man
title: The man with the medal
meet: black_partridge

Black Partridge wears a medal on his chest that the American general Wayne hung on him at Greenville, seventeen years ago, when the peace was made. He has kept faith with it all this time. He has told his young men to leave the Americans alone, and mostly they have listened.

This week they do not listen. They say the soldiers have broken their word, pouring the whiskey and the powder away. Black Partridge sat by his fire all afternoon and said nothing. Tonight, they say, he will walk into the captain's house and give the medal back. I do not know what that means for tomorrow. Nobody does. Ask him, if you can find him. He will tell you the truth, because he has never told anything else.

## s12-pm-credit
roles: potawatomi_man
title: A winter on credit

The traders give us goods in the fall on credit: powder, lead, blankets, kettles, traps, a gun if you are a good hunter. In spring we pay them back in furs. If the winter is good, you pay and have something left. If the winter is bad, you owe. And the next fall you owe more.

My father owed the old French trader at the river mouth before the Americans came. I owe the trader with the big house now. He is fair, as traders go. He makes silver that my wife loves. But when a man always owes, he is not quite free. The Americans call it trade. My father had another word for it.

## s12-pm-lacrosse
roles: potawatomi_man
title: The great game

Last summer we played the ball game against the people from the St. Joseph River, a hundred men on each side, from noon until the sun touched the trees. The goals were half a mile apart. I broke my stick and two fingers and played on with a borrowed stick.

We won. My wife won three kettles and a blanket betting on us, and she said I could break all my fingers if it meant another blanket. We call the game the little brother of war. It teaches a man to run, to take a blow, to keep his head when everyone around him has lost theirs. This summer nobody plays. The big brother has come instead.

## s12-pm-hunting
roles: potawatomi_man
title: The deer that waited

In the winter our family goes to the hunting grounds on the Kankakee, three lodges, my brothers and their wives and children. Last winter the snow was deep and the deer were few. For twelve days I hunted and brought back nothing.

On the thirteenth day I sat in the snow beside a trail at dawn and prayed and waited, and a doe walked out of the trees and stopped and looked at me, as if she had come on purpose. I thanked her before I shot. We ate. My children lived. Now some men say we should go back to the old ways, as if we had ever left them. I never left. I always thank the deer.

## s12-pm-not-us
roles: potawatomi_man
title: It was not our people at the farm

In April strangers came to the farm on the South Branch and killed the two men there. The soldiers said it was Potawatomi. It was not. They were Winnebago, from the west, men none of us knew. Even the Frenchman at the farm could see it by their paint before they killed him.

But the soldiers do not care whose paint it was. To them we are all one people, the same face. Since April they look at us all the same way. A man who is looked at like an enemy every day for four months begins to wonder why he should not be one.

## s12-pm-greenville
roles: potawatomi_man, potawatomi_elder
title: The treaty at Fort Wayne

Three years ago at Fort Wayne the American governor, Harrison, sat with some of our chiefs and the Miami and the Delaware, and when they got up again the Americans had three million acres along the Wabash. Some of our men had touched the pen. Some had not been asked.

That is when Tecumseh began to speak in every village. He said the land belongs to all the nations together, like the air, and no chief can sell what is not his alone. I believed him. I still believe him. I have seen what happens when you let men sell the earth one piece at a time. In the end there is nowhere left to stand.

## s12-pm-wells
roles: potawatomi_man, miami
title: The man with two peoples
meet: wells_william

William Wells came in yesterday with the Miami. He was born an American, taken by the Miami as a boy, raised as one of them. He fought beside Little Turtle against the Americans and beat them, and then he went back to the Americans and fought beside them against us.

Some men here hate him more than any soldier, because he knows our ways and uses them against us. Some men respect him, because he has never pretended to be what he is not. I do not know which I feel. I know he came to take his niece home. A man who would ride into this camp for his niece is brave, or a fool, or both.

## s12-pm-fishing
roles: potawatomi_man
title: Fishing at the river mouth

At the mouth of the river where it meets the lake, the fish come up in the spring so thick you can spear them from the bank. Sturgeon as long as a man, whitefish, pike with teeth like a dog's. We build weirs of stakes and catch them by the hundred and smoke them on racks over slow fires.

The soldiers watched us the first year, and then they tried to do it themselves with nets, and caught each other more than fish. Now they buy from us, or did. I took a string of fish to the fort this spring and a soldier paid me with tobacco and a joke I did not understand. He laughed so hard I laughed too. That was before.

## s12-pm-horse
roles: potawatomi_man
title: The black horse

I have a black horse I got from the Sauk two summers ago, in trade for a gun and two blankets. He is the fastest horse on this river. The soldiers' officers have tried to buy him three times. The captain's wife looked at him the way some men look at women.

I would not sell him. A horse like that is a man's legs and his pride. This week the young men talk of what they will take from the fort when it is empty, horses and guns and kettles. I already have the best horse. I only want to keep him. That is all most men want, in the end.

## s12-pm-whiskey
roles: potawatomi_man
title: What the whiskey does

The whiskey the traders bring makes men into something else. I have seen a good man, a father, a hunter, drink until he beat his own wife and did not know her face. I have seen men trade a whole winter's furs for a keg and then sit in the snow with nothing.

The Prophet said give it up. Some of us did. So when the soldiers poured theirs into the river last night, half the camp was angry and half the camp was glad. Me? I was glad. But I understand the anger too. A promise is a promise, even when what was promised is poison.

## s12-pm-amherstburg
roles: potawatomi_man
title: Presents from the British

Every summer the British at Amherstburg, down the river from Detroit, give presents to every nation that comes: blankets, kettles, guns, powder, silver, ribbons, vermilion. They call us their children and the king our father, and they feast us for days.

The Americans give presents too, at their treaties, but it is never the same. The Americans want land. The British, so far, want only friends. I am not a fool. I know the British want something too. But when a man must choose between a friend who wants nothing yet and a neighbour who wants everything, he chooses the friend. Ask Fox Running what the British promised. He has just come from them.

## s12-pm-young-old
roles: potawatomi_man
title: Old men and young men

In every lodge this week the old men and the young men are arguing. The old men say, we have traded with these people for nine years, we have eaten their bread and they ours, we have children together. The young men say, they took the land on the Wabash, they burned the Prophet's town, they poured away what they promised us.

Both are right. That is the cruel thing. When everybody is right, it is the young men who decide, because they are the ones with the strength to act. I am neither old nor young. I have been sitting between them all week, with my mouth shut and my heart heavy.

## s12-pm-kinzie-friend
roles: potawatomi_man
title: The trader's friends
meet: topenebe

The trader with the big house has friends among us, real friends, men who would stand between him and a war party. Topenebe of the St. Joseph River is one. His sister is married to a trader too. He has known the Kinzies for years.

Topenebe will not let them be hurt if he can help it. He has been coming and going all week, quietly, talking to the young men and to Kinzie both. If you want to see a man walking a very narrow path, find Topenebe. He is old and his steps are careful, and he has been careful all his life.

## s12-pm-prairie-fire
roles: potawatomi_man
title: Fire on the prairie

In the fall we burn the prairie. We set the fires downwind of the camp and let them run, and the grass goes up in a wall of flame higher than a lodge, and the deer and the elk come running out of it to where the hunters wait. In the spring the burned ground comes up green and sweet, and the animals come back to graze on it.

The soldiers were terrified the first time they saw it. They rang their bell and filled every bucket. Now they know. But the prairie fire is a good teacher. It says: everything burns, and everything grows back, if the roots are deep enough. I wonder how deep our roots are.

## s12-pm-gathering
roles: potawatomi_man
title: So many fires

Every day more people come into the camps. From the Illinois River, from the Kankakee, from the Fox, from Milwaukee, from the Wabash. Five hundred men, they say, and their families. More fires on the prairie at night than stars.

There is not enough food for so many. The women have been out every day digging roots and the men hunting, and still the children are hungry. A hungry camp is an angry camp. The soldiers have food in their storehouse. The soldiers are leaving. You do not need a wise man to tell you what the young men are thinking.

## s12-pw-cornfield
roles: potawatomi_woman
title: My cornfield

The cornfield is mine. My mother's mother cleared it, and her daughters have planted it, and when I die it will be my daughter's. The men hunt and fight and talk at councils. The women own the fields and the lodges, and a man who forgets that will sleep outside.

I plant in the spring when the oak leaves are the size of a mouse's ear. Corn, beans, squash, all together in hills, as the Creator taught us. This year the corn is fine and tall. And the camp is so full of strangers that I have had to set my sister's boys to guard it from hungry people every night. I have never had to do that before.

## s12-pw-mats
roles: potawatomi_woman
title: Weaving the lodge

Our summer lodge is covered with mats I wove myself from cattails and bulrushes, cut in the marsh and dried and sewn with a bone needle and basswood thread. It takes a whole summer to make enough mats for a lodge. When the wind blows, the mats breathe, and the lodge is cool.

In the winter we use bark, elm or birch, which is warmer. A woman who weaves good mats is respected. A woman who weaves bad ones is talked about. I have been talked about once, when I was young and lazy. Never again. You do not want the old women talking about you.

## s12-pw-soldiers-wives
roles: potawatomi_woman
title: The soldiers' wives at the river

There are a few women in the fort, soldiers' wives, who come down to the river to wash. At first they were afraid of us and we laughed at them. They wear so many clothes in summer, layers and layers, that they look like onions.

Now we nod. One of them, with yellow hair, has a baby the same age as mine. We sit on the bank and let the babies look at each other, which babies like more than anything. She gave me a little mirror. I gave her a pair of moccasins for the baby. This week I have not gone down to the river. I do not know if she has.

## s12-pw-archange
roles: potawatomi_woman, settler_woman
title: A woman married across the river
meet: ouilmette_archange

Archange, who lives across the river with her French husband Ouilmette, is one of us. She married him years ago and she keeps her house the way we keep ours, and his way too, both at once. Her children speak French to their father and our language to her and English to the soldiers.

This week her people are on one side of the river and her husband's friends on the other, and she lives in the middle. Her husband says they will stay home tomorrow. Just stay home. Find Archange if you can. She will give you something to eat, whoever you are. That is how she has always been.

## s12-pw-hungry
roles: potawatomi_woman
title: Too many mouths

When people come to a camp, you feed them. That is the law of the Creator and every woman knows it. So when the people came from the Wabash and the Illinois and the Fox, we fed them, until the corn of last year was gone and we started on this year's green ears.

Now I dig for roots in the marsh every day, and my daughters pick berries, and the men bring a duck or two. It is not enough. My youngest cried all night last night. I told her a story to make her sleep. When she slept I sat by the fire and thought about the soldiers' storehouse, full of flour, and their wagons, ready to carry it away.

## s12-pw-ribbon
roles: potawatomi_woman
title: Ribbons on a skirt

I bought silk ribbon from the trader last spring, red and blue and yellow, and I have been sewing it onto my best skirt all summer, cutting it in shapes and turning the edges under, layer on layer, the way my mother taught me. The shapes come from my dreams. Nobody else has this pattern. Nobody is allowed to copy it.

When I wear it to a dance, the women will know it is mine before they see my face. That is what ribbonwork is for. The ribbon comes from the traders, from France, from England, from wherever silk is made. The pattern comes from me.

## s12-pw-husband-tippecanoe
roles: potawatomi_woman
title: He came home from the Wabash

My husband went south last fall to the Prophet's town, with a gun and new moccasins I had made him. He came home in the first snow with his arm in a sling and his eyes different. He did not speak for many days.

He has healed now, mostly. He can hunt again. But at night he talks in his sleep, and once he woke and did not know where he was and reached for his gun. This week the young men are talking war again, and he sits with them. I want to tell him he has done enough. I do not. A woman learns when a man cannot hear her.

## s12-pw-medicine
roles: potawatomi_woman
title: The plants by the marsh

My grandmother taught me the plants: which root eases a fever, which leaf to put on a burn, which bark makes a tea for a cough. The soldiers' young doctor came to watch me gathering once and asked through the interpreter what each one was for. He wrote it all down in a little book.

I asked him why he wanted to know. He said his medicines came from very far away and often did not work. I liked him for saying so. Most of them think nothing that grows here can be any good. That doctor will learn this country, if anyone gives him time.

## s12-pw-sugar
roles: potawatomi_woman
title: Sugar in birch bark

I make the sugar every spring. I tap the maples, catch the sap in bark pans, and boil it in a brass kettle I bought from the trader, all day and all night, until it is thick enough to beat into grains. Then I pack it in birch bark boxes called mokuks, sewn with root and scratched with pictures on the lid.

The trader buys my sugar. His family puts it on everything. His children love it. Last spring his boy came running to me with his hand out, the oldest boy, John. I gave him a piece and he said thank you in our language, very badly. I laughed. He laughed. That was a good day.

## s12-pw-daughter-trader
roles: potawatomi_woman
title: My daughter and the Frenchman

My daughter is going to marry a Frenchman who trades on the Des Plaines. He came to my lodge and sat with my husband and gave presents, as is right, and asked for her properly. He speaks our language well enough. He is not a bad man.

But I am afraid for her. When a woman marries a trader, she becomes the bridge between his people and ours. Everyone walks across her. In a good year, it makes her strong. In a year like this one, a bridge is the first thing that burns.

## s12-pw-grief
roles: potawatomi_woman
title: For a child

Two winters ago I lost my son to the coughing sickness. He was four. For a year I wore my hair loose and my face unpainted, and I kept a bundle with his things in it, his little moccasins and his toy bow, and I fed it at every meal as if he were still with us. At the end of the year we gave a feast and gave his things away and let him go on his road.

I tell you this because the soldiers' wife lost her baby in the spring, the captain's wife, and I saw her walking alone on the beach. I wanted to tell her how we do it, how there is a year for grief and then a feast. I did not have the words. I wish I had.

## s12-pe-du-sable
roles: potawatomi_elder
title: The first trader at the river

When I was a young man the first trader to live at the river mouth was a Black man named Point du Sable. He was French in his speech and from the south, from the islands some said. He married a woman of our people, Kitihawa, and built a big house on the north bank, where the trader Kinzie lives now.

He was a good man. He had cattle and horses and a mill, and he traded fair. When he left, about twelve summers ago, he sold everything and went down the Illinois River and we never saw him again. People forget him now. I do not. He was the first one who came here to live with us instead of to take from us.

## s12-pe-pontiac
roles: potawatomi_elder
title: When I was young

When I was very young, the French were our fathers, and then the English came and the French went away. My father fought for the great war chief Pontiac, who tried to drive the English out. That was fifty summers ago. The English won, and then a few years later they made peace and gave us presents.

Then the Americans fought the English and won, and the English went over the river into Canada, and the Americans came. Every few summers a new father. I am old. I have learned that the fathers change but the land stays the same. It was here before all of them. It will be here after.

## s12-pe-winter-stories
roles: potawatomi_elder
title: Stories wait for the snow

The old stories, the true ones, about how the world was made and how the animals got their ways, we tell only in the winter, when there is snow on the ground. That is how my grandmother taught me. In the summer the spirits are awake and listening, and it is not polite to talk about them behind their backs.

So I will not tell you one of those. But I will tell you a story of my own life, which I may tell in any season. When I was born, the French were here. Then the English. Now the Americans. And every one of them told us, this land is ours now. As if the land had ever listened to anyone.

## s12-pe-first-fort
roles: potawatomi_elder
title: The day the soldiers came

Nine summers ago, a ship came across the lake and soldiers came marching in along the shore, and they walked up to the high ground by the bend of the river and started cutting trees. They cut so many trees. My people stood on the bank and watched them dig and build, and we said to each other, what are they afraid of, that they build such walls?

Their captain, Whistler, was a fair man. He paid for the logs and let us trade at his gate. But I remember thinking that a people who build a wall around themselves in someone else's country must believe they will need it. Now, nine summers later, they are leaving their wall. I wonder if they ever needed it, or if the wall was what made them need it.

## s12-pe-smallpox
roles: potawatomi_elder
title: The spotted sickness

When I was a boy the spotted sickness came through the villages. It came with the traders' goods, people said, in blankets and cloth. My mother died of it, and two of my sisters, and so many others that there were not enough people left to bury them properly.

I lived. I have the marks on my face still, here, and here. The sickness does not care if you are brave, or wise, or kind. It is the one enemy every nation fears the same. When I hear the young men talk of war, I think: there are worse things than soldiers. I have seen them.

## s12-pe-calm
roles: potawatomi_elder
title: What an old man knows

Young men think that courage is running at the enemy. Old men know that courage is sitting still when every part of you wants to run. Tonight I am going to sit by the fire and not speak and not sleep, and listen to the young men talk, and try to keep my face calm.

Tomorrow will come whether I sleep or not. The soldiers will march, and the young men will do what they have decided, and the old men will not be able to stop them. But someone must sit still and remember everything. That is the old man's job. Somebody has to tell the story afterwards.

## s12-mia-little-turtle
roles: miami
title: Little Turtle is gone
meet: wells_william

A month ago our great chief Little Turtle died at Fort Wayne, of the gout and old age, in the house of his son-in-law, William Wells. He was the one who beat two American armies, long ago, and then made peace and kept it. He went to see the President in Philadelphia and they made him a sword.

Wells loved him like a father. When he buried him, the Americans gave him a soldier's funeral, with guns firing. Now Wells has ridden here with us to bring his niece home. He has not spoken of Little Turtle once on the road. That is how I know how much it hurts him.

## s12-mia-escort
roles: miami
title: Thirty Miami on the road

We rode from Fort Wayne, about thirty of us with Captain Wells. We were sent to bring the soldiers and their families safely home along the lake. That is our job. It is a strange job, walking between the soldiers and the Potawatomi, with old friends on both sides.

The Potawatomi here look at us with hard eyes. Some of them are our cousins. Some of them think we have become the Americans' dogs. I do not think about that. I think about the road, and the wagons, and the women and children, and how to get them home. One thing at a time. That is how you get through a hard road.

## s12-mia-two-sides
roles: miami
title: Between two nations

My grandfather fought the Americans with Little Turtle and won. My father made peace with them at Greenville. I ride with an American now, Wells, who is also a Miami, who was once an American, who is now both. If that confuses you, imagine how it feels to be us.

The Miami are tired of war. We have lost too many men, too many villages, too much land. Some of our young men went to Tecumseh. Most stayed home. We want to plant our corn and raise our children. Is that so much to want? Tomorrow we will find out.

## s12-mia-horses
roles: miami
title: The horses are tired

Our horses are tired from the road and there is not enough grass near the fort for them, with so many Potawatomi horses already grazing every blade. I have been taking them out to the prairie at dawn, further each day, with two others to watch.

Out there you can see how many lodges there are now. More every morning. I counted the smoke one dawn and stopped at three hundred. I came back and told Captain Wells. He only nodded. He knew. He has known since the day we arrived.
