# The Government blacksmith shop at the foot of State Street — research dossier

**Record:** `data/structures/blacksmith_shop_state_st.json` · **Scene status:** standing on
1835-07-01 on a continuity argument · **north bank, near the Agency House** ·
`review_required: true`

David McKee's smithy: the Chicago agency's blacksmith shop, kept under a treaty obligation, one
of the ring of small log buildings grouped round Cobweb Castle. Its existence rests on four
independent statements and **nothing at all is known of its shape**.

---

## 1. The statements

Andreas, opening "Early Manufactories of Wood, Iron, Etc." — the town's manufacturing history
starts with a blacksmith:

> "After the second Fort Dearborn was built, **David McKee was appointed Government blacksmith,
> his shop being situated near the Agency House, at the foot of State Street.** … it must be
> acknowledged that David McKee's little shop and scant kit of tools bore but a slight family
> resemblance to the machine shops and rolling mills of to-day."

Andreas, on the 1825 tax list: "**David McKee lived on the North Side, near the agency-house of
Dr. Wolcott**."

Andreas, of the north side about 1830: "The Government agency-house, known as 'Cobweb Castle,'
was left unoccupied by the death of Dr. Wolcott. **In its vicinity were small log buildings
occupied by the blacksmith, Mr. McKee, and Billy Caldwell**, an Indian chief, who was also
interpreter for the agency."

*Wau-Bun*, 1831: "Around the Agency House were grouped **a collection of log buildings, the
residences of the different persons in the employ of Government** … **blacksmith**, striker, and
laborers."

And McKee's own account of his arrival, printed by Andreas: he "found but two houses on the north
side of the river … The third house was built near the agency-house, by Joseph Porthier, and the
fourth by Mr. McKee himself. **All these houses were of logs** — the agency-house being afterward
clapboarded part way up."

## 2. Dates

- The **1821 treaty** obliged the Government to keep a blacksmith at the Chicago agency for the
  Potawatomi. McKee, then in Cincinnati, was engaged for the post by Colonel Kercheval, Indian
  Agent at Detroit.
- By his own recollection he **reached Fort Dearborn in June 1823**. Andreas's own sentence dates
  the appointment only to "after the second Fort Dearborn was built" (1816), which is why the
  record opens the range at **1823** — the first year the shop can have stood under him — rather
  than at 1816.
- June 1827: McKee marries; about that time he is appointed **mail-carrier** to Fort Wayne and
  makes monthly trips through 1827–28. He later farmed on the Des Plaines.
- The 1830–31 agency staffing still lists "**David McKee as blacksmith, and Joseph Porthier as
  striker**".
- After 1831, **nothing**.

The 1833 treaty also provided for blacksmiths, so a Government smith was still owed to the
Potawatomi in 1835. That is the argument for the shop standing on the scene date, and it is an
argument about an **obligation**, not about a building.

## 3. Position

Set 32 m west of this project's `cobweb_castle` placement, on the same riverbank strip, its south
face 16 m north of the drawn 1834 bank. **The direction and the distance are invented**: "near"
is unquantified and no source says which side of the agency house the smithy stood on. Working
uncertainty about 40 m.

**A competing placement is on the record and is not adopted.** One of Andreas's recollections of
the 1820s puts "the blacksmith shop of David McKee and Joseph Porthick (Porthier)" beside a
double log house "nearly opposite the fort" occupied jointly by John Kinzie and Alexander
Wolcott — the Kinzie house cluster, some 400 m further east. Three statements name the agency
house or the foot of State Street against that one, so the outlier is treated as a conflation of
the two north-bank clusters.
