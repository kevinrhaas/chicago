== premise
Rough stone, deep round arches and towers: four Romanesque houses from Eighteenth Street to Twenty-first.

== opening
JAUNT: ROMANESQUE STYLE. It is a winter afternoon in 1904. The elm trees are bare, so you can see the stone. In the 1880s an architect from Boston, Henry Richardson, made heavy stone houses popular again. He used rough blocks, deep round arches like a castle gate, and towers. Chicago loved it. For a few years, rich people here wanted houses like forts.

There are four houses on foot, from 1800 to 2110 Prairie. Look for rough stone, arches that seem to sink into the wall, and towers.

Start with the house that started it all, the one its neighbors hated.

== stop glessner
This is 1800 Prairie, the Glessner House, the first of this style on the street. Its walls are brick covered with big slabs of granite. The slabs are cut deeper around the doors and windows, so the whole wall looks like solid rock. The roof has red tiles. Richardson was a very big man. He said city windows were not for looking out of. So the street side has few windows, and the family rooms open onto a courtyard in back. George Pullman, across the corner, called it "that thing staring me in the face". Across the street and a little south, the style caught on.

== stop ames
This is 1811 Prairie, the Coleman house, built in 1886. Its architects were famous for this style. The front is rough brown sandstone. An old picture shows the style's best trick. There is a deep round arch on bunched columns over a wide stair. Above it, a window box sticks out like a lookout on a castle wall. Notice that the arch is the door. In this style, the way in is like a tunnel into the stone. Walk south past Twentieth Street for two smaller forts.

== stop r2011
This is 2011 Prairie, next to the Mayer house. It has a rough stone front, a round arched doorway, and a round tower on the corner with a pointed cap. A tower on a city house is just for show. It is a castle tower on a town house! Across the street and one block on, the last house fits this style onto one narrow lot.

== stop rees
This is 2110 Prairie, Harriet Rees's narrow house, built in 1888. The same architects who built the Coleman house built this one too. Here they used pale limestone, under a tall pointed roof facing the street. Compare the two houses. They are by the same builders, but one is dark and one is light.

== ending finish
That is the end of the Romanesque walk. Rough stone, round arches and towers were the fashion of the 1880s on this street.

Richardson's stone fort outlasted almost every house around it, and it is still standing. Walk back up to Eighteenth Street and look at it once more in the winter light.
