== premise
An autumn afternoon with the architects who rebuilt Chicago. Meet Jenney, Richardson, Cobb and Frost, Burnham and Root, Beman and Hunt, and end where Burnham met his wife.

== opening
JAUNT: ARCHITECTS. It is an autumn afternoon on Prairie Avenue in 1904. The low sun shines across the stone. Thirty-three years ago, a huge fire burned Chicago. The city built itself again, bold and new. Many of the architects who did it built houses on this street, for rich people who could pay for anything.

There are seven houses on foot, from 1701 to 2100 Prairie. You will meet the man who built the first skyscraper and a giant from Boston. You will meet two pairs of partners. You will meet a man who planned a whole town, and the man who made the base of the Statue of Liberty. And you will see where Daniel Burnham got his start. He married his client's daughter!

Start at 1701, with a plain house by a bold builder.

== stop jenney
This is 1701 Prairie, William Hibbard's house, built in 1869. Mr. Hibbard ran a big hardware business. His architect was William Le Baron Jenney. Jenney was trained as an engineer. Fifteen years later he built a building downtown with a frame made all of metal. People call it the first skyscraper. Many young builders learned the job in his office. Two of them were Louis Sullivan and Daniel Burnham. For Mr. Hibbard he made a plain house with a low roof and brackets under the edges. Later, Louis Sullivan will have a free office here until he dies in 1924. In 1932 the house will be torn down. Walk south to the corner.

== stop richardson
This is 1800 Prairie, the house Henry Richardson designed for John Glessner. Mr. Glessner said his architect weighed 370 pounds. He called him big in mind, big in body and big in heart. Three weeks before he died, Richardson was too sick to leave his bed. He marked on the drawings where the lights would go. Then he told Mr. Glessner his part was finished. He died in April 1886. Building began that June. The builders built it for the price they promised, not a dollar more. The neighbors hated it. Now look next door.

== stop cobb-frost
This is 1808 Prairie, next door. Cobb and Frost designed it for Osborn Keith in 1886. A notice from that year tells its size and price. It has three floors and a granite front, and it cost forty thousand dollars. It went up the same year as the Glessner house next door, granite answering granite. When this house is torn down in 1968, its north wall will be left standing. That wall will keep the Glessner courtyard private. Two doors south is the firm that would build more than anyone.

== stop burnham-root
This is 1812 Prairie, George Wheeler's house, built in 1884 by Burnham and Root. It is a brick row house in the Dutch style. Its front rises into a tall, curvy peak over a stone first floor. It will be torn down in 1968. Within ten years, Burnham would be in charge of building the 1893 World's Fair, called the White City. His motto was "Make no little plans". Cross the street to a castle by a man who once planned a whole town.

== stop beman
This is 1801 Prairie, Solon Beman's castle-like house for William Kimball, the piano maker. It was built in 1890 in a French style, with a steep slate roof and a round tower. When Beman was in his twenties, George Pullman asked him to design a whole town for Pullman's workers, south of the city. No one in the country had planned a town like it before. Beman also added the palm room and glass plant room to Pullman's own house. Walk south to the street's most famous name.

== stop hunt
This is 1905 Prairie, Marshall Field's house, by Richard Morris Hunt of New York. Hunt was the first American to study at a famous art school in Paris. He designed the base of the Statue of Liberty. He also designed Biltmore, the biggest private house in America. For Chicago's richest man, he built a wide brick and stone mansion with a steep mansard roof. Later it will become a flying school. In 1955 it will be torn down. One more stop is at 2100, where a young architect met his wife.

== stop sherman
This is 2100 Prairie, John Sherman's house, built in 1874. It was the first of about a dozen houses Burnham and Root built on this street. Mr. Sherman ran the Union Stock Yards. While the house was going up, his daughter Margaret married the young architect Daniel Burnham, in 1876. Then Mr. Sherman told all his neighbors to hire Burnham. That is how a firm gets a whole street! Burnham and Root built more houses on Prairie Avenue than anyone else. This house will be torn down in 1936.

== ending finish
The walk ends here, at 2100 Prairie. These seven houses are like a sketchbook of the architects who rebuilt Chicago after the fire. In a few years, Burnham will take what he learned here to the whole city. In 1909 he will make the Plan of Chicago. It was the first big plan for how a city in America should grow.

Look for their other houses as you explore. Each one is signed in stone.
