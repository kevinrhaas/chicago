== premise
Walk all the way around Richardson's last house in the summer of 1904. Go from the street that stared at it to the sunny courtyard it hid, in ten stops on foot.

== opening
JAUNT: MEET THE GLESSNER HOUSE. It is a summer morning in 1904, at 1800 Prairie Avenue. The morning sun shines off the lake onto the stone front. John Glessner lives here. He sells farm machines and comes from Ohio. His wife, Frances, has kept a diary since 1879. And here is the house that shocked the street.

There are ten stops on foot, all the way around one house. You will see the corner, the side street, the alley, the front door, the carriage tunnel and the courtyard inside. Most of the story comes from Mr. Glessner. He wrote it down for his children in 1923. You will only see the outside.

Start at the corner, where the neighbors first saw the house and did not like it.

== stop corner
People used to call Prairie Avenue the sunny street for the chosen few. Pullman's mansion stands across the corner. Kimball's French castle is across the street. Then there is this one, with rough stone, small deep windows and steep red roofs. It was the last house Henry Richardson designed. He never saw it built.

== stop north-wall
People talked about this side when the house was new. They noticed the narrow windows, just big enough to light the halls. Richardson said city windows were not for looking out of. People just cover them with shades and curtains anyway. So the house keeps its back to the street.

== stop arch
The great arch is the back door. A small hall through it leads to the kitchen. It is also how music sneaks in. On New Year's Day 1903, eight horn players slipped in by this door. They went up the back stairs, and at four o'clock they began to play in the upstairs hall.

== stop stable
The west side is the stable and carriage house. Its wide carriage door opens onto Eighteenth Street. Inside, the walls are white shiny brick. The furnace room is under the stable. That way the dust, dirt and noise of coal and ashes never come into the house.

== stop gate
Around the corner, these gates in the alley open into the courtyard. The Glessners used them for a trick. On 1 December 1887 the family moved everything in through here before the neighbors even guessed they were moving. That night they lit the new fireplace with fire carried from the old house.

== stop door
This is the front door. It is heavy oak with iron straps and a small window behind an iron grille. Richardson would not let anyone paint the iron. It was treated a special way so it would not rust. The wall looks like solid rock, but it is really brick covered with big granite slabs.

== stop porte
Not every visitor uses the front door. Here a carriage could leave Prairie Avenue and roll right under the house. It went through two big oak doors hung on heavy iron straps, and out into a private courtyard. Follow it through.

== stop court-north
Inside, the house changes. From the street it looked shut tight. Here it opens up. Richardson put big windows on this sunny courtyard side. Mr. Glessner wrote that they let in a flood of sunlight when the sun shines in Chicago. The family rooms look in at this quiet yard, not out at the street.

== stop hall-bow
The round part above the steps is the front hall. Its door to the courtyard has yellow glass in it, so the hall feels sunny even on grey days. The vines here came from the family's old house. One was fifty-five feet long and was planted again in one piece. It grew well.

== stop family
Behind these walls, 1904 is a busy year. Frances Glessner has set up a workbench in the basement to make silver things. Every winter and spring her Monday Reading Class meets in the library. Years ago her son George and his friends ran a telegraph company from his room, with wires to seven houses.

== leg 0
In 1885 Mr. Glessner paid $50,000 for this corner.

== leg 1
A company from Massachusetts built the house for the price they promised, with no extra bills.

== leg 2
Three weeks after the horns, a whole orchestra came to dinner. The players made music on pots, pans and dishes.

== leg 3
Mr. Glessner remembered his architect as a very big man, with a big mind and a big heart.

== leg 4
The house has held parties for more than four hundred guests, all fed from its own kitchen.

== leg 5
The house next door used the same pink brick, so every wall of this courtyard matches.

== ending finish
Richardson never saw any of this. He was very sick in April 1886. Three weeks before he died, he marked on the drawings where the lights would go. He told Mr. Glessner that his part was finished. Digging began that June, and the Glessner children threw the first shovels of dirt. Mr. Glessner believed it was the house Richardson would most have liked to live in himself.
