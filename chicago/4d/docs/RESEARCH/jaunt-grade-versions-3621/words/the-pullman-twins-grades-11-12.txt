== premise
George Pullman bequeathed his twin sons a meager $3,000 annually and a name they could never justify. One is already dead. At dusk in 1904 the survivor still rides recklessly, and his mother continues paying.

== opening
JAUNT: THE LAST PULLMAN TWIN. Dusk on a Monday in November 1904, as the gas lamps are illuminated along Prairie Avenue.

Four stops, from the most imposing residence on the avenue to the home of the single Pullman child who fulfilled her father's expectations. George Pullman manufactured sleeping cars, constructed a company town and accumulated an immense fortune, but he proved incapable of manufacturing respectable sons. His twins, George Jr. and Sanger, were born in 1875 into greater wealth than almost anyone in America, and they devoted their lives to demonstrating publicly what such privilege can inflict upon a boy.

Begin at the Pullman mansion, where the widow resides with her shades partially drawn.

== stop widow
1729 Prairie, the Pullman mansion, the most commanding edifice on the block. Harriett Sanger married George Pullman in 1867, and they had four children: Florence, Harriett, and in 1875 the twins, George Jr. and Walter Sanger, universally called Sanger. Pullman died in 1897, bequeathing his widow this residence, which she still maintains. Upon the twins he pronounced a verdict, codified in his will: "Pullman's will stipulated that the twins were to be given a yearly stipend of only $3,000". Their father evidently understood them. Mrs. Pullman's carriage waits at the entrance; Henry Windish, the German-born coachman, has prepared it, and Miss Robinson, her companion, accompanies her. The mansion will be demolished in 1922. Proceed to the conservatory, where the boys were raised.

== caption widow
Harriett Pullman emerges on her companion's arm toward her waiting carriage while the housekeeper observes from the doorway.

== stop boys
The Pullman conservatory, an enclosure of glass and palms alongside the mansion. The twins were raised and privately educated here, and rumor alleged they were "arranging and promoting pit bull fights". As adolescents they "frequently were seen riding around Chicago in cabs filled with champagne bottles". Their father dispatched them east to a preparatory academy for Harvard, where "Sanger and George were ranked 147 and 148 out of a class of 148 students", occupying the final two positions in perfect fraternal proximity. Moreover the more intellectually capable twin was Sanger, "widely acknowledged as the smarter of the 2", although his intelligence never redeemed him. Cross the avenue to regard the residence where the other twin's narrative concluded.

== caption boys
Samuel Whatter Jr., the groom, leans against the stable gate beside the conservatory, conversing with a stable hand.

== stop george
From the western sidewalk, regard the Pullman mansion in the dusk. The other twin has departed. George Jr. secretly married Lynn Fernald, "his brother's betrothed", appropriating his own twin's fiancée. Frederick Bowers subsequently sued him for $100,000, "eventually settling for $50,000". In November 1901 George succumbed to pneumonia at twenty-six, and the World published the headline "Death of George Pullman a Warning to Rich Fathers". Sanger therefore survives as the last twin, twenty-nine and still financially incorrigible: expelled from the New York Athletic Club for delinquent dues, his obligations so entangled that a creditor once impounded his sister-in-law's luggage for five months. His mother has intervened previously. Walk south to the sister who never required intervention.

== caption george
Two gentlemen promenade past the Pullman mansion and pause to contemplate it.

== stop sister
1912 Prairie, the Lowden residence. Florence, the eldest Pullman child, married Frank Orren Lowden, an attorney, and they reside here with their children. Because Lowden practices law, whenever misfortune visits the family it customarily arrives at his office. Their mother once "paid the unnamed woman $10,000 to leave Sanger and never return". The Lowden children play at their nurse's feet on a tranquil Monday evening, as the twins once did along this identical avenue. The residence will be demolished in 1939. Meanwhile the last twin remains somewhere beyond, riding recklessly toward his conclusion.

== ending ride
The walk concludes here. Sanger Pullman survived nine more months. In August 1905 he "died after being thrown from a horse and carriage after yet another wild ride". His mother had now buried her husband and both sons; her husband rested at Graceland within a "lead-lined mahogany coffin, which was then sealed inside a block of concrete", so that nobody could ever disturb him. Nothing could reach the boys either: neither paternal discipline, nor inherited fortune, nor their mother's $10,000. The World had articulated it in 1901, and it remained accurate: "a Warning to Rich Fathers", and perhaps an indictment of a society that mistook wealth for character.
