== premise
Enter from the open shore south of the fort: commence at the factor's house below Fort Dearborn, pass its stockade, locate a store on South Water Street and conclude at a public house's threshold.

== opening
JAUNT: FROM PRAIRIE TO TOWN. Sunset, and you have traveled up the undeveloped shore from the south, which is precisely how many newcomers first encountered Chicago. When Philo Carpenter disembarked on this shore in July 1832, Joe Ellis conveyed him to the fort "with an ox team", and inland he perceived only "the flat prairie, covered with sedgy grass".

Three years later the prairie is filling with astonishing rapidity: since the federal land office opened, purchasers have "filled the streets and overflowed improvised camps on the adjacent prairies". Ride north past the fort, locate a store on the river street, and reach a public house before nightfall.

== stop factors-house
Commence at the U.S. Factor's House below the stockade, the government trading house. Hubbard, acquainted with it from 1818, recalled a two-story residence of squared logs, and Harrison's 1830 plan identifies it here. Beyond the military Reservation the shore lies undeveloped: Wright's 1834 map designates that ground Fractional Section 15, and nobody had yet purchased it. This is the identical shore where Philo Carpenter disembarked in 1832.

== narrative factors-house
Establish your orientation: the town lies ahead, and the prairie behind.

== stop the-stockade
North to Fort Dearborn's stockade at the river mouth. Juliette Kinzie, who resided within it in 1831, described high pickets and substantial gates to the north and south. Federal troops have garrisoned the post since June 1832, so today it operates as a functioning military installation. Observe it from outside, since the gates are depicted closed.

== narrative the-stockade
The earliest permanent works of the settlement.

== stop the-store
West along the river to South Water and LaSalle and P. F. W. Peck's store, the village's first two-story frame structure, its lumber "brought from Detroit by a boat". His advertisement in the Democrat offered dry goods, hardware, groceries, crockery, boots and shoes, and hats. In May 1833 a newly arrived minister lodged in its unfinished loft, an arrangement characteristic of the improvised settlement.

== narrative the-store
Somewhere to replenish whatever the journey consumed.

== stop the-public-house
Finally, proceed to Lake and Market and the Sauganash, on the eastern bank of the South Branch. Wau-Bun recalled it as white and two stories tall, with bright-blue shutters, and on 10 June 1835 the Democrat reported it repaired and newly furnished. Moses and Kirkland characterize it as "the most attractive hostlery the city possessed". Whether accommodation remains available tonight is entirely uncertain.

== narrative the-public-house
A public house at the journey's conclusion, and a fiddle somewhere within.

== ending in-town
You reach the Sauganash's threshold having entered from the south: from the final dwelling below the fort, along the river street to a store, and onward to a public house. Three years ago this was sedgy grass and an ox team; tonight it is indisputably a town.
