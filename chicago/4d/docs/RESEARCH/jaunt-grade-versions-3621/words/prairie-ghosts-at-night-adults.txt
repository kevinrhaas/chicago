== opening
JAUNT: GHOSTS OF PRAIRIE AVENUE. An autumn night in 1904. The gas lamps are lit and the carriages are home. This is Millionaire's Row, where mansions for George Pullman, Marshall Field, John J. Glessner and Philip Armour stand shoulder to shoulder, and where the richest families in Chicago lock their doors at night against everyone but their ghosts, and their secrets.

Seven stops, on foot, from the corner of Eighteenth Street south to 1919 Prairie. A battlefield where the dead rotted in the sand, a tycoon sealed in concrete against his own workers, an architect who died before his masterpiece, a widow who entertained empty rooms, a sleepless man who walked into the lake, and a gunshot that may have come from a brothel. The ghosts are legends, told by ghost tours and by the Glessner House Museum itself. The deaths behind them are real, and few of them were gentle.

Mind the shadows. Start where the street starts: on the battle ground of 1812.

== stop battle-ground
Prairie at Eighteenth, after dark. Before the mansions there were sand hills here, and on 15 August 1812 the Battle of Fort Dearborn was fought across them. The dead 'lay in the sand, half buried where they were killed, until 1816': four years of bones in the dunes. A Tribune writer swore in 1900 that the Pullman house 'stands about where the massacre took place'. In 1893 Pullman set up a bronze of the Potawatomi chief Black Partridge saving Margaret Helm near this corner. According to legend the fallen still walk here on clear, cold nights. 'The Americans go searching for their families and friends while the Natives continue to patrol their land.' The man who put up that statue is not walking anywhere. His family made sure of it.

== stop pullman
1729 Prairie, the Pullman house. George Pullman made his first fortune lifting Chicago out of its own mud, then built the sleeping car. In 1894 he cut his workers' pay but 'he didn't reduce the rent in Pullman', his company town, and they struck. He 'died of a heart attack' in 1897, and his family feared what would happen to the corpse: 'a lot of high-profile grave robberies lately', and the fear that 'some of the employees weren't quite done getting revenge'. So, the story goes, he went into a 'lead-lined mahogany coffin, which was then sealed inside a block of concrete', with steel rails 'bolted together at right angles' on top. A man buried like a bank vault, to keep his own workers off him. Mrs. Pullman still lives here; the house is razed in 1922.

== stop glessner
1800 Prairie, Glessner House, the granite fortress Pullman meant when he grumbled, 'I do not know what I have ever done to have that thing staring me in the face every time I go out of my door'. Its architect, Henry Hobson Richardson, died on 26 April 1886, five weeks before building began. According to legend he came to see it anyway: cold spots creep into warm rooms, and 'If you're lucky, you might catch the man's spirit standing on that same staircase.' One visitor called it a 'pleasant presence'. The Glessners' daughter Frances grew up to build something less pleasant: eighteen tiny, perfect rooms of real deaths, the Nutshell Studies of Unexplained Death, still used to train detectives. Across the avenue, a house is rattling.

== stop kimball
1801 Prairie, the Kimball house, all steep slate and turret. William W. Kimball started in 1857 'beginning with only four pianos' and built a factory 'churning out around 100 pianos and organs every week'. This winter the music stops: he 'died at his home in Chicago on December 16, 1904'. His widow Evaline lived on here until 1921, and in her last years, it is said, she took to 'entertaining empty rooms she thought were full of visitors': a rich old woman lost in her own house among her Old Masters. Today the paintings hang in the Art Institute, and the big north windows shake when there is no wind. The house museum itself asks, 'Could it be that the spirit of Evaline Kimball has returned to the house'.

== stop keith
1900 Prairie, Elbridge G. Keith's house, its mansard roof black against the sky. A few doors south, at 1906, lives the widow of his brother Edson, the merchant. Edson Keith had been sleepless and ill for at least ten years. Early on Monday 21 September 1896 he slipped out of his house, walked to the lake at the foot of 16th Street, and walked in. The papers said he 'committed suicide Monday morning by drowning himself in Lake Michigan'. All that money, and no cure for ten years without sleep. Ghost hunters say 'his ghost has been seen at his brother's house, where he sometimes appears in the windows.' Look up at the dark panes as you pass. Across the avenue, the richest man in Chicago is asleep.

== stop field
1905 Prairie, Marshall Field's house. 'Give the lady what she wants', said the merchant, and the ladies made him 'the wealthiest and most powerful businessman in Chicago', worth $125 million by 1905. Tonight it all looks safe. It is not. In thirteen months his son, next door, will be shot. Six weeks after that, Field plays golf on New Year's Day 1906 with Robert Todd Lincoln, catches pneumonia and dies on 16 January. Father and son, both dead in one winter, and $125 million could not buy either of them a single extra day. The house lasts until 1955, then it is razed too. Walk on to the son's house, the one with the footsteps.

== stop field-jr
1919 Prairie, Marshall Field Jr.'s house. On Wednesday 22 November 1905 a shot goes off. He dies five days later at Mercy Hospital, saying 'I shot myself with that revolver – accidentally', and the coroner agrees. Chicago does not. Reporters are tipped that he spent the evening at the Everleigh Club, a brothel on Dearborn Street; 'The most popular story is that he was in fact shot at the Everleigh Club' and carried home to look like an accident. Others say he meant to do it. Nobody ever proved anything. Later owners heard footsteps through empty halls and 'strange cries in the dead of night', and one family reportedly called in an exorcist for a spirit that 'sours the air around it'. Some houses keep their secrets. This one keeps its ghost.

== ending finish
End of the walk at 1919 Prairie. One more ghost, for the road. Years later the Clarke House, 'purported to be the city's oldest standing house' and 'twice relocated', will come back to this neighbourhood, and in the park beside it, they say, a woman in a settler's dress drifts among the bushes after dark, while carriage horses clip past that 'always seem to have their bits, but never their harnesses'.

The living moved on. The dead stayed. Sleep well, Prairie Avenue.
