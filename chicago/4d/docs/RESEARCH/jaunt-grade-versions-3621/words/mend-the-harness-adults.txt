== opening
JAUNT: MEND THE HARNESS. Sunset in the wagon yard behind the Western Hotel, the air thick with dung and flies, and you leave on the long road west at first light. The teams in this yard outnumber the guests. While you harness up for a trial turn, the near trace shows a crack by the buckle. Out on the prairie a broken trace is not an inconvenience. It is a team loose, a wagon stuck, and days from help.

Two trades mend an outfit: the saddler sees to leather and the smith to iron. Both came to Chicago in 1833, the year immigration 'assumed proportions surpassing all expectation', and both keep shop at the same crossing. Find the trouble, get it fixed before dark, and decide over supper at the Green Tree whether the outfit is fit for the road, or whether your pride is.

== stop stable
Behind W. H. Stow's Western Hotel at Randolph and Canal stands a large stable and the yard the wagon trains drive into, with entrances from both streets. Stow's account calls it one of the most important parts of the house, for the teams were as numerous as the guests: a hotel that earns from horses as much as from men. Under the county's 1831 tariff a horse kept all night cost 50 cents and a feed 25, four times a man's bed: a team is not cheap to stand idle, and every day spent mending is money spent.

== stop saddler
Up Canal to Lake Street. Goss & Cobb opened a saddle and harness shop at this corner in 1833, promising that repairing would be immediately attended to when brought to their shop. Silas B. Cobb was one of that year's arrivals, in the flood of newcomers that year. The partners parted in February 1835, and Cobb's June card says he will carry on alone. Tonight, as every night, there are traces waiting.

== stop smithy
Leather is the saddler's; iron is the smith's. On the same crossing Asahel Pierce, another arrival of 1833, began a blacksmith shop in October that year, and Andreas records him doing the ironing for the first stage line between Chicago and St Louis in January 1834, in the dead of winter. A man who irons the St Louis stage can make one worn hook sound. Ask yourself honestly whether you looked hard enough to find it.

== stop tavern
East along Lake to West Water and the Green Tree, which James Kinzie built in 1833 as the first house in the place put up for hotel purposes, 'so called from an oak tree standing close by'. Old settlers recalled Chester Ingersoll keeping it from 1834 to 1837. It will outlast nearly everything in sight: moved in 1880, it still stood in 1895, 'the oldest memento of early taverns'. Most of the men who ate supper here and took the road west at dawn left no memento at all.

== ending short-run
A short run near town tomorrow, then, and the long road another day. Whatever was left unchecked or only bound with twine will be tested close to help. Careful is not quite ready, but it is not reckless either, and the prairie does not forgive reckless.

== ending sound-tack
Leather to the saddler and iron to the smith: the trace restitched, the whiffletree hook made sound, and every strap and buckle looked over before dark. At first light the outfit takes the long road, fit for whatever the prairie throws at it, and you are poorer but safer for the evening.

== ending hook-left
The trace is sound again, but you found the worn hook and drove past the forge with it, to save a few coins or an hour. The leather will hold on the long road; whether the iron will is a question for the first hard pull, a long way from Asahel Pierce and anyone who could help.

== ending leather-only
The trace is restitched and the outfit takes the long road. Nobody looked at the iron, so nobody knows the whiffletree hook is worn thin. Sound leather, untried iron, and a long way to walk if it parts. Next time, look over everything.
