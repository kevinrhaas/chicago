== premise
Three blocks west of the mansions, two sisters from Virginia run the most famous club in Chicago, with gold spittoons and a $15,000 piano. Prairie Avenue pretends not to know the way.

== opening
JAUNT: THE SISTERS OF THE LEVEE. It is dusk on a Monday in November 1904, at Eighteenth Street and Prairie Avenue.

There are four stops. You walk west from the street of rich families toward the Levee, a rough part of town, and end at a church on its edge. Prairie Avenue is only three blocks from the most famous address in Chicago, and it is not a family home. In 1900 two sisters who called themselves Everleigh opened a club on Dearborn Street. Some of the richest men in the world find their way there.

Start at the corner. Watch which way the cabs turn.

== stop corner
This is Eighteenth and Prairie, as the street lamps come on. Eighteenth Street is a border. To the north and east are the mansions. To the south and west, a few blocks away, is the Levee. It runs from Eighteenth Street to Twenty-second Street. It is full of clubs and saloons that break the law. The ladies of Prairie Avenue never talk about it. But for a while, one of the most famous of those houses stood right on Prairie Avenue. The two men hurrying around this corner are not going home. Follow them west.

== caption corner
Two men in evening coats come around the corner and walk fast to the west along Eighteenth Street.

== stop sisters
This is Indiana Avenue, one block west. Three more blocks west is Dearborn Street, and the Everleigh Club. It is run by two sisters, Ada and Minna. They say they are from Kentucky, but that is a made-up story. They were really born in Virginia, with the last name Simms. They ran a house in another city first, then came to Chicago. They took a new last name from their grandmother, who signed her letters "Everly yours". The club opened on 1 February 1900. On the first night they turned many visitors away. The sisters knew that saying no makes people want something more.

== caption sisters
A man comes down the steps of a house on Indiana Avenue. He checks his watch and turns west toward the Levee.

== stop rooms
Walk on south along Indiana. Inside the Everleigh Club are fifty rooms in two mansions. There is a Japanese room, a room of mirrors, and a silver parlor. There is a piano covered in gold that cost $15,000, and there are gold spittoons. One bottle of fancy wine costs $12, when a whole dinner could cost fifty cents. Just getting in the door costs $50. Ada keeps the money records. Minna greets the guests. In 1902 a German prince even came to visit. The club has up to 25 cooks and maids.

== caption rooms
Two maids, done with work, come up the side path to Indiana Avenue and hurry north, talking.

== stop church
This is First Presbyterian Church, at Indiana and Twenty-first Street. It stands at the edge of the Levee, close enough to hear the noise on a summer night. People say famous actors and writers visit the club. Some even say young Marshall Field Jr., who lives a few blocks away, is one of them. Later, a rival told a mean, false story about the sisters, and the rumor never went away. The minister's door is lit. So is the club's. Tonight, like every night, the Levee is busier.

== caption church
A family comes out of the church hall into the evening while a cab goes by the other way.

== leg 0
West along Eighteenth Street, past the last of the mansions, to Indiana Avenue.

== leg 1
South along Indiana Avenue, with the noise of the Levee two blocks to the west.

== leg 2
South to Twenty-first Street and the church at the edge of the Levee.

== ending close
The walk ends here. The Everleigh Club lasted eleven years. On 24 October 1911 the mayor ordered it closed. Minna said she would close up shop and walk out with a smile on her face. The building was torn down in 1933. Prairie Avenue never once said it knew the way to Dearborn Street. It only ever knew the way back.
