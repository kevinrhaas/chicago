== premise
Every Monday from October to May the women of Chicago gather in the granite fortress at Eighteenth and Prairie to sew and listen. This Monday, the mansion across the street is waiting for a death.

== opening
JAUNT: MONDAY AT THE FORTRESS. Monday morning, 21 November 1904, at the intersection of Eighteenth Street and Prairie Avenue.

Five stops in and around the Glessner House. Today is Monday, which means the Reading Class: the wives of University of Chicago professors and the ladies of the South Side, assembling in the library at the invitation of Frances Glessner. Within these walls everything operates with clockwork precision, and the servants are rarely visible. Across Eighteenth Street, in the mansion of the piano manufacturer, a different clock is running down.

Begin at the corner, where the neighbors first confronted what Richardson had designed.

== stop fortress
The corner of Eighteenth and Prairie. The house is granite, low and severe, with street-side windows barely wide enough to illuminate a corridor. Its architect, H. H. Richardson, died in April 1886, before construction even began in June; the house rose between June 1886 and December 1887. Not everyone admired it. George Pullman, diagonally across the way, complained: "I do not know what I have ever done to have that thing staring me in the face every time I go out of my door". Pullman has been dead seven years, and the house continues to stare. What passers-by never observe is the opposite side: generous windows opening onto a sunny courtyard, and a household that functions like a machine. The ladies are arriving on foot.

== stop hostess
The front door. In 1893 William Rainey Harper, president of the newly founded University of Chicago, asked Frances Glessner how the professors' wives might become acquainted with the women of the city. Her solution was the Monday Morning Reading Class, which meets in the library for two hours every Monday from October to May. During the first hour the ladies sew while a professional reader reads aloud; the second hour offers lighter reading or a lecture. Nobody joins merely by asking: "Membership was by invitation only", and for a professor's wife newly arrived in Chicago, an invitation is the door into society. Mrs. Glessner greets her guests personally, while Christine Nelson, the parlor maid, waits to collect their wraps. Then the door closes, and the house performs its intended function.

== caption hostess
Frances Glessner welcomes her guests at the door while Christine Nelson stands behind her.

== stop hall
Inside, the house turns its back on the street and opens toward the courtyard. The courtyard door contains yellow glass beneath chipped white glass, giving the hall "a feeling of sunlight even in cloudy weather", and today is overcast. The floor plan resembles a puzzle box: every principal room has two or more entrances, and the passages are "so planned that servants rarely are in evidence". So while needles move through fabric in the library, an entire second household circulates around them unseen: the cook, the maids, the man who tends the fires. Frances Glessner wanted a house in which the labor never showed, and she achieved it. The ladies sew, the reader reads, and the staff remain hidden in the court, where only you can observe them.

== caption hall
Beneath the hall bay, the household works out of sight: a maid crosses the court with a tray while the cook waits at the service door.

== stop bench
The service stair, descending to the basement. Below is the newest room in the house: the workbench Frances Glessner installed this year, where she shapes silver with hammer and torch and stamps each piece with "a G encircling a honeybee". Upstairs she is the most correct hostess in Chicago; downstairs she is an apprentice craftsman taking lessons from a Hull House jeweler. Both identities are genuine, and she has run this household, and that class, by the clock for eleven years. But across Eighteenth Street a neighbor has suffered a prolonged illness, and the doctor is calling again this morning. Look out through the gates toward the corner mansion of William W. Kimball, the piano maker.

== caption bench
August Billings hauls coal up from the cellar and pauses on the stair to look toward the street.

== stop kimball
1801 Prairie, across Eighteenth Street: the Kimball house. William Wallace Kimball arrived in Chicago in 1857 and traded his Iowa land for four pianos, which he sold from a second-floor office. Since 1881 his factory has produced roughly a hundred pianos and organs every week, and his name appears on instruments throughout America. He and his wife Evaline have no children, and he is dying. He will die "at home after a lingering illness" on 16 December, and his funeral will be held in this house. Watch the doctor ascending the steps, and the steward who admits him. Among the honorary pallbearers will be "Marshall Field, Robert T. Lincoln" and, from the house of the Reading Class, "J. J. Glessner".

== ending clock
The walk ends here. W. W. Kimball died on 16 December 1904, and John Glessner crossed Eighteenth Street to stand among his honorary pallbearers. The Reading Class continued, meeting every Monday from October to May "until 1930". When Frances Glessner died, her husband attempted to capture who she had been: "She had a clean and wholesome and orderly mind, a heart overflowing with love for family and friends and for all in any need". That sentence describes the house as well: clean, wholesome, orderly, and filled with people you never see.
