== premise
George Pullman left his twin sons only $3,000 a year, and a famous name they could not live up to. One twin is dead. In 1904 the other is still riding wild, and his mother is still paying.

== opening
JAUNT: THE LAST PULLMAN TWIN. It is dusk on a Monday in November 1904. The street lamps are coming on along Prairie Avenue.

There are four stops. You start at the biggest house on the street. You end at the house of the one Pullman child who turned out the way her father wanted. George Pullman built railroad sleeping cars, a whole town, and a huge fortune. But he could not make his sons turn out well. His twins, George Jr. and Sanger, were born in 1875 with more money than almost anyone in America. They spent their lives showing the city what too much money can do to a boy.

Start at the Pullman house, where the widow lives.

== stop widow
This is 1729 Prairie, the Pullman house, the biggest house on the block. Harriett Sanger married George Pullman in 1867. They had four children: two girls, Florence and Harriett, and then the twins, George Jr. and Sanger. George Pullman died in 1897. He left his wife this house. He left the twins just $3,000 a year each. Their father knew them well. Look: Mrs. Pullman's carriage is at the door. Henry Windish, the coachman, has it ready. Her friend Miss Robinson is with her. The house will be torn down in 1922. Go around to the glass room, where the boys grew up.

== caption widow
Harriett Pullman comes out on her friend's arm to her waiting carriage. The housekeeper watches from the door.

== stop boys
This is the Pullman conservatory, a glass room full of palm trees at the side of the house. The twins grew up here. They had lessons at home. People said they set up dog fights. As teenagers they rode around Chicago in cabs full of bottles of fancy wine. Their father sent them to a school to get ready for college. Out of 148 students, the twins came in last and next to last. Side by side, as twins should be! Sanger was the smarter one, people said, but it never helped him. Cross the street to look back at the house.

== caption boys
Samuel Whatter Jr., the groom, leans on the stable gate by the glass room and talks with a stable hand.

== stop george
From the other side of the street, look back at the Pullman house in the evening light. The other twin is gone. George Jr. secretly married the woman his own twin brother was going to marry. Another man took him to court for $100,000, and George paid $50,000 to settle it. Then in November 1901 George got very sick and died. He was 26. A newspaper said his death was a warning to rich fathers. So Sanger is the last twin. He is 29 now, and he is still bad with money. He owes people all over. His mother has paid his debts before. Walk south to the sister who never caused trouble.

== caption george
Two men stroll past the Pullman house and turn to look at it.

== stop sister
This is 1912 Prairie, the Lowden house. Florence, the oldest Pullman child, married Frank Lowden, a lawyer. They live here with their children. When trouble comes to the family, it often comes to Mr. Lowden's office. Their mother once paid a woman $10,000 to leave Sanger and never come back. The Lowden children play at their nurse's feet on this quiet Monday evening, the way the twins once played on this same street. This house will be torn down in 1939. And the last twin is still out there, riding wild.

== ending ride
The walk ends here. Sanger Pullman lived nine more months. In August 1905 he died after he was thrown from his horse and carriage on another wild ride. His mother had now lost her husband and both her sons. All the money in the world could not save the boys. Not their father, not his fortune, not their mother's $10,000. The newspaper had said it in 1901, and it was still true: it was a warning to rich fathers.
