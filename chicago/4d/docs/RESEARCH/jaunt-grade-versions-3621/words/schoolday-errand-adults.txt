== opening
JAUNT: A SCHOOLDAY ERRAND. Your household has one question: where could a child learn? In this town, getting to school is half the lesson, and a dangerous half. The schoolmaster Granville Sproat remembered streets of mud 'half a leg deep, up to the hubs of the carts and wagons', and carrying the smaller scholars on his back, 'not daring to trust them on the slippery plank'. Once he fell into the mire with a child in his arms and lost 'both overshoes'.

Ride past the places Chicago's first classes met, on both sides of the river, and end where the Democrat is printed, with a notice dated today about building a real school house, in a town that has already sold the land meant to pay for one.

== stop michigan-street
On Michigan Street, between Cass and Rush, Andreas has John Watkins, hired in 1833, later teaching in a house; Moses and Kirkland make it Richard J. Hamilton's home, where Hamilton lived for nineteen years. Watkins had started in 1832, a subscription school for parents who could pay, 'in a log house about twelve feet square', with only twelve pupils in his first quarter. Chicago's schools began very small, in rooms borrowed from men of property.

== stop north-bank
Down to the bank east of Clark. Andreas says Hamilton and Owen built a school-house here in 1833, the first built for the purpose, though Watkins himself did not remember it, and that by 1835 it was the North Side's public school. South of the river, Sproat's school met upstairs in the Baptist church; he called it 'a very pleasant one, the pupils obedient and attentive'. Schoolmasters tend to remember their pupils kindly.

== stop the-log-house
Over the river to State Street, by the old military reservation. Andreas puts Eliza Chappel's infant school of 1833–34 in a log house here, divided into school-room and lodging quarters: a young woman teaching by day and sleeping on the other side of the wall by night. Other accounts differ. In 1834 it moved into the Presbyterian church.

== stop the-notice
On to South Water and Clark, where the Democrat was printed from 1833. Today's number repeats: School District No. 4 meets in the Presbyterian Church on 7 July at six, to consider building a school house. Money is tight because the school section, the square mile meant to pay for the town's schools, was sold in 1833 for $38,865. 'The sale was a great mistake': the blocks would have brought 'a hundred times more had they been held twenty years'. The children's inheritance went cheap, and the men who bought it did very well.

== ending two-answers
The north-bank school written down, and the District No. 4 meeting copied out for 7 July. Two answers to one household question: a school already teaching, and one still to be argued over by men who would rather spend their money on lots.

== ending the-meeting
No school written down yet, but the notice is copied out: 7 July, six in the evening, the Presbyterian Church. Whether a school house is built is for the meeting to say, and for whoever is willing to pay. You mean to be there.

== ending a-long-way
The north-bank school is written down, but the river lies between it and the stores, and that is a long way each morning for a child of five, over planks and through mud. Somebody may have to carry the little one, as Sproat did, and hope not to fall.

== ending a-scholars-walk
The north-bank school is written down: a walk and a river crossing each morning, no great thing for a child of ten who is not needed for work at home. Whether there is room for one more scholar is a question for the schoolmaster.

== ending still-asking
Nothing written down and nothing copied: the question goes home as it came. The schools you passed are real, the meeting is next week, and in a town busy getting rich, the asking goes on.
