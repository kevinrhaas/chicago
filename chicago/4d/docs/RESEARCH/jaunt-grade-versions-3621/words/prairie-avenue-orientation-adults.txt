== opening
JAUNT: MEET THE GLESSNER HOUSE. A summer morning, 1904, at 1800 Prairie Avenue. The morning sun off the lake is on the granite front, and the smoke of the city is already in the air. Behind it live John J. Glessner, a farm-machinery man from Ohio who became a vice president of International Harvester, his wife Frances, who has kept a journal since 1879, and the house that shocked the street: a rich man's home built like a prison wall on the outside, and a sunny private world within.

Ten stops on foot, all the way round one house: the corner, the Eighteenth Street side, the alley, the front door, the carriage passage and the courtyard inside. The story is mostly the owner's own, from the account he wrote for his children in 1923, and owners tell their houses kindly. Exteriors only.

Let's start at the corner, where the neighbors first saw it and did not like what they saw.

== stop corner
An old Chicago saying called Prairie Avenue 'the sunny street that holds the sifted few': sifted meaning everyone else was kept out. Pullman's mansion stands diagonally across the corner, Kimball's French chateau across the avenue. Then this: rough granite, small deep windows, steep red roofs. Richardson's last house. He died before it was built.

== stop north-wall
This is the side people talked about. Passers-by remarked on the narrow windows, 'just enough to light the narrow corridors'. Richardson held that the windows of a city house were 'not to look out of': people only shroud them in curtains anyway. The house turns its back on the street, and on the city.

== stop arch
The great arch is the back door, the servants' way: a small corridor through it leads to the kitchen. It is also how the orchestra sneaks in. On New Year's Day 1903 a double quartet of horns slipped in by the Eighteenth Street door and up the back stairs, and at four o'clock began to play.

== stop stable
The west wing is the stable and carriage house, with its wide carriage doorway onto Eighteenth Street. Inside, white glazed brick. The furnace room is underneath, so the dust, dirt and noise of coal and ashes never come into the house: the dirty work is done under the horses, out of the family's sight.

== stop gate
These gates in the alley open into the courtyard, and they let the Glessners play a trick. On 1 December 1887 the family moved everything in through here, 'before our neighbors suspected even that we were about to move'. Even moving is done in secret. That night they lit the new hearth with fire carried from the old.

== stop door
The front door: heavy oak strapped with iron, a square window behind an iron grille, more fortress than welcome. The ironwork was Bower-Barffed against rust. The wall looks like solid rock, but it is brick faced with granite slabs, with deep stones at every opening to keep up the illusion. Even a fortress is partly show.

== stop porte
Not every visitor uses the front door. Here a carriage could leave Prairie Avenue and roll right under the house, through two paneled oak doors hung on heavy iron straps, and out into a private courtyard, without its passengers ever setting foot on the public street. Follow it through.

== stop court-north
Inside, the house changes its mind. From the street it looked shut tight; here it opens up. Richardson put generous windows on this courtyard side, letting in, Glessner wrote, 'a flood of sunlight when the sun shines in Chicago'. The family looks in, never out.

== stop hall-bow
The rounded bay above the terrace steps is the entrance hall. Its door to this courtyard is glazed with yellow glass under chipped white glass, so the hall feels sunny even on grey days: bought sunshine. The vines here came from the family's old house; one was fifty-five feet long and replanted whole.

== stop family
Behind these walls, 1904 is a busy year. Frances Glessner has set up a silversmith's bench in the basement. Every winter and spring her Monday Morning Reading Class meets in the library. Years ago her son George's friends ran a telegraph company from his school room, wired to seven rich houses, all centering on this one.

== ending finish
Richardson never saw any of this. About three weeks before he died in April 1886, too ill to leave his bedroom, he marked on the drawings where the lights would go and said to Glessner: 'There, Mr. Glessner if I were to live five years longer, that is the last thing I would do on your house, my part is finished.' He had three weeks. Digging began that June, and George and Frances threw the first soil. Glessner was convinced it was the house Richardson would most have liked to live in himself. The dying man designed the one house he would never enter.
