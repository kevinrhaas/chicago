== premise
At daybreak a widow rises at 1906 Prairie, eight years after her husband, sleepless and ill for a decade, walked out of this door at six in the morning and never returned. Retrace his final walk, from his brother's door to the lake.

== opening
JAUNT: THE WALK TO THE LAKE. Daybreak on a Monday in November 1904, at the southern end of Prairie Avenue.

Four stops, northward along Prairie toward the lake. The Keiths are, by every outward measure, the steadiest family on the avenue: three brothers, three houses, one formidable wholesale firm. Yet on a Monday morning eight years ago, at almost exactly this hour, the gentlest of them rose from another sleepless night, put on his hat and coat, and stepped out of his front door. He walked, alone, toward the water.

Begin at his door, where his widow still lives.

== stop door
1906 Prairie, at daybreak. The widow in this house is Mrs. Edson Keith. On Monday, 21 September 1896, her husband, a merchant, "disappeared from his home at 1906 Prairie avenue about 6 o'clock in the morning". He was sixty-three and had arrived in Chicago in 1854. For at least a decade he had scarcely slept, and illness had steadily worn him down, so that on that final night he paced the hallways once more, and "his butler found him in a state of deliriousness at 2 AM". By six he was gone, and the census of 1900 still lists her as a widow. Hilda Johnson, the ladies' maid, is the first awake; Paul Rasmussen, the Danish-born coachman who keeps his own household in the rear building, crosses the yard. Mrs. Keith comes to the door to judge the weather. Walk north to the brother's house next door.

== caption door
Mrs. Edson Keith opens her door at first light and gazes up the street toward the lake, her maid standing behind her.

== stop brother
1900 Prairie, the residence of his brother Elbridge, built in 1870 to designs by the architect John W. Roberts, in the Second Empire style that once dominated this street; it is now that style's last survivor here. Elbridge and his wife saw the catastrophe approaching. A few weeks before that Monday they spoke with Edson's wife, who confided, "I … suspect that he was losing control of his mind through long suffering". His personal secretary later insisted he had never once heard Edson say anything nasty or unkind about anyone. That was Edson Keith's reputation: the gentle brother, the one who never raised his voice. Elbridge is up early this morning as well, and he still passes his brother's door on his way out. Walk north up Prairie, retracing Edson's route.

== caption brother
Elbridge Keith descends his front steps in the half light while his wife watches him go from the doorway.

== stop firm
1808 Prairie, the Osborn R. Keith house. Osborn Keith and Albert Faxon founded the family firm on Lake Street in 1858, and by the mid-1880s Edson Keith and Company was selling $4.5 million in goods annually, employing over two hundred people in Chicago and five hundred more in a Milwaukee factory. Three Keith houses stood on one street, with a considerable fortune behind them, although by 1904 the Blue Book lists Stanley Field at this address, yet the neighborhood still thinks of it as a Keith house. Edson passed it in the darkness that Monday, heading north, toward the water. For a man in his exhausted condition it was a long and deliberate walk. The house will be demolished in 1968, except for its north wall, preserved to screen the Glessner courtyard. Continue north to Sixteenth Street.

== caption firm
A milkman's boy hurries along the morning round past 1808, the only other figure on the street.

== stop lake
Sixteenth Street, the northern end of the avenue. East of here, beyond the railroad tracks, lies the lake. The street stops here, but Edson Keith did not; on the beach that Monday morning someone discovered a hat and a coat, and fishermen described what they had witnessed. The newspapers could not even agree on the location: one placed it at the foot of Sixteenth Street, another at the foot of Thirteenth. The papers reported that he had taken his own life by drowning in Lake Michigan, and the search continued all day Monday, and the household staff understood what had happened only on Tuesday, when they read it in the newspaper. Stand here and look east into the first light. The lake is grey, flat and immense, indifferent to the grief of the avenue behind you.

== caption lake
A solitary gentleman in hat and coat walks east along Sixteenth Street toward the lake and never looks back.

== leg 0
North two doors, following his route, to his brother Elbridge's house.

== leg 1
North up Prairie Avenue in the half light, past the houses the family firm built.

== leg 2
North the entire length of the street to Sixteenth, the long walk toward the lake.

== ending dragged
The walk ends here. The news reached the family "with paralyzing suddenness", and a dispatch reported that "the lake is now being dragged for the recovery of the remains". Edson Keith had signed his will the previous year, on 13 March 1895, leaving $300,000 each to his wife Susan and to their two sons, Edson Jr. and Walter. His widow remained at 1906 Prairie, and his brother remained next door, while the avenue remembers him as the gentle brother, the man who never spoke an unkind word about anyone, who walked out of his own front door at six one Monday morning and went to the lake. His story is a reminder that wealth and a respectable address could not protect anyone from long and private suffering.
