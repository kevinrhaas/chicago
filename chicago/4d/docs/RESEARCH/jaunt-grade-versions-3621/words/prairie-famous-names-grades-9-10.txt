== premise
A summer dusk on Millionaire's Row: Pullman, Glessner, Kimball, both Marshall Fields and Armour, and how each accumulated a fortune within a single lifetime.

== opening
JAUNT: FAMOUS NAMES. A summer dusk on Prairie Avenue, 1904. The lamplighters have made their rounds, the carriages are returning from the Loop, and behind these walls sit some of the wealthiest families in America. People call the street Millionaire's Row: at one point "sixteen of the 60 members of the Commercial Club of Chicago lived on Prairie Avenue".

Six houses on foot, from 1729 to 2115 Prairie, north to south: the man who taught America to sleep on trains, a farm-machinery manufacturer in a granite fortress, the piano king, the merchant prince and his son, and the meatpacker who used everything but the squeal. Every one of these fortunes was made in Chicago within a single lifetime.

Begin at the corner of Eighteenth Street, beside the largest house on the block.

== stop pullman
1729 Prairie, the Pullman mansion, built between 1870 and 1876, containing a theater of about two hundred seats, a pipe organ, a bowling alley, and later a palm room and an enormous conservatory. George M. Pullman grew rich young by raising Chicago out of its mud, jacking up entire buildings "on average six feet without causing them any damage". Then he manufactured sleeping cars, and when Lincoln's body rode home to Springfield in one, "hundreds of thousands of people lined the route in homage". Pullman died in 1897, despised by the strikers of his company town; his widow still maintains this house, which will be demolished in 1922. He also despised one thing on this street, and it stands directly behind you.

== stop glessner
1800 Prairie, John J. Glessner's house. Pullman complained, "I do not know what I have ever done to have that thing staring me in the face every time I go out of my door". Glessner earned his money manufacturing farm machinery for prairie agriculture, and two years ago his firm merged into the colossal International Harvester, with him as vice president. His architect, Henry Hobson Richardson, died weeks before construction began in 1886; the family moved in on 1 December 1887 and remain here. Sixty years from now the house will narrowly escape demolition, rescued in 1966 "for just $35,000". Across the avenue, listen for pianos.

== stop kimball
1801 Prairie, the Kimball mansion of 1890, a French chateau designed by Solon S. Beman with a steeply pitched slate roof and a round tower facing Eighteenth Street. William W. Kimball arrived in Chicago in 1857, "beginning with only four pianos", and by 1881 his factory was "churning out around 100 pianos and organs every week". This is his final summer: he "died at his home in Chicago on December 16, 1904". Now walk one block south to the wealthiest man in the city.

== stop field
1905 Prairie, Marshall Field's mansion, designed by Richard Morris Hunt of New York. Field came from Massachusetts and became "the wealthiest and most powerful businessman in Chicago", worth $125 million by 1905. His store's guiding principle was "Give the lady what she wants", and the ladies made him rich. He and George Pullman both purchased land on this street early, and the rest of Chicago's elite followed them here. His house will stand until 1955, when it will be demolished. Next year his world will collapse, and the collapse begins two doors south.

== stop field-jr
1919 Prairie, Marshall Field Jr.'s house. He acquired it in 1890 and repeatedly expanded it; in 1902 D. H. Burnham and Company built an enormous addition, now two years old. Upstairs live his children, including Marshall Field III, ten years old. In November 1905 the father will be shot in this house, and within two months the grandfather will also be dead, of pneumonia contracted on a New Year's golf course. The boy will grow up in England. For now, the lights glow warmly. One more house remains, at the far end of the walk.

== stop armour
2115 Prairie, the Kelley-Armour house, built in 1871 and home to the Armour family for nearly fifty years. During the gold rush, Philip D. Armour skipped the shovels and "started a business employing out-of-work miners to construct sluices". Back in Chicago he built Armour and Company, "the world's largest food processing and chemical manufacturing enterprise", boasting that he used "everything but the squeal". In 1893 he donated a million dollars to found the Armour Institute of Technology. He died in 1901; his widow still lives here and will outlast nearly everyone on the avenue. The house itself will be demolished in 1937.

== leg 0
Across Eighteenth Street. The Battle of Fort Dearborn of 1812 was fought in the sand hills beneath this end of the street.

== leg 1
Across the avenue to the turret, where Kimball's chateau faces Glessner's fortress.

== leg 2
South past the Coleman and Sears houses. By 1886 "the finest mansions in the city, each equipped with its own carriage house, stood on Prairie Avenue".

== leg 3
Two doors south, where father and son lived side by side.

== leg 4
Onward past Twentieth Street, down the long east sidewalk to the last great house.

== ending finish
The walk ends at 2115 Prairie. Mrs. Armour remained: the district marker records her among the street's last residents, dying here in 1927. Most of the others had already departed. After Bertha Palmer built her castle on Lake Shore Drive, "the elite residents began to move north", and one by one these mansions became boarding houses, warehouses and parking lots.

A few survived. Glessner's fortress still stands, as does Kimball's chateau. Return after dark some night, when the avenue tells its ghost stories.
