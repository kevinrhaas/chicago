== premise
Play a young runner of the settlement at Fort Dearborn in the week the order to abandon the post arrives, August 1812: choose who hears it from you first, and where you are on the last nights before the gate opens.

== opening
JAUNT: ERRANDS BEFORE THE MARCH. Monday, 10 August 1812, at the mouth of the Chicago River. You are thirteen, you live in the settlement outside the pickets of Fort Dearborn, and you are the fastest pair of legs on the post: soldiers, traders and families all send you with their messages. The country has been at war with Britain since June. Last night an order came from the general at Detroit, and by this morning half the post has guessed what it says.

It is read on the parade today, and by noon everyone at the river mouth will know that the fort is to be given up. Who hears it from you first, and where you are on the last nights before the march, is up to you. The people and the places are real; the runner is you.

== stop the-order
10 August 1812, morning, on the parade. General William Hull's order is read aloud: 'It is with regret I order the Evacuation of your Post owing to the want of Provisions'. The garrison is to go. The arms and ammunition are to be destroyed, and the goods in the government store handed out, some of them to 'the Poor & needy of your Post'. Wau-Bun says the officers' 'unanimous advice was, to remain where they were, and fortify themselves as strongly as possible.' They are overruled. Captain Heald will obey, and the men who argued with him will march out of the gate beside him. You heard all of it from the back of the crowd.

== stop kinzie
Across the river and up the bank to the Kinzie house, 'the dwelling-house and trading-establishment of Mr. Kinzie', and 'The fort was situated on the southern bank, directly opposite this mansion'. Kinzie's trade with the garrison is a monopoly: in January Heald made 'Messrs. Kinzie & Forsyth' the fort's only sutlers. An abandoned post is a lost market, and a garrison that leaves takes its custom with it. It is family news as well: Lieutenant Helm's wife Margaret is the 'step-daughter of old John Kinzie'. The Kinzies will not march with the soldiers. When the day comes, they will go by boat.

== stop agency
West of the fort, the government's log houses: the factor's store, and beside it the Agency House, 'an old-fashioned log building, with a hall running through the centre'. Since April the people outside the pickets have 'intrenched themselves in the Agency House'. Its piazzas 'were planked up, for greater security, port-holes were cut, and sentinels posted at night.' Heald has enrolled 'fifteen militiamen from the civilian population outside the fort.' Behind the factor's door is the store the order gives away, down to the last blanket. By the end of the week the shelves will be bare.

== stop council
12 August, afternoon, on the open ground west of the pickets. Heald wrote that the people of the country round 'came in from all quarters in order to receive the goods in the factory store, which they understood were to be given them.' Their leaders meet him here in council, and he promises them the goods. 'Captain Heald alone attended on the part of the military.' His officers will not stand with him. They take the blockhouses that overlook this ground, open the port-holes and point the cannon 'so as to command the whole assembly.' It is a promise made under the guns. What he means to do with the powder and the whiskey, he does not say.

== stop covered-way
13 August, evening, above the fort's north-east corner. All day the factory store has been emptied: 'blankets, broadcloths, calicoes, paints'. Now comes the part of the bargain that was never spoken. 'The same evening the ammunition and liquor were carried, part into the sally-port, and thrown into a well which had been dug there to supply the garrison with water in case of emergency'. The muskets 'were broken up and thrown into the well'. The rest goes 'as secretly as possible through the northern gate, the heads of the barrels knocked in, and the contents poured into the river.' Heald feared they 'would make bad use of it'. You carry, and you keep quiet, and the knocking carries anyway.

== stop the-gate
14 August, sunset, at the main gate. The store is empty: Heald has given out its goods 'and a considerable quantity of provisions which we could not take away with us.' Captain William Wells rides in from Fort Wayne with his escort, Heald says 'about 30 Miamies'. Wells is Rebekah Heald's uncle, and he has come to bring her and her husband out. Wau-Bun says he came 'all too late': the ammunition is already destroyed. You saw him on the road before the sentry did. Tomorrow 'at 9 o'clock in the morning' the gate opens, and everyone inside walks out of it.

== ending strong-grog
By morning the water will taste of strong grog, and you know exactly why. You carried the order to the Kinzie house on the first day and rolled the barrels on the last night but one. On Saturday the garrison marches and the Kinzies go by boat. Whatever the fort leaves behind, there is nothing left in it to drink, and everyone outside the pickets knows it.

== ending news-both-ways
Your week began with news carried out of the fort and ends with news riding into it: the order to the Kinzie house, and Captain Wells through the gate at sunset, too late to change anything. Tonight nobody on the post is asleep. At nine tomorrow the band strikes up, the gate opens, and the column takes the beach with the lake on its left. The fort burns the morning after.

== ending nothing-left-behind
You told the Agency House first, and on the last night but one you helped make sure the fort would leave no powder and no whiskey behind it. The sentinels on the planked-up piazzas have one more night to stand. On Saturday at nine the garrison and the twelve militiamen march out, and the next morning, Heald wrote, 'they set fire to the fort and left the place'.

== ending the-last-sunset
From the Agency House on the first morning to the gate at the last sunset, you carried the week's news on your own feet. Captain Wells is inside now, and the gates shut behind him for the last time. Tomorrow at nine they open, and the fort's week of waiting is over. Two days from now it will be ash; the army will come back in 1816 and build again on the same ground.
