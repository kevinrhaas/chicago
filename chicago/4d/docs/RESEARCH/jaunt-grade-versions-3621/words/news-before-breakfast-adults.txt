== opening
JAUNT: NEWS BEFORE BREAKFAST. Daybreak, 1 July 1835. Chicago prints two weekly papers this summer, and one of them is three weeks old. The cholera has been in the state again, with deaths counted in the towns down the rivers, and everyone wants to know how close it is. The papers also want to sell you things, and fear sells better than anything.

Learning to tell the two apart may be the most useful thing a newcomer can do, and in a cholera summer it may keep you alive, or at least keep your money. Choose your question, ride from the Sauganash to both printing offices, decide which columns report and which ones sell, and carry one clipping to breakfast at the Tremont House.

== stop question
Start at the Sauganash, the white two-storey house with bright-blue shutters that Juliette Kinzie remembered; the Democrat of 10 June reported it repaired, fitted up and furnished with neatness. Both papers print a short ride east along the river. Before the town had a paper, news came with the mail, 'often irregular and long delayed', and somebody read it out standing on 'a dry goods box'. Now you can read it yourself, and decide for yourself how much of it to believe. The Democrat comes out on Wednesdays, the American on Saturday mornings.

== stop democrat
John Calhoun printed the Chicago Democrat at this corner from 1833; by 20 May its office was over Jones & King's hardware on South Water Street. The 24 June number, the last before today, gathers cholera reports: a Springfield paper on the health there, the Alton Spectator's count of about twenty deaths in three weeks, most cases recovering, nothing in the Galenian, and an Ottawa rumour it could not trace. It calls Chicago uncommonly healthy, as any town paper would in a land-sale summer, and promises to print the facts if the disease appears.

== stop american
Near the drawbridge, T. O. Davis's Chicago American is three weeks old. Its 27 June number carries Frederick Thomas's Cholera Elixir, the Fire King's Specific, offered as a certain preventive and cure of the asphyxiated cholera, in a notice dated 24 June: the day the Democrat printed its count of the dead. A deadly disease, and a druggist down the street selling a certain cure for it by the bottle. Whatever is in the bottle, the certainty is the thing being sold.

== stop tremont
Breakfast at the Tremont House, Alanson Sweet's frame of 1833 at Lake and Dearborn, kept since 1834 by Ira Couch. Moses and Kirkland call it 'the nucleus of the present Tremont House': its brick successor of 1850 was thought so 'absurdly large' that people called it Couch's Folly. This frame house will burn in 1839. The Democrat's third page announces a new auction house on South Water Street, its first public sale set for the 1st of July: today. Plague in the next column, profit in this one.

== ending cholera-answered
You asked whether the cholera was near and carry back a report, not a promise: twenty dead in three weeks at Alton, a rumour that could not be traced, and the editor's word that Chicago is healthy, given by a man whose town is selling land. Keep reading.

== ending sale-today
You asked what was new today and carry back a sale: an auction room's first, on South Water Street, this very morning. What will be sold the notice leaves to whoever brings the goods, and what it fetches depends on how much the room has had to drink.

== ending one-fooled-you
One of the two notices fooled you, which is worth knowing too, and better learned over breakfast than over a sickbed. The Democrat named the papers its cholera news came from; the elixir was signed, dated and sold by the druggist. Read them again before you trust either.

== ending cure-kept
You kept the advertisement. It promises a certain cure, and a promise is all it is: signed by the seller, reported by no editor, priced for frightened people. A useful thing to know, and you carry it back.

== ending a-useful-clipping
Two papers compared before breakfast and one clipping in your pocket. The morning's lesson travels with it: a report names where its news came from, an advertisement names who is selling, and in a frightened town the second is louder.
