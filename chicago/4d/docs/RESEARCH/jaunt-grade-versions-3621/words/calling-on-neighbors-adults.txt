== opening
JAUNT: CALLING ON NEIGHBORS. You have taken a room in town this summer and carry two letters of introduction, which in 1835 is how a stranger becomes a neighbour, and how a nobody gets credit. Chicago is a town of strangers: the Democrat found them 'crowding every room of our public houses, and every room in which they can obtain accommodations, even to the extent of sleeping on the floor'. Any man can claim to be anyone here. A letter from home is the only proof he is not a swindler.

Present a letter, read a merchant's household, find where the town meets, and leave your card. Come winter, this town dances, and not always with propriety: at one ball Charles Hoffman saw 'a veteran officer in full uniform balancing to a tradesman's daughter, still in her short frock and trousers'. In summer it does business. Mind your manners, and your reputation.

== stop the-room
Start behind Peck's store at Mrs Rufus Brown's log boarding house, which Andreas names as one of the first-class boarding houses of the town. You lodge here, sharing a table with strangers, and you carry two letters of introduction from home. In a town where nearly everyone arrived within the last three years, a letter from someone back east is how people learn who you are, whether you pay your debts, and whether your family is worth knowing. Choose the one to present first.

== stop the-merchant
A few steps round to Peck's corner of South Water and LaSalle. P. F. W. Peck came in July 1831 with a stock of goods, and when the Rev. Jeremiah Porter arrived in 1833 Peck gave him 'a lodging place and study in the second story of his store building', where 'one of the earliest Sunday-schools organized in the town' met. This is also the year Peck marries Mary K. Wythe of Philadelphia, and years later he will be credited 'with having $40,000 in cash locked in an iron safe in his dwelling'. Piety and money, under one roof. Read the household on its card.

== stop the-exchange
West to Lake and Wells and the Exchange Coffee House, kept by Mr. and Mrs. Murphy since August 1834. The town's Democrats met here in April and again in June 1835 to name their candidates, and in 1834 the dancing master J. A. Marshall asked would-be pupils to leave their address here. A public house is post box, notice board, party hall and marriage market at once, and the men who drink here decide who runs the town. Leave your card, or carry it on.

== stop the-sauganash
Along Lake Street to Market and the Sauganash, newly fitted up in June and, Moses and Kirkland say, 'the most attractive hostlery the city possessed'. A week after the refit the Democrat found strangers crowding every room in town, and every one of them, like you, is a name on a card, some of them honest names. A card still in your pocket is left here, among the land agents and the men who live on credit and nerve.

== ending merchant-at-the-exchange
A merchant's letter presented and your card left at the Exchange, where the Democrats meet and the dancing master took his names. By next week the right people will know who you are, and what you are worth to them.

== ending minister-at-the-exchange
A minister's letter presented and your card left at the Exchange, where the town comes to drink and vote. With luck, a congregation calls on you before Sunday, and vouches for you to the men who lend money.

== ending card-at-the-sauganash
Your card is left at the Sauganash, among a summer's crowd of strangers. In a year most of them will be neighbours, a few of them will be rich, and some will have slipped out of town owing everyone.
