== premise
Make a brief crossing between the settlement's divisions and discover why the forks of the river mattered so much.

== opening
JAUNT: ACROSS WOLF POINT. Daybreak in spring, with the river running high. This is where Chicago originated: at the forks, where the North and South Branches converge and flow east to the lake as a single river. Before there were streets there were taverns here, along with the first bridges, and a ferryman who doubled as the fiddler.

Cross the river as the town did: from the tavern that gave Wolf Point its name, down the west bank past the Green Tree, over the South Branch bridge, and up to the Sauganash. Four stops, nothing to purchase, and the entire early settlement in view.

== stop the-fork
Begin on the west bank at the tavern that named Wolf Point, constructed partly of logs and partly of frame. Its sign was fashioned by an army officer, Lieutenant Allen, from a piece of a packing box, with a wolf painted on it. Whether it still accommodates travelers is disputed: one account has William Walters operating it until 1836, while another claims it closed in 1834. Look east: the two branches converge off this bank and flow to the lake as one river.

== narrative the-fork
Read the fork before you cross it.

== stop west-bank
South along the bank to the Green Tree, at Lake and West Water, which James Kinzie built in 1833 as the first house here constructed specifically for hotel purposes, "so called from an oak tree standing close by". Old settlers remembered Chester Ingersoll operating it from 1834 to 1837. It is the remarkable survivor among the town's taverns: sixty years later it still stands, relocated to Milwaukee Avenue, "the oldest memento of early taverns".

== narrative west-bank
Face east: the south side is your destination.

== stop the-crossing
A few steps south lies the South Branch bridge between Lake and Randolph. Moses and Kirkland report that it "at first consisted of floating logs tied together"; four old settlers attested that it rested on log abutments and two bents, about ten feet wide and six feet above the water, high enough for teams to pass beneath it on the ice. It was financed by public subscription, $486.20, "of which the Indians of the vicinity contributed $200".

== narrative the-crossing
This is the crossing: the deck, never the water.

== stop south-side
Off the bridge and north to Lake and Market stands the Sauganash, white and two stories tall, with bright-blue wooden shutters, which the Democrat of 10 June 1835 found newly refurbished. Moses and Kirkland describe it as "the most attractive hostlery the city possessed". Its landlord, Mark Beaubien, was "fiddler, landlord, merchant, ferryman and a patron of horse racing", and in winter the town raced its horses on the frozen river you have just crossed.

== narrative south-side
Down the west bank, over the bridge, north to this corner: you know the route now.

== leg 0
Observe the two branches first, then walk along the one you will cross.

== leg 1
The South Branch is the water separating you from the south side.

== leg 2
Arrive on the south side at the town's most celebrated house.

== ending crossed
You have crossed between the divisions exactly as the town did, by the bridge rather than the water, from the oldest tavern corner to the most famous one. Return when the river freezes, for the races.
