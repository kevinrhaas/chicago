== premise
Scrutinize a land notice, observe the room where lots are auctioned, locate your corner and evaluate the terrain before committing any money.

== opening
JAUNT: LOOK BEFORE YOU BUY A LOT. Chicago in the summer of 1835 is consumed by speculative mania, and "The opening of a land office in May, 1835, added fuel to the already brilliant blaze of excitement". Lots that sold for $10 to $50 in 1830 now command "twenty or thirty times as much", and a portion of the town that sold for $20,000 last summer resold this May for $100,000.

Sales are conducted by public auction, and a crier in scarlet on a white horse parades through town announcing the hour, and "Crowds flocked around and followed him and hung upon his entrancing words". Before you join them, scrutinize what is genuinely being offered: the room, the notices, the corner and the terrain itself.

== stop auction
Commence on Dearborn Street at John Bates Junior's new auction room. On 12 and 13 June the Democrat's notice offered fifty town lots of Marseilles here, with fifty more to follow, although Marseilles constitutes an entirely separate town. During this speculative boom, Moses and Kirkland observe, "The whole land seemed staked out and peopled on paper", and "But few of these paper towns ever came to anything". Reread the advertisement considerably more attentively.

== stop notice
West to Clark, where the Democrat was printed from 1833 until, by 20 May, its office had relocated above Jones and King's hardware. Your copy contains two land notices: Hubbard's lots in the School Section and the original town, and Fell's land along the proposed canal route. Valuations have become extraordinary: this spring Gurdon Hubbard and his partners purchased eighty acres for $5,000, platted them in New York and "sold half the lots at auction for $80,000", a sixteenfold return on half the property.

== stop corner
Continue west to LaSalle, where Peck's two-story frame store occupies the corner his own advertisements identify. A lot is defined by its corners and street lines, so establish your orientation: the river lies north, and the town's lots extend south. Philo Carpenter, who arrived in 1832, paid for "two lots on South Water street, forty feet frontage, at a cost of $75". During the speculative frenzy, "Lot 1, block 2, sold in June for $5,000 and resold in August for $10,000".

== narrative corner
Locate the surveyed corner and face south, toward the rows of platted lots.

== stop ground
Several steps east, a modest drain passes beneath South Water to the river, and the street traverses it on logs. An account of the early town characterizes the South Division as generally low and marshy, wetter still along the river, and covered with tall grass, reeds and rushes. The schoolmaster Granville Sproat recalled mud "half a leg deep, up to the hubs of the carts and wagons". Determine what the condition of the terrain communicates about its value.

== ending inquire-town
You will investigate one particular lot from Hubbard's notice and stand upon its actual terrain before paying. In a town where valuations double between June and August, such diligence makes you the rarest purchaser in Chicago.

== ending inquire-canal
You will apply at the New York House, as Fell's notice directs, for the terms of one parcel along the canal route, and traverse it before committing payment. Everyone insists the canal is imminent, so verify that the land is equally substantial.

== ending held
You retain your money, since a notice, a sale room and a waterlogged street have instructed you sufficiently for one day. Remember it when the speculative bubble collapses: after 1836, nearly every purchaser at the first canal-lot sale forfeits his lots, and only Peck completes his second payment.
