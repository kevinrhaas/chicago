== opening
JAUNT: THE LAST PULLMAN TWIN. Dusk on a Monday in November 1904. The lamps are coming on along Prairie Avenue.

Four stops, from the biggest house on the street to the house of the one Pullman child who turned out the way her father wanted. George Pullman built sleeping cars, a company town and a fortune, and held his workers' wages down to keep it. He could not build sons. His twins, George Jr. and Sanger, were born in 1875 into more money than almost anyone in America, and spent their lives showing the city what that does to a boy: fighting dogs, champagne, a stolen bride, a woman paid to go away, and a fast horse.

Start at the Pullman house, where the widow lives with the shades half drawn.

== stop widow
1729 Prairie, the Pullman house, the biggest pile on the block. Harriett Sanger married George Pullman in 1867, and they had four children: Florence, Harriett, and in 1875 the twins, George Jr. and Walter Sanger, whom everyone calls Sanger. In 1894 Pullman cut his workers' wages, but 'he didn't reduce the rent in Pullman', his company town. He died of a heart attack in 1897 and left his widow this house, which she still keeps. To the twins he left a verdict, written into his will: "Pullman's will stipulated that the twins were to be given a yearly stipend of only $3,000." A man who knew the price of everything knew his sons. Henry Windish, the coachman, has Mrs. Pullman's carriage at the door. The house will be razed in 1922. Go round to the glasshouse, where the boys grew up.

== stop boys
The Pullman conservatory, glass and palms at the side of the house. The twins grew up here, schooled at home on Prairie Avenue, and allegedly "arranging and promoting pit bull fights": dogs set on dogs, by the sons of the richest house on the street. As teenagers they "frequently were seen riding around Chicago in cabs filled with champagne bottles." Sent east to a training school for Harvard, "Sanger and George were ranked 147 and 148 out of a class of 148 students." Last and next to last, side by side, as close as twins should be. And Sanger was "widely acknowledged as the smarter of the 2". It never helped him. Cross the street to look back at the house where the other twin's story ended.

== stop george
From the west side of the street, look back at the Pullman house in the dusk. The other twin is gone. George Jr. secretly married Lynn Fernald, "his brother's betrothed": he took his own twin's bride. Frederick Bowers sued him for $100,000, "eventually settling for $50,000." In November 1901 George died of complications from pneumonia, at 26. The World ran the headline: "Death of George Pullman a Warning to Rich Fathers." So Sanger is the last twin, 29 now and "very poor with money": expelled from the New York Athletic Club for unpaid dues, his debts so tangled that a creditor once held his sister-in-law's luggage for five months. His mother has paid before. Walk south to the sister who never needed paying for.

== stop sister
1912 Prairie, the Lowden house. Florence, the eldest Pullman child, married Frank Orren Lowden, a lawyer, and here they live with their children. When trouble comes to this family it tends to come to Lowden's office, and what the law cannot settle, the mother's money does. Once she "paid the unnamed woman $10,000 to leave Sanger and never return." The woman has no name in the record; that was what the $10,000 was for. The children play at their nurse's feet on a quiet Monday evening, the way the twins once did on this same street. The house will be razed in 1939. And the last twin is still out there, riding hard.

== ending ride
End of the walk. Sanger Pullman lived nine more months. In August 1905 he "died after being thrown from a horse and carriage after yet another wild ride". His mother had now buried her husband and both her sons.

Her husband lay in Graceland in a "lead-lined mahogany coffin, which was then sealed inside a block of concrete", under steel rails, for fear that 'some of the employees weren't quite done getting revenge'. He had guarded his body from his workers. He could not guard his boys from his money. The World's headline of 1901 still stood: "a Warning to Rich Fathers."
