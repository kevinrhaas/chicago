== opening
JAUNT: TAVERNS OF CHICAGO. A summer night in 1835, and the town is bursting at the seams and drinking to it. The land office opened in May and, as one history put it, 'added fuel to the already brilliant blaze of excitement'. Every bed is taken, the bar-rooms are loud with men who made money today and men who lost it, and the whole town is talking about lots, banks and boats, louder with every gill.

Four public houses stand within a short ride of the forks, kept by some of the best characters in town. Start at the Sauganash, where the landlord plays the fiddle, ride over to the west bank, and finish wherever you like. The county has fixed the price of a drink since 1831: '6¼ for a gill of whiskey', half the price of a night's lodging, which tells you which one the town buys more of. Drink or don't. Either way, keep your ears open and your hand on your purse.

First, decide how much money you are carrying.

== stop purse
The Sauganash, at Lake and Market: the white two-storey house with bright-blue shutters that Juliette Kinzie remembered, and in June the Democrat found it repaired, fitted up and furnished with neatness. Moses and Kirkland call it 'the most attractive hostlery the city possessed', and its landlord, Mark Beaubien, 'with his merry fiddle, the most popular of hosts'. He was 'the fiddler par excellence of the town'. Judge Caton said Mark could 'lift the sluggard from his seat, and set him whirling over the floor like mad'. A fiddler who sells the drink and then plays the tune that makes men thirsty is a landlord who knows his trade.

== stop wolf-point
Across the river at the forks stands the Wolf Point Tavern, with its painted wolf sign. William Walters is named its landlord until 1836, though one chronology says it stopped taking guests in 1834, so whether you get served depends on whom you believe, and how thirsty you are. At the bar the talk is the papers' talk: no house in Chicago is big enough, and a grand new one, the Lake House, is going up across the river. It 'aimed to cater for only the highest class of guests'. Wolf Point never did, and the men drinking here know exactly which class they are.

== stop green-tree
A short way south, the Green Tree, which James Kinzie built in 1833, the first house here put up for hotel purposes, 'so called from an oak tree standing close by'. Chester Ingersoll keeps it. Guests remembered a bar-room, whitewashed walls, board partitions and chambers about twelve feet square with two windows: board partitions, so you hear every snore, quarrel and bargain in the next room. It is a plain house and a lucky one: it will outlive every tavern in this town, moved to Milwaukee Avenue in 1880, 'the oldest memento of early taverns'.

== stop western
End the circuit a block south at W. H. Stow's Western Hotel, Randolph and Canal: a forty-foot front, a sixty-foot wing, a big stable and a yard the farmers' wagon trains drive into, full of teamsters who have been on the road for days and mean to make up for it. Not everyone approves of all this drinking. In 1832 Philo Carpenter, the druggist, 'prepared and circulated the first temperance pledge among the convivial citizens' of the town. It has not caught on: the census at the end of this year will count eight taverns and five churches, and soon the trustees will ban 'gaming houses, and the sale of liquors on Sunday'. Six days a week, the town is wet.

== ending talk-is-free
You brought no purse and missed nothing. The talk was free, the fiddle was free, and you carry home every word of a boom town's night, sober enough to remember which of the men boasting of fortunes were lying.

== ending clear-head
You declined every glass and kept your purse whole. Philo Carpenter would have you sign his pledge on the spot. A clear head carries the night's talk home intact, and wakes tomorrow without regret, which is more than most of the town can say.

== ending two-houses
A gill in two bar-rooms, at about the county's price, and still a modest evening. You let the gossip stay gossip and ride home slowly under the stars, warm, a little unsteady, and none the poorer.

== ending a-sensible-evening
One glass, a great deal of talk and a purse barely touched. A sensible evening, in a town that has lost its head over land, and will lose a good deal more than its head before the boom is done.
