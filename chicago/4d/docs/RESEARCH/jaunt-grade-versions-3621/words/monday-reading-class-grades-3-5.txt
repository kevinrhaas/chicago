== premise
Every Monday from October to May, the ladies of Chicago come to the stone house at Eighteenth and Prairie to sew and listen to a reader. This Monday, the man across the street is very sick.

== opening
JAUNT: MONDAY AT THE FORTRESS. It is Monday morning, 21 November 1904, at the corner of Eighteenth Street and Prairie Avenue.

There are five stops in and around the Glessner House. Today is Monday, so it is the day of the Reading Class. The wives of college teachers and the ladies of the South Side meet in the library. Frances Glessner invites them. Inside these walls everything runs like a clock, and you hardly ever see the servants. Across Eighteenth Street lives the man who makes pianos. He is very ill.

Start at the corner, where the neighbors first saw this house.

== stop fortress
This is the corner of Eighteenth and Prairie. The house is made of granite stone. It is low and hard. The windows on the street side are very narrow. The architect, H. H. Richardson, died in 1886, before it was done. It was built from 1886 to 1887. Not everyone liked it. George Pullman, across the street, said he did not know what he had done to have "that thing staring me in the face" every time he went out. Pullman has been dead seven years, and the house is still staring. People walking by never see the other side, with big sunny windows on a courtyard. The ladies are arriving on foot.

== caption fortress
Ladies walk down Prairie Avenue in pairs for the Monday class.

== stop hostess
This is the front door. In 1893 the president of the new University of Chicago asked Frances Glessner a question. How could the teachers' wives meet the women of the city? Her answer was the Monday Morning Reading Class. It meets in the library for two hours every Monday from October to May. In the first hour the ladies sew while a reader reads out loud. The second hour is lighter, a reading or a talk. You cannot just ask to join. You must be invited. Mrs. Glessner greets her guests herself. Christine Nelson, the maid, waits to take their coats.

== caption hostess
Frances Glessner greets her guests at the door. Christine Nelson stands behind her.

== stop hall
Inside, the house turns its back on the street and opens to the courtyard. The door to the courtyard has yellow glass in it. It makes the hall feel sunny even on a cloudy day, and today is cloudy. The house is like a puzzle box. Every big room has two or more doors. The halls are planned so the servants are hardly ever seen. So while the ladies sew in the library, a second household moves around them out of sight: the cook, the maids, and the man who keeps the fires going. The ladies sew. The reader reads. The helpers stay out of sight in the courtyard, where only you can see them.

== caption hall
Out of sight, the house is busy. A maid crosses the courtyard with a tray, and the cook waits at the kitchen door.

== stop bench
These are the back stairs, down to the basement. Down there is the newest room in the house. This year Frances Glessner set up a workbench. She makes silver things with a hammer and a torch. She stamps each one with her mark, a G around a honeybee. Upstairs she is the most proper hostess in Chicago. Downstairs she is a craftsman taking lessons. Both are true. But across Eighteenth Street a neighbor has been sick for a long time, and the doctor is coming again this morning. Look out through the gates at the corner house. It belongs to William Kimball, the piano maker.

== caption bench
August Billings brings coal up from the cellar. He stops on the stairs to look toward the street.

== stop kimball
This is 1801 Prairie, across Eighteenth Street, the Kimball house. William Kimball came to Chicago in 1857. He traded his land in Iowa for four pianos, and he sold them from a small upstairs office. Now his factory makes about a hundred pianos and organs every week. His name is on pianos all over America. He and his wife, Evaline, have no children. He is very sick, and he will die at home on 16 December. Watch the doctor go up the steps, and the house helper who lets him in. At the funeral, John Glessner from across the street will be one of the men honoring him.

== ending clock
The walk ends here. William Kimball died on 16 December 1904. John Glessner crossed Eighteenth Street to honor him at the funeral. The Reading Class went on. It met every Monday from October to May until 1930. When Frances Glessner died, her husband wrote that she had "a clean and wholesome and orderly mind", and a heart full of love for family and friends and anyone in need. That is the house in one sentence: clean, kind, orderly, and full of people you never see.
