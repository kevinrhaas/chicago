== premise
Marshall Field Jr. has a wife, three children, a lavish new wing on his house and the largest fortune in Chicago awaiting him. On this November afternoon, he has exactly one year and one day to live.

== opening
JAUNT: ONE YEAR AND A DAY. A Monday afternoon, 21 November 1904, at the Field residences on Prairie Avenue.

Four stops between two houses: the father's and the son's. Marshall Field built one of the great department stores of America and amassed the largest fortune in Chicago. His only surviving son lives two doors away, thirty-six years old, wealthy, and still waiting, as he has waited his entire life, to become the man his father is. Count the days alongside him. Exactly one year and one day from now, a gunshot will echo through the house at 1919.

Begin at the son's front door.

== stop son
1919 Prairie, the residence of Mr. and Mrs. Marshall Field Jr. He acquired it in 1890, and in 1902 D. H. Burnham and Company constructed "a huge addition" for him. He married Albertine Huck, and they have three children: Henry, Marshall III and little Gwendolyn. Observe them on the front walk this afternoon, the nurse holding the youngest, the boys bundled in their coats. Everything a man could desire is assembled on this walk, except his own name, which belongs to his father. He has been Junior all his life, and the title has never once loosened its grip. Walk north, two doors up, to the house where the name truly resides.

== caption son
Marshall Field Jr. and his wife step out onto the walk where the nurse waits with their children.

== stop father
1905 Prairie, the father's mansion, where the son was raised. It was "the first in Chicago to be completely wired for electricity". There had been a first son, Louis, who died in infancy; this son survived and was groomed to inherit the store that taught America to "Give the lady what she wants". His father is "the wealthiest and most powerful businessman in Chicago", and he does not surrender power. Indeed, he intends to pass it beyond his son entirely: his will is designed to hold the fortune in trust for forty years for the grandsons now playing on the walk at 1919. The son climbs to his father's door for his customary Monday visit, and Benson the butler admits him. The mansion will be demolished in 1955.

== caption father
Marshall Field Jr. walks up to his father's door, and Thomas Benson opens it for him.

== stop talk
Farther up the street, turn and regard both houses. This is what the street observes: a dutiful son paying his respects. And this is what the street murmurs behind its curtains. The Levee district lies three blocks west, and the Everleigh Club on Dearborn Street, a notorious brothel, counts the wealthiest young men of Chicago among its patrons; the gossips include Marshall Field Jr. Nobody has proved it, and nobody ever will. Yet a year and a day from now, the rumor will harden into legend: "The most popular story is that he was in fact shot at the Everleigh Club", then transported home to disguise it as an accident. Others will insist it was suicide. Return to 1919.

== caption talk
A gentleman and a lady stroll past the Field houses, and the gentleman inclines his head toward 1919.

== stop shot
1919 once more, as the afternoon fades. The children have gone inside. On Wednesday, 22 November 1905, one year and a day from this moment, Marshall Field Jr. will be shot in this house, and he will survive five days. At Mercy Hospital he will declare, "I shot myself with that revolver – accidentally", and the coroner's jury will conclude that he "had shot himself unintentionally while examining a revolver". Two reporters will receive tips that he spent that evening at the Everleigh Club; the sisters will deny it, and nobody will ever confirm it. Study the lighted windows. The son of the richest man in Chicago is home for dinner, with one year and a day remaining.

== ending six-weeks
The walk ends here. Marshall Field Jr. died at Mercy Hospital on Monday, 27 November 1905. His father, seventy-one and recently remarried, went out to play golf on New Year's Day, contracted pneumonia, and "six weeks later, Marshall Field, Sr. was dead". The fortune, valued at $125 million, passed in trust to the boys from the front walk at 1919, exactly as the will had arranged, with Marshall III receiving sixty percent and Henry forty. The son never became anything but Junior; his sons inherited everything.
