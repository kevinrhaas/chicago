== premise
Play the Chicago Democrat's new apprentice on publication day, 1 July 1835, in a town reading about cholera: choose where the paper goes first and where you linger, and come back to the office with your own story of the morning.

== opening
JAUNT: APPRENTICE AT THE DEMOCRAT. Wednesday, 1 July 1835, and you are thirteen. In May the Chicago Democrat printed: 'Wanted immediately, an apprentice at this office to the printing business'. It added that 'A good opportunity is offered by an immediate application'. You applied immediately. Now you sweep, ink the type, fold the sheets and run, and on Wednesdays you carry the paper through a town that is half boom and half rumour.

The rumour this summer is cholera. It has been in the state, the papers are counting the dead, and everyone who opens your paper looks for that column first. Today's number is under your arm. Where it goes first, and where you linger, is up to you. The places and the papers are real; the apprentice is you.

== stop old-corner
This corner of South Water and Clark is where John Calhoun printed the first number of the Democrat, on 26 November 1833. The office has moved since: by May it was upstairs over Jones & King's hardware store, along South Water Street, and that is where you work. The paper comes out every Wednesday; today's is No. 11 of its second volume. And since 8 June the town has a second weekly, the Chicago American, out on Saturday mornings, chasing the same readers and the same advertisers.

== stop sauganash
West along the river to Lake and Market and the Sauganash. Juliette Kinzie remembered 'a pretentious white two-story building, with bright-blue wooden shutters'. Your own paper looked it over this June and found it repaired, fitted up and furnished with neatness and taste, which is the kind of notice a landlord likes to read. Moses and Kirkland call it 'the most attractive hostlery the city possessed', and Mark Beaubien 'the fiddler par excellence of the town'. When the mail came, 'often irregular and long delayed', somebody would 'mount a dry goods box' and read the news out. Now it is printed, and sold.

== stop rival
East along South Water Street, near the drawbridge, is the office of the Chicago American, T. O. Davis's paper, three weeks old. Its number of 27 June carries a druggist's notice for the Fire King's Specific, a cholera elixir offered as a certain preventive and cure of the asphyxiated cholera, dated the very day the Democrat printed its own cholera column. That column counted about twenty deaths in three weeks at Alton, chased a rumour it could not trace, and called Chicago uncommonly healthy. One paper sells reassurance, the other sells the cure.

== stop the-wharf
On to the river and Newberry & Dole's warehouse. They have advertised in the Democrat since its first issue, and their card is in today's number on page four: storage, forwarding and commission, agents for the Merchants' Line, dry goods, groceries and provisions. Advertisers like these keep a paper alive. When the schooner Illinois came up through the new harbour cut on 12 July 1834, she was cheered at their wharf. Which bank the warehouse stood on is still argued. You have a few copies left and a morning running out.

== stop the-draw
East to the foot of Dearborn Street and the first drawbridge, which the ship-carpenter Nelson R. Norton began in March 1834. Moses and Kirkland make it '300 feet in length, by sixteen in width', with 'a double draw'; Andreas gives an opening of about sixty feet and gallows frames at either end. Vessels kept striking it, and the town kept paying to patch it, in 1834 and again this year. Every sail that comes up to the wharves passes through this gap, and whatever it carries, cargo or contagion, is news. Stay on the south bank.

== stop the-tremont
Up to Lake and Dearborn and the Tremont House, Alanson Sweet's frame hotel of 1833, kept since 1834 by Ira Couch. Its brick successor of 1850 would be thought so 'absurdly large' that people called it Couch's Folly. Last week's Democrat announced a new auction and commission house on South Water Street, its first public sale set for 'the 1st day of July'. Today, and a first sale is news worth running for.

== ending two-papers
Back up the stairs over Jones & King's with ink on your fingers and two papers' news in your head: what the American is selling against the cholera, and what came up the river through the draw. Nobody asked you to look at either. An apprentice who notices may one day be the one who writes it up, or the one who decides what is left out.

== ending the-river-news
Back to the office with an empty arm and the river in your head: the Sauganash read first, the sails at the draw last. The Democrat comes out again next Wednesday. If the cholera comes up the river with one of those sails, it will be in that number too.

== ending sale-day
Every copy delivered, and both papers read before noon: the American's elixir at one end of the morning, the auction's first sale at the other. Fear and money, the two things that sell a paper in July. Upstairs over Jones & King's there is type to clean and a week to fill.

== ending the-round-done
The round is done: the Sauganash first, the Tremont last, every copy delivered before noon. Upstairs over Jones & King's, next week's type is waiting. Six weeks ago you answered a notice for an apprentice; this morning the town read, in the paper you carried, how healthy it was.
