== premise
Compare four of the town's lodging houses from the street and decide where a modest purse should inquire first.

== opening
JAUNT: A BED FOR THE NIGHT. The sun is setting on the busiest summer Chicago has ever experienced, and you have nowhere to sleep. Since the land office opened in May, buyers and speculators have "filled the streets and overflowed improvised camps on the adjacent prairies", and the American counted some hundreds of strangers in addition to the town's own two or three thousand residents.

Four houses that accommodate strangers lie between the wagon yards of the west side and Lake Street. Assess what each offers, decide whether cost or convenience takes priority, and choose where to knock, bearing in mind that in this town "full meant three in a bed sometimes, with the floor covered besides".

== stop west-side
Begin at the Western Hotel, at Randolph and Canal, which W.H. Stow built with a forty-foot front, a sixty-foot wing and a wagon yard as the stopping place for farmers and teamsters arriving from the west; he dates it 1834, while a chronology says 1835. Back in 1831 the county fixed what an innkeeper could charge: 12½ cents for a man's lodging, 25 for supper, and 50 for keeping a horse overnight. In a summer like this one, finding a bed at those prices would be remarkable.

== narrative west-side
Cost or convenience: settle the question before you knock on any door tonight.

== label west-side cost
Cost comes first

== consequence west-side cost
You will inquire after the plainer, cheaper bed, accepting whatever company it brings.

== label west-side convenience
Convenience comes first

== consequence west-side convenience
You will inquire after a bed close to the business of the town, whatever it costs.

== stop the-sauganash
Across the South Branch at Lake and Market stands the Sauganash, repaired and newly fitted up in June. A week later the Democrat described "the immense congregation of strangers crowding every room of our public houses", some "sleeping on the floor". The landlord, Mark Beaubien, had his own solution to a full house: he liked to say he "kept hotel like —" a place unmentionable in polite company, and described how he would put a man to sleep on the floor with a blanket and, once the traveler was "slumbering soundly", reclaim it for the next guest.

== narrative the-sauganash
A newly fitted house, and a landlord who counts his blankets.

== stop the-boarding-house
East to LaSalle, behind Peck's store, is Mrs Rufus Brown's log house, which Andreas ranks among the first-class boarding houses. A boarding house feeds you by the week at a common table, and this one is packed: "full meant three in a bed sometimes, with the floor covered besides". On 13 June the American found strangers filling both the public houses and the streets, and store houses thrown open for the emigrants who had no roof at all.

== narrative the-boarding-house
A bed and a place at the common table by the week, which is the plainer choice.

== stop lake-street
Continue along Lake to Dearborn and the Mansion House, Dexter Graves's log tavern of 1831, which received a frame front extending to the street line two years later. Haddock, his son-in-law, kept it until 1835, when Abram Markle took over; because nobody recorded the month, either man may be behind the bar tonight. Almost directly opposite stands the first Tremont House. It is time to choose where you will try your luck.

== narrative lake-street
Choose where to inquire first, remembering that nobody promises a vacant room.

From a hotel to a boarding house, which offers an entirely different kind of bed.

== leg 2
One final house to inspect, followed by your decision.

== ending thrift
Because cost came first, you knock initially at Mrs Brown's, and if any corner of a bed remains, it is yours, although you may have company in it, since in June 1835 every house in town was full.

== ending close-at-hand
Because convenience came first, you inquire initially on Lake Street, and if the house is full there is always the floor, and in June 1835 plenty of strangers slept on it.

== ending second-thoughts
You set out wanting one thing and chose according to another, which a full town makes easy. Sleep with one hand on your blanket, because Mark Beaubien is not the only landlord who counts them.
