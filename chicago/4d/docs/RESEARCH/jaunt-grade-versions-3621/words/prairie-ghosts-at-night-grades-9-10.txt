== premise
A nocturnal walk down Millionaire's Row: a battlefield underfoot, a tycoon entombed in concrete, a widow who rattles the windows, a drowned brother and the unexplained gunshot at 1919.

== opening
JAUNT: GHOSTS OF PRAIRIE AVENUE. An autumn night in 1904. The gas lamps are lit and the carriages are stabled. This is Millionaire's Row, where the mansions of George Pullman, Marshall Field, John J. Glessner and Philip Armour stand shoulder to shoulder, and where the wealthiest families in Chicago lock their doors at night against everyone except their ghosts.

Seven stops, on foot, from the corner of Eighteenth Street south to 1919 Prairie. You will encounter a battlefield beneath the pavement, a tycoon sealed in concrete, an architect who never saw his masterpiece, a widow who rattles the windows, a man who walked into the lake, and a gunshot nobody has adequately explained. The stories are legends, repeated by ghost tours and by the Glessner House Museum itself; the deaths behind them are entirely real.

Mind the shadows. Begin where the street begins: on the battleground of 1812.

== stop battle-ground
Prairie at Eighteenth, after dark. Before the mansions there were sand hills here, and on 15 August 1812 the Battle of Fort Dearborn was fought across them. The dead "lay in the sand, half buried where they were killed, until 1816", and for a century an enormous cottonwood marked the site. A Tribune writer insisted in 1900 that the Pullman house before you "stands about where the massacre took place". George Pullman was aware of it: in 1893 he erected a bronze of the Potawatomi chief Black Partridge rescuing Margaret Helm near this corner. According to legend, the fallen still walk here on clear, cold nights: "The Americans go searching for their families and friends while the Natives continue to patrol their land". The man who erected that statue walks nowhere, as his family ensured.

== stop pullman
1729 Prairie, the Pullman mansion. George Pullman earned his first fortune raising Chicago out of its own mud, jacking up entire buildings "on average six feet without causing them any damage". Then came the sleeping car, and Lincoln's body rode home to Springfield in one while "hundreds of thousands of people lined the route in homage". In 1894 he slashed his workers' wages, but "he didn't reduce the rent in Pullman", his company town, and his workers struck. When he died in 1897, his family feared grave robbers, so, the story goes, he was placed in a "lead-lined mahogany coffin, which was then sealed inside a block of concrete", beneath steel rails "bolted together at right angles". Mrs. Pullman still lives here; the house will be demolished in 1922. Her husband despised the house across the street.

== stop glessner
1800 Prairie, Glessner House, the granite fortress Pullman meant when he grumbled, "I do not know what I have ever done to have that thing staring me in the face every time I go out of my door". Its architect, Henry Hobson Richardson, died on 26 April 1886, five weeks before construction began on 1 June, and never saw it built. According to legend, he came to inspect it anyway. The family sensed a presence; cold spots invade warm rooms; "If you're lucky, you might catch the man's spirit standing on that same staircase". One later visitor described it as a "pleasant presence". Less pleasant was the eventual hobby of the Glessners' daughter Frances, who built tiny, perfect dioramas of real deaths, the Nutshell Studies of Unexplained Death, still used to train detectives. Now turn around: the house opposite is rattling.

== stop kimball
1801 Prairie, the Kimball mansion, all steep slate and turret. William W. Kimball began in 1857 "beginning with only four pianos" and built a factory "churning out around 100 pianos and organs every week". He and his wife Evaline filled the entrance hall with Old Master paintings. This winter the music stops: he "died at his home in Chicago on December 16, 1904". Evaline remained here until 1921, and in her final years, it is said, she hosted receptions for "empty rooms she thought were full of visitors". Today the paintings hang in the Art Institute, yet the large north windows shake and rattle when there is no wind whatsoever. The museum itself wonders, "Could it be that the spirit of Evaline Kimball has returned to the house" Perhaps she is searching for her pictures.

== stop keith
1900 Prairie, Elbridge G. Keith's house, its mansard roof black against the sky. A few doors south, at 1906, lives the widow of his brother Edson, a merchant. Edson Keith had not slept properly in a decade. Early on Monday, 21 September 1896, he slipped out of his house, walked to the lake at the foot of Sixteenth Street, and walked in; the papers reported that he "committed suicide Monday morning by drowning himself in Lake Michigan". He never returned home. Or did he? Ghost hunters claim "his ghost has been seen at his brother's house, where he sometimes appears in the windows". Glance up at the dark panes as you pass. Across the avenue, the richest man in Chicago is sleeping.

== stop field
1905 Prairie, Marshall Field's mansion. "Give the lady what she wants", declared the merchant, and the ladies made him "the wealthiest and most powerful businessman in Chicago", worth $125 million by 1905. Tonight everything appears secure; it is not. In thirteen months his son, next door, will be shot. Six weeks after that, Field will play golf on New Year's Day 1906 with Robert Todd Lincoln, Abraham Lincoln's son, contract pneumonia and die on 16 January. Father and son will both perish in a single winter. The mansion will survive until 1955, when it too will be demolished. Walk on to the son's house, the one with the footsteps.

== stop field-jr
1919 Prairie, Marshall Field Jr.'s house, with its massive new Burnham addition. On Wednesday, 22 November 1905, a shot will ring out upstairs. He will die five days later at Mercy Hospital, insisting, "I shot myself with that revolver – accidentally", and the coroner will agree. Chicago will not. Reporters will be tipped that he spent the evening at the Everleigh Club, a brothel a few blocks west on Dearborn Street: "The most popular story is that he was in fact shot at the Everleigh Club" and carried home to disguise it as an accident. Nobody ever proved it. Yet later owners heard footsteps echo through empty halls and "strange cries in the dead of night", and one family reportedly summoned an exorcist to banish a spirit that "sours the air around it". This house keeps its ghost.

== leg 0
Across the corner to the Pullman mansion, the largest pile on this block, its windows gaslit.

== leg 1
Over Eighteenth Street to the southwest corner and the rough granite wall facing Pullman's door.

== leg 2
Back across Prairie Avenue to the corner turret of the Kimball mansion.

== leg 3
South along the west sidewalk. This was Millionaire's Row: by 1886 "the finest mansions in the city, each equipped with its own carriage house, stood on Prairie Avenue".

== leg 4
Cross to the east side; Field's mansion is the broad one with the corner bay.

== leg 5
Two doors south, where father and son lived side by side.

== ending finish
The walk ends at 1919 Prairie. One final ghost for the road. Years later the Clarke House, "purported to be the city's oldest standing house" and "twice relocated", will return to this neighborhood, and in the park beside it, they say, a woman in a settler's dress drifts among the bushes after dark, while phantom carriage horses clip past that "always seem to have their bits, but never their harnesses".

Sleep well, Prairie Avenue.
