== premise
Round-headed windows, bracketed cornices and elevated stoops: five Italianate houses from 1612 to 2031 Prairie, representing the avenue's earliest generation of fronts.

== opening
JAUNT: ITALIANATE STYLE. An autumn morning, 1904. Before the mansards and the granite, Prairie Avenue was built in the Italianate manner: houses dressed like villas on a Tuscan hillside, the prevailing fashion of the 1860s, when the first substantial houses rose along this street.

Look for three features: tall round-headed windows, deep cornices supported on carved brackets, and high stoops elevating the front door above the street. Five houses on foot, from 1612 to 2031 Prairie, all from the avenue's oldest generation.

Begin at 1612, with the finest Italianate façade on the street.

== stop goodman
1612 Prairie, Joseph Goodman's house of 1869, built two years before the Great Fire. Its photograph shows the Italianate formula executed in stone: three bays, every window round-headed beneath an elongated keystone, a belt course marking the principal floor, and an arched portico on columns crowning a high stoop. The stoop is essential to the design: a caller ascends well above the street, and above the dust of its carriages, before the door even opens. The house will stand until it is demolished around 1960. Walk one block south, where the same year's style acquires a famous architect.

== stop hibbard
1701 Prairie, William G. Hibbard's house, also from 1869, designed by William Le Baron Jenney for the president of a major wholesale hardware firm. The district register classifies it as Italianate: a low hipped roof on bracketed eaves above a stone-based body, with a rear wing added in 1874. Jenney would later engineer the first skyscraper; this house will be demolished in 1932. Two doors south stands something rarer on this street: an Italianate house constructed of wood.

== stop walker
1720 Prairie, the Tyrrell and Walker house, which the district register identifies as an Italianate house built of timber: a low hip on deep bracketed eaves, a central gable, a canted bay and a porch spanning the full width. The brick and stone fronts crowding around it reveal the direction the street ultimately took. In 1910 wreckers will make it the first of many Prairie Avenue houses demolished, as residences yield to offices, warehouses and factories. Compare its wooden brackets with the stone examples nearby. Cross Eighteenth Street to a house that updated its wardrobe.

== stop jones
1834 Prairie, Fernando Jones's house, designed by John M. Van Osdel and built in 1866 for "one of Chicago's earliest pioneers - Fernando Jones". It was originally Italianate. Two decades later the entire street had adopted mansards, so in 1886 Jones added a mansard "to bring it into fashion with many of its neighbors". Here, then, is an Italianate house wearing a newer hat, which it will keep until demolition in 1942. Prairie Avenue was a fiercely competitive street. One final stop lies past Twentieth.

== stop tolman
2031 Prairie, the northern house of a row of three built in 1869 with pale Lemont limestone fronts. The district register classifies it as Italianate: a flat roof concealed behind a bracketed cornice, above three stories. The three houses share a single cornice line, resembling one unified palazzo, yet each retains its own door. Look for where the brackets pause and resume.

== ending finish
The Italianate walk ends here. Count the brackets you passed: these are the avenue's oldest fronts, constructed before the fire and before the enormous fortunes that followed it.

By 1904 they already appear dated beside the mansards and the granite. Walk the Second Empire or Romanesque routes to discover what came next.
