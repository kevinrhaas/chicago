== opening
JAUNT: A LETTER HOME. You have been in Chicago a few days, and you owe the people you left behind a letter, and perhaps an apology for leaving. Mail is the town's lifeline. In winter, Moses and Kirkland say, 'There was nothing coming in, and there was no means of sending anything out of the town', and when the mail did arrive, 'often irregular and long delayed', the whole town gathered to hear it read aloud from 'a dry goods box'. A letter sent today may reach home in weeks. A reply may never find you.

Decide what your letter is for, find paper and ink, learn how the town handles its mail, and plan where to post it. Be honest in it, or be kind. You may not manage both.

== stop purpose
Begin at Mrs Rufus Brown's log boarding house, which Andreas places behind Peck's store. Sit down at the common table, if you can find a corner of it among the other lodgers, and decide first what the letter is for. A quick word that you arrived safely, which is what they want to hear. A long description of the town's prospects for anyone thinking of following you west, which may be more hope than fact. Or a page of questions that will need an answer to find you, wherever you are by then.

== stop paper
In front stands Peck's store; his printed cards name dry goods, hardware, groceries and crockery. For paper, though, the Chicago American of 20 June 1835 points elsewhere: Frederick Thomas's new drug store on Water Street advertised letter paper, wafers, wax, and black and red ink. A wafer or a drop of wax seals the folded sheet; nobody here uses envelopes yet. Write small: paper costs money.

== stop notice
At the corner where the Democrat began, read its notices; by May its office had moved over Jones and King's hardware store. On 27 May 1835 it printed a List of Letters remaining in the Chicago post office on 31 March: names nobody had called for. Every name is somebody's son or husband or sister who moved on, or never came, or did not live to ask, and somewhere a family is still waiting for an answer to a letter that sits unread in a box on South Water Street.

== stop mail-corner
End at the old mail corner. John S. C. Hogan was made postmaster in November 1832 and handed out the mail from 'a room partitioned off' in his log store, forty-five feet by eighteen, where Lake and South Water meet: the government's post office was a corner of a shopkeeper's store, and the postmaster sold you groceries while you waited. Andreas has the office here until about July 1834. This spring Hogan's card offers goods 'one door below the Post Office', and the Democrat still calls him Postmaster.

== ending safe-and-well
Your letter says you arrived safely and are well, which is true enough today. It is folded, sealed with a wafer and ready for the counter on South Water Street. What you left out, they will not miss.

== ending come-west
Your letter describes a busy, muddy, booming town and its prospects, a little brighter than you have seen them. It will be read aloud at home, and somebody will be on the next boat, and whatever they find here will be on your conscience.

== ending write-back
Your letter asks for news from home, and now you know what happens to a letter nobody calls for: a name in the paper, and silence. Give them an address that will last. It is ready to send.
