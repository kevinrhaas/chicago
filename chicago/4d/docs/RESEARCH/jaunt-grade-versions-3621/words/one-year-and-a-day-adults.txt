== opening
JAUNT: ONE YEAR AND A DAY. A Monday afternoon, 21 November 1904, at the Field houses on Prairie Avenue.

Four stops between two houses: the father's and the son's. Marshall Field built one of the great stores of America and the biggest fortune in Chicago, $125 million by next year. His only surviving son lives two doors away. He is 36, rich, married, a father of three, and he has spent his whole life waiting to become the man his father is, under a father who has no intention of letting him. Exactly one year and one day from now, a revolver will go off in the house at 1919, and the story of where and why will never be settled.

Start at the son's front door.

== stop son
1919 Prairie, the house of Mr. and Mrs. Marshall Field Jr. He bought it in 1890, and two years ago D. H. Burnham & Company built "a huge addition" for him: more room, more show. He married Albertine Huck, and they have three children: Henry, Marshall III and little Gwendolyn. Look at them on the front walk this afternoon, the nurse with the youngest, the boys in their coats. Everything a man could want is on this walk, except his own name, which belongs to his father. He has been Junior all his life, and in this family Junior is not a title anyone outgrows. Walk north, two doors up, to the house where the name lives.

== stop father
1905 Prairie, the father's house, where the son grew up. It was "the first in Chicago to be completely wired for electricity". There was a first son, Louis, who died a baby; this one lived, and was raised to inherit the store that taught America to "Give the lady what she wants". His father is "the wealthiest and most powerful businessman in Chicago", and he does not hand over power. His will is going to put the fortune in trust for forty years, for the grandsons playing on the walk at 1919, passing over his son entirely. The son walks up to his father's door for his Monday call. Benson the butler lets him in. The house will be razed in 1955.

== stop talk
Up the street, look back at the two houses. This is what the street sees: a dutiful son making his call. Here is what the street says, behind its curtains. The Levee is three blocks west, the vice district, and the Everleigh Club on Dearborn Street, a brothel, counts actors, authors and the richest young men in Chicago among its clients; the gossips name Marshall Field Jr. among them. Nobody has proved it, and nobody ever will. But a year and a day from now, the story they tell will be this: "The most popular story is that he was in fact shot at the Everleigh Club", and carried home to look like an accident. Others will say he shot himself on purpose. Go back to 1919.

== stop shot
1919 again, as the afternoon goes. The children have gone in. On Wednesday, 22 November 1905, one year and a day from this afternoon, Marshall Field Jr. will be shot in this house. He will live five days. At Mercy Hospital he will say, "I shot myself with that revolver – accidentally". The coroner's jury will agree that he "had shot himself unintentionally while examining a revolver". Two reporters will be tipped that he spent the evening at the Everleigh Club; the sisters will deny it, a rival madam will accuse them of murder, and nobody will confirm any of it. Look at the lit windows. The son of the richest man in Chicago is home for dinner, with one year and a day left.

== ending six-weeks
End of the walk. Marshall Field Jr. died at Mercy Hospital on Monday 27 November 1905, of what the Tribune called an accidentally self-inflicted gunshot wound. His father was 71 and had just remarried. On New Year's Day he went out to play golf, caught pneumonia, and "Six weeks later, Marshall Field, Sr. was dead." The fortune went in trust to the boys from the front walk at 1919, just as the will had planned. The son never got to be anything but Junior. Whatever happened that night, at home or on Dearborn Street, the official story was the gentle one.
