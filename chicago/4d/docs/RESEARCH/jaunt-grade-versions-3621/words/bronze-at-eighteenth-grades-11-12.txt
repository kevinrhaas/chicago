== premise
George Pullman installed a bronze commemorating the 1812 battle beside his own door; a year later the army returned to Chicago to suppress his striking employees. A walk about public memory, patronage, and who is entitled to cast history in metal.

== opening
JAUNT: THE BRONZE AT EIGHTEENTH STREET. A Monday afternoon in November 1904, at the intersection of Eighteenth Street and Prairie Avenue.

Four stops surrounding a single intersection. Beneath the pavement lies a battlefield; beside it stands a bronze offering one deliberately heroic interpretation of that battle; behind the bronze rises the mansion of the industrialist who financed it; and across the intersection looms the granite residence that confronted him daily. Each stop interrogates what actually occurred, and who possessed the authority to determine how it would be commemorated.

Begin where the avenue begins: on the contested ground of the battle itself.

== stop ground
Prairie Avenue at Eighteenth Street. Before the mansions, this was an undulating landscape of sand hills along the lakeshore. On 15 August 1812 the Battle of Fort Dearborn was fought across them, and the dead "lay in the sand, half buried where they were killed, until 1816". For more than a century an enormous cottonwood marked the site, although contemporary accounts disagree about its precise location. In 1900 a Tribune correspondent asserted that the Pullman residence "stands about where the massacre took place". Observe the avenue in 1904: brick, granite, gas illumination, and a schoolteacher escorting her pupils on an educational excursion. Ninety-two years after the bloodshed, the battle has been transformed into curriculum, which raises the essential historiographical question of who composed that curriculum. Walk east along Eighteenth Street to the bronze.

== caption ground
A teacher conducts a procession of schoolchildren along Eighteenth Street toward the monument.

== stop bronze
The monument. George Pullman commissioned it and installed it in 1893 near his own entrance: Carl Rohl-Smith's bronze depicting the Potawatomi chief Black Partridge rescuing Margaret Helm, an officer's wife. It is theatrical and ennobling, and it may be entirely apocryphal. "Some historians regard the incident as the product of romantic imagination instead of eyewitness testimony", contending that "Mrs. Helm rode her horse to the shore of Lake Michigan where she found refuge". The sculptor recruited his models at Fort Sheridan among survivors of Wounded Knee, whom he characterized as "Indians of the most untamed sort", and one historian concludes that the figures "may have owed more to Buffalo Bill Cody's Wild West Show than to actual research". The teacher recites the inscription aloud. Now consider the mansion this sculpture effectively guards.

== caption bronze
The teacher recites the inscription to her pupils at the base of the monument while the children contemplate the bronze.

== stop strike
1729 Prairie, the Pullman residence. The monument's patron manufactured railroad sleeping cars and constructed an entire company town south of the city, where his employees paid him rent. In 1894 he reduced their wages, but "he didn't reduce the rent in Pullman", and they struck. Here is the irony the bronze is constitutionally incapable of expressing: "a year after the statue's dedication", the Seventh Cavalry, veterans of Little Big Horn and Wounded Knee, "came to Chicago to quell the Pullman riots of 1894". A regiment from the frontier wars the monument romanticizes arrived to suppress a labor uprising against the very industrialist who had installed that romanticized battle at his doorstep. Pullman died of a heart attack in 1897, and the residence will be demolished in 1922. Cross Prairie to the house that confronted his.

== caption strike
Two laborers carrying tools pause on the opposite sidewalk to scrutinize the Pullman residence, then continue on their way.

== stop grave
The Glessner corner, diagonally opposite Pullman's entrance. Pullman resented this house intensely: "I do not know what I have ever done to have that thing staring me in the face every time I go out of my door". He spent his final years positioned between the bronze and the granite. Following his death, amid notorious grave robberies and anxieties that former employees still harbored vengeance, his family interred him in a "lead-lined mahogany coffin, which was then sealed inside a block of concrete", beneath steel rails "bolted together at right angles". The historian Adam Selzer acknowledges the limits of the evidence: "Whether it's true or not, we don't really know". That epistemological humility is this corner's lesson: bronze, granite and concrete, and beneath everything, the sand.

== ending sand
The walk concludes here. In 1904 the bronze continues to stand at Eighteenth Street, narrating its episode from a single, flattering perspective, although numerous historians question whether the rescue it immortalizes ever occurred. Pullman lies at Graceland beneath his concrete; the strike he suppressed occupies a permanent place in the history of American labor; and the ground beneath this intersection still contains the sand of 1812. A monument reliably reveals who financed it and what that patron wished posterity to remember. Everything else, the conscientious historian must excavate independently.
