== premise
17 June 1812, sunset, the fort gate: Chicago's first recorded murder. The victim was the interpreter Jean Lalime; the killer was John Kinzie, later celebrated as "The Father of Chicago". Nine stops.

== opening
JAUNT: CHICAGO'S FIRST MURDER. Chicago in 1812 is a small, crowded community: a garrison of about fifty soldiers under Captain Nathan Heald, a government trading house, and a few houses on the north bank. Everyone knows everyone, and the two men who will meet at the gate have been rivals for years, over a house, over the soldiers' trade, and over how to treat their neighbors as war with Britain approaches.

Nine stops: the killing first, then the story from 1804 to the night of the killing and the morning after, with the time of day and the season set for each. Three versions are told side by side: the Kinzie family's, repeated for a century; that of a girl who watched from inside the fort; and the letters the factor and the surgeon's mate sent to Washington within weeks.

The story begins at its end: outside the gate of Fort Dearborn, at sunset on 17 June 1812.

== stop cold-open
17 June 1812, sunset, outside the gate of Fort Dearborn. Jean Lalime, the fort's interpreter, lies dead in the dust. The man walking away, bleeding from the shoulder, is John Kinzie, the trader who supplies the garrison, whom later histories would call "The Father of Chicago". Who did it has never been seriously doubted; why, and whether it was murder, depends on whom you believe. To understand how it came to this, travel back eight years, across the river, to the finest house in Chicago.

== stop du-sable-house
May 1804, morning. This is the finest house in Chicago, built by Jean Baptiste Point du Sable, the trader who settled here first. In 1800 he sold out, and the deed recorded in Detroit names the buyer as Jean Lalime, a French interpreter and trader, using money from their shared employer, the fur trader William Burnett. The witness who signed it was John Kinzie, another of Burnett's traders. Now Kinzie moves his family in and claims it as his own, although no record of any sale from Lalime to Kinzie has ever been found. The family later insisted Kinzie bought the place from a "Le Mai", and historians long treated "Lalime" as a misspelling. Lalime did not leave; he moved a few doors west, close enough to watch Kinzie occupy his house.

== stop lalime-house
26 May 1811, afternoon. Lalime had wintered in the big house in 1803 and 1804 with the fort's first surgeon, who called him "a very decent man and a good companion". When Kinzie took possession, Lalime moved here, to a smaller house west along the bank, and became the fort's interpreter, the man who speaks for the garrison with its neighbors. Today he writes to Governor William Clark at St. Louis that "An Indian from the Peorias passed here yesterday" with word of horses stolen from the southern settlements. His letters reveal, Kirkland wrote, "a man of ability and education". Across the river, a new arrangement is about to make Kinzie richer, and Lalime his enemy.

== stop factory
10 March 1812, morning, at the factory, the government's own trading house, operated at cost by the factor, Matthew Irwin. The sutler is something different: a private merchant licensed to sell to the soldiers. In January Captain Heald made "Messrs. Kinzie & Forsyth", Kinzie's trading firm, the garrison's exclusive sutlers, and today Irwin informs Washington that the pair had offered someone "400$ per annum" to secure them the post. Two men side with Irwin: the young surgeon's mate, Isaac Van Voorhis, and the interpreter, Lalime. Kinzie, an older officer once wrote, was "a man of ungovernable temper". Within weeks, a dispute over money escalates into talk of killing.

== stop bad-blood
16 April 1812, the officers' barracks. Ten days ago men "supposed to be Winnebagoes" killed two farmhands at Hardscrabble, up the South Branch, and fear has turned against everyone. Irwin writes today that "Propositions were openly made to murder" neighbors who had always been peaceable, French settlers among them, and he identifies who pressed hardest: "the Suttler for this garrison & his Son-in law—Lt Helm", meaning Kinzie and Lieutenant Linai Helm, the fort's second officer, married to Kinzie's stepdaughter. Ensign George Ronan, the youngest officer, threatens to shoot Lalime, and Helm swears violent revenge against the factor. In May, Heald arrests Helm for plotting to kill six Sauk men who had come in peace, then releases him. By June everyone at the fort has chosen a side.

== stop the-quarrel
17 June 1812, about six in the evening, inside the fort. Tomorrow, in Washington, Congress will declare war on Britain, although the news will not reach this fort until the middle of July. On the parade Helm denounces the factor, concluding, as Van Voorhis wrote, "by saying he would give a very handsome treat if the Factory would be done away!" Kinzie supports his son-in-law. Lalime stands up for Irwin's store: he "defended the pure motives and just dealings of the Institution". Voices rise. Then Kinzie and Lalime walk out of the gate together, just before the gates close for the night, and only one of the two men will walk back.

== stop the-gate
17 June, sunset, back outside the main gate. Victoire Porthier, a girl inside the fort, remembered: "It was sunset, when they used to shut the gates of the fort". She heard Helm call to Kinzie to look out for Lalime, "as he had a pistol". "We heard the pistol go off and saw the smoke. Then they fell down together". Kinzie rose, wounded in the shoulder; Lalime did not. Irwin, the factor, witnessed everything from twenty or thirty yards away, and Van Voorhis wrote that "it was a perfect assassination and can be proven to be such". The Kinzie family called it self-defense. A man lies dead at the gate, the killer is the commander's sutler and the lieutenant's father-in-law, and the officers must decide what to do with him.

== stop the-flight
17 June, about 11:20 at night. According to Irwin, Ensign Ronan told Kinzie to flee, and Helm concealed him in the garrison for about four hours, then walked him to the river's edge, shook his hand and released him. According to the family, Kinzie "returned to the house covered with blood", and his wife took him into the woods, where "she dressed his wounds" and kept him hidden for days. According to Porthier, "In the night he packed up some things and my father took him to Milwaukee". These are three versions of a single night, while at the gate, Lalime's body still lies where it fell.

== stop the-grave
18 June, morning. The townspeople want an inquest, a public inquiry into the death, but Irwin wrote that instead "a file of soldiers headed by Lt. Helm carried it away by force". Lalime was buried on this bank, about "two hundred yards west of the Kinzie house", inside a picket fence, in plain view of the house he had once owned on paper. In 1891 workers digging a cellar near Cass and Illinois streets discovered a skeleton in "a rude pine coffin", and the bones were transferred to the Chicago Historical Society.

== ending verdict
That night Heald ordered the factor to help arrest Kinzie. Irwin refused and was expelled from the garrison; Heald's own letter explains why: "a Citizen who had but a few days previous stabed an other citizen to the heart in your presence". Irwin departed on 5 July and was captured at Mackinac. Van Voorhis died on the lake shore on 15 August. Kinzie survived, returned in 1816, and his family told the story for a century.

So was it murder? Historians still disagree, and sharply.

THE CASE AGAINST KINZIE. Irwin, writing after the fall of the fort, accused him of helping bring on the disaster of 15 August, and so "destroying the Witnesses to the murder of Lalime". Milo Quaife, who discovered Irwin's letters, warned in 1915 that readers who saw Kinzie "as a kind of demigod" would find that charge "a distinct shock". The Chicago Reader in 2018 called the self-defense story "a popular lie".

THE CASE FOR KINZIE. In the family's telling, Lalime, "insanely jealous over Kinzie's success as a trader, treacherously attacked him". Currey (1912) reports an officers' inquiry reaching "a verdict of justifiable homicide". Porthier: "You see Kinzie wasn't to blame at all". And then: "That is what they all said".

WHO TOLD IT. Quaife observed that only the version "originating with the relatives and friends of the slayer gained the public ear". Lalime's bones remain in a box in the Chicago History Museum's storage. You have stood where he fell. Decide for yourself.
