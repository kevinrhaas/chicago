== opening
JAUNT: THE SCANDAL AT 1612 PRAIRIE. A Monday afternoon in November 1904, at the quiet north end of Prairie Avenue.

Four stops along one short block. The house at 1612 has been a showplace for twenty years. This year it is a battlefield. A husband has sued his wife for divorce on grounds of drink and worse, a judge has handed her the house anyway, and in November the husband went back to court with fresh charges that the whole city has now read in the Tribune. On a street that lives on its reputation, nothing travels faster than somebody else's. The neighbours keep their curtains closed and their ears open.

Start next door, where you can see the house the way it used to be.

== stop showplace
1608 Prairie, and beside it the big square house at 1612. Twenty years ago it belonged to a Studebaker, of the Studebaker Brothers carriage works, and it was the brightest house on the street. In February 1884 some two hundred guests came here from a family wedding, and "The large residence was brilliantly illuminated, and every room was elaborately decorated with smilax and cut flowers." In 1887 a society column watched the door: "Morning and night, a showy turnout is noticed before the squarely-built house, with the number 1612 over the portals." This street notices who comes and goes. It is noticing now. No turnout stands at the curb this afternoon, and a maid from next door is watching from her steps. Walk to the gate of 1612.

== stop decree
1612 Prairie. The Tribune has the story, and so does everybody else. Last May the husband, Charles E. Brown, filed a bill for divorce against his wife Catherine, 27, "the daughter of Gen. Graves, a wealthy resident of Buffalo". The grounds were "Habitual drunkenness and statutory charges", and the papers leave the rest to the reader's imagination. Then Judge Tuley gave Mrs. Brown possession of this house, where she and her husband and their son had lived, and $200 a month while the suit was pending. So the accused wife holds the house, and the husband who accused her is outside it, paying. Behind those windows live a woman the papers have made notorious and a small boy. The house will stand until it is razed circa 1960. Step next door.

== stop charges
1616 Prairie, the next door south, where the neighbours read the Tribune with their coffee. In November Mr. Brown went back to court and charged that his wife "had conducted herself in a disgraceful manner, had been habitually intoxicated, and had done many freakish things, such as playing a violin in a broken automobile while a man pushed the machine along a public highway, and dancing about the rear of their home clothed only in a nightrobe." Then he named the man: "E. C. Edsall, an automobile salesman." With Edsall, the husband charged, she ran off to an eastern summer resort and later to New York City. A general's daughter and a car salesman: the avenue has not had a better scandal in years. But a divorce is not only about two people. Look for the boy.

== stop boy
Across the street at 1613, look back at the west side of the block. The boy is Charles Edward Brown, the couple's only son, and in a fight like this one a child is something to be won. A nurse walks him along the sidewalk past his own front door. His mother has the house; his father wants it back, and wants him with it. The court has not decided. In the newspapers he is a line at the end of every story. On this walk he is the only person in the whole affair who has done nothing at all. The end will come early in 1905, and it will not be kind to his mother.

== ending march
End of the walk. After a bitter fight, early in 1905, the husband won everything: the divorce, the house on Prairie Avenue and the custody of the boy. On 8 March 1905 he sold the house to Charles Palmerston Anderson, the Episcopal bishop of Chicago.

That same month Catherine Brown was arrested at 3667 Wabash Avenue, where, according to the police, she had been living under the name of Kittie Casey, and fined $85 for disorderly conduct. "I am penniless," she said. "I cannot pay the fine." In her cell: "It's all because a woman is jealous of me, I guess." Justice Hurley called her "a problem in humanity to me." The general's daughter from Buffalo had gone from a Prairie Avenue mansion to a police cell in less than a year.
