== opening
JAUNT: GOSSIP OR PRINTED NOTICE? Morning at the forks, and two rumours are already going round: the land sale took in a million dollars, and a bank is opening in a brick house on the square. In a town drunk on land, a rumour is worth money to whoever hears it first, and men buy and sell on less. The mail comes 'often irregular and long delayed'. When it does come, 'some one with a good voice would mount a dry goods box' and read the news aloud, and 'If politics ran high, two dry-goods boxes and two readers of opposite parties would be utilized.'

This summer there are two newspapers in town. Carry the rumours to both printing offices and read what the papers actually said before you repeat a word, or bet on one.

== stop the-forks
Start at the forks, under the Wolf Point Tavern's painted wolf sign, hung by about 1833. One account has William Walters keeping the house until 1836; another says it stopped taking guests in 1834, a lesson in how far to trust any one account here. At the door the talk is all money: a million dollars taken at the land sale, and a bank opening on the square within weeks. The land office opened in May and 'added fuel to the already brilliant blaze of excitement'; nobody in town talks about anything else, and a rumour that lifts prices suits anyone with a lot to sell.

== stop the-exchange
Over the river to the Exchange Coffee House, which Mark Beaubien built at Lake and Wells and Mr. and Mrs. Murphy have kept since August 1834. The rumours have beaten you here, and they have grown on the way, as rumours do when men have been drinking and money is involved. Somebody at the door wants to know whether they are true, and perhaps whether to buy before noon.

== stop the-democrat
At South Water and Clark, the Democrat's first office; by 20 May it printed over Jones & King's hardware. Today's number admits it cannot say what the Land Office took, and is only 'led to believe' it was between five and some hundred thousand dollars; the surviving copy loses the second figure. Even the newspaper is guessing, in print, with authority. Either way, it is not a million, and a man who bought on the million is already overpaying.

== stop the-american
Near the drawbridge, T. O. Davis's American is three weeks old. Its 27 June number reprints the Sangamon Journal: the State Bank's directors have decided on a branch at Chicago, but its officers are not yet known. The brick building on the square near the court house is Springfield's, not Chicago's. Half a rumour is true, which is the most dangerous kind: it is how sensible men are talked into foolish bargains, and how the men doing the talking get rich.

== ending true-to-the-page
You carry back both items as printed: a land-sale take the Democrat would only guess at, and a branch bank decided with no officers named and no house in Chicago yet. Less than the forks said, and true to the page. Before the year is out the census will count one bank in town, 'a branch of the State bank'. Nobody thanks you for the correction.

== ending one-checked
You carry back the bank item as printed and leave the land sale alone, because you did not note it. Half a correction, honestly made, in a town that prefers the rumour.

== ending not-yet-known
You carry back that nothing is settled: one paper would only guess, and the other names no officer and no house. Uncertainty is an answer too, though it sells no lots.

== ending owed-an-answer
You promised an answer at the Exchange and carry none back. The rumours will be repeated there without you, a little larger each time, until Saturday's American or next week's Democrat prints more.

== ending held-tongue
You say nothing, and the rumours go round without you. Saturday's American and next week's Democrat will print more; until then, holding your tongue is a fair way to read the news, and a cheap way to keep your money.
