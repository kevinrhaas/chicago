== premise
From 6 April to 16 August 1812 in seventeen stops: the fort, the killings at Hardscrabble, the order to leave, the march down the beach, the surrender, and the houses where the Kinzies and Mrs. Helm hid.

== opening
JAUNT: THE BATTLE OF FORT DEARBORN. Spring 1812, where the Chicago River meets Lake Michigan, and the nearest American post is Fort Wayne, far to the south-east, while Detroit, where the general commands, is farther still. War with Britain is approaching, and every communication that reaches the fort makes it seem smaller and more isolated from assistance.

Seventeen stops in chronological order, with the time of day and the season established for each.

The narrative commences with the fort itself, on the afternoon of 6 April 1812.

== stop the-fort
6 April 1812, afternoon, before the fort, where this story begins. In the summer of 1803 Captain John Whistler's company marched in from Detroit and constructed it of logs: barracks surrounding a modest parade, 'Two blockhouses' and 'a double row of palisades'. It has stood for nine years, and since 1810 it has been commanded by Captain Nathan Heald, with fifty-four regular soldiers. Across the river resides John Kinzie, the trader who supplies the garrison, together with his family and his clerks; his stepdaughter Margaret is married to the fort's lieutenant, Linai Helm. A handful of other families occupy the banks: the Ouilmettes, the Burnses, and the hands at Charles Lee's farm. This precarious equilibrium is about to collapse three miles upriver, shortly before sunset.

== stop hardscrabble
6 April 1812, a little before sunset. Three miles up the South Branch lies a farm called Lee's Place, subsequently known as Hardscrabble. Charles Lee, who 'did not live on the premises, but occupied a house near the fort', owns it, and it grows food the garrison purchases. Inside the log house are Liberty White, its manager, a discharged soldier, a Canadian laborer and Lee's young son. Eleven armed men walk in and sit down, men whom Captain Heald described ten days later as 'supposed to be Winnebagoes'. Apprehending danger, the soldier 'pointed to the cattle which were standing among the haystacks on the opposite bank, and made signs that they must go and fodder them'; he and the boy descend to the canoes and paddle across.

== stop the-haystacks
6 April, sunset, across the river from Lee's Place. Concealed behind the haystacks, the soldier and the boy abandon all pretence of foddering: 'they took to the woods' and make for the fort, three miles distant. Behind them two shots ring out, and in the cabin White and the Canadian are killed. The fugitives hasten downriver through the deepening dusk, while a family with a day-old infant remains entirely unaware of what approaches.

== stop burns
6 April, dusk. Racing downriver toward the fort, the two pause opposite this house, where the Burns family live, and shout an urgent warning across the water. At the fort Ensign George Ronan, 'a young cadet direct from West Point', takes five or six soldiers upriver in a flat-bottomed scow and brings Mrs. Burns out, 'with her infant of scarcely a day old, upon her bed to the boat'. A corporal and six men are still upriver fishing, so 'the commanding officer ordered a cannon to be fired'. Returning through the darkness past the silent farm, they discover a scalped body by touch and regain the gate about eleven at night. Heald enrolls fifteen townsmen as militia, and for four months the settlement waits anxiously behind its artillery, until the decisive blow arrives as correspondence.

== stop the-order
10 August 1812, morning, on the parade. The United States has been at war with Britain since June, and the British have captured Mackinac, the American post farther up the lake. General William Hull, commanding at Detroit, sends a runner, Winnemac, with a written order for Captain Heald: 'It is with regret I order the Evacuation of your Post owing to the want of Provisions'. The arms are to be destroyed and the government store's goods distributed. Wau-Bun says the order 'was read next morning upon parade', and that the officers' 'unanimous advice was, to remain where they were, and fortify themselves as strongly as possible.' Heald nevertheless elects to obey, while outside the pickets hundreds of people, already informed, are assembling to discover what Heald will promise them.

== stop council
12 August, afternoon, on this open ground outside the pickets. Heald wrote that the Potawatomi of the country round 'came in from all quarters in order to receive the goods in the factory store'. He meets their leaders in council here and promises them the goods, but his own officers refuse to attend: 'Captain Heald alone attended on the part of the military.' Instead they occupy the blockhouses overlooking this esplanade, open the port-holes and point the cannon 'so as to command the whole assembly.' It is a negotiation conducted beneath the artillery, and although he has promised the goods, he has not disclosed his intentions regarding the firearms and the whiskey.

== stop factory
13 August, early afternoon. From this log building, the factory or government trading house, now goes everything on the shelves: 'blankets, broadcloths, calicoes, paints'. Heald adds 'a considerable quantity of provisions which we could not take away with us.' Hull's order specified that the goods could be distributed to those 'desirous of Escorting you on to Fort Wayne'. Whatever Heald withholds, the firearms, the powder and the whiskey, matters most to every observer, and that night, within the walls, Heald undertakes something he hopes will escape notice.

== stop covered-way
13 August, evening, observed from above, where a covered way descends from the fort's north-east corner to the river. 'The same evening the ammunition and liquor were carried, part into the sally-port, and thrown into a well'. Muskets are dismantled and deposited afterward. The rest of the liquor goes 'as secretly as possible through the northern gate, the heads of the barrels knocked in, and the contents poured into the river.' Heald apprehended that the assembled warriors 'would make bad use of it'. The operation is scarcely clandestine: the knocking carries, and 'the taste of the water the next morning was, as one expressed it, "strong grog".' By morning the river tastes of whiskey, and a rider from Fort Wayne is approaching the gate.

== stop the-gate
14 August, sunset, at the main gate, where Captain William Wells rides in from Fort Wayne with a Miami escort, Heald says 'about 30 Miamies'. Wells, Rebekah Heald's uncle, has come to escort her and her husband to safety, but Wau-Bun says 'all too late', for the ammunition has already been destroyed. His niece and Heald conduct him to Heald's quarters, and at nine tomorrow the gate will open and the entire garrison will depart through it.

== stop the-medal
14 August, evening, at Heald's quarters on the parade. Black Partridge, 'a Pottawatomie chief from the Illinois River', has come to the fort; he 'entered the quarters of the commanding officer' and surrenders his peace medal: 'I will not wear a token of peace while I am compelled to act as an enemy.' Helm and Griffith both record his warning, and 'Helm alone mentions the surrender of the medal.' It constitutes the most explicit warning anyone delivers, and at nine tomorrow the column departs.

== stop river-mouth
15 August 1812, nine in the morning. 'On the 15th, at 9 o'clock in the morning, we commenced our march', Heald wrote. Out of the gate, down to the river's mouth here, and south along the beach: Wells and part of the Miami escort in front, then the fifty-four regulars, the wagons with the women and children, and the twelve townsmen of the militia. Wau-Bun says the band played the Dead March, and that Wells 'had blackened his face before leaving the garrison, in token of his impending fate.' The Kinzie family's boat, forewarned, waits at this river mouth, while a mile and a half ahead, concealed behind the sand bank, warriors are waiting.

== stop the-bank
About ten in the morning, two miles down the beach, with 'the lake on our left, and a high sand bank on our right'. 'We had proceeded about a mile and a half,' Heald wrote, 'when it was discovered that the Indians were prepared to attack us from behind the bank.' His own journal says 'two miles from the Fort', and the Kinzies later pointed to two cottonwoods here, 'near the place where the attack began'. The soldiers charge up the bank, while behind them, at the wagons, Quaife writes, 'a deadlier combat, which we may perhaps think of as a separate battle,' is raging. The militia are 'cut down to a man'. Among the dead are 'Cicely, Mrs. Heald's negro serving-woman' and 'her infant son', two of the few people recorded as held in slavery at Chicago. Heald retains only a few dozen men capable of fighting.

== stop cottonwoods
About half past ten. Heald gathers his last men on 'a small elevation in the open prarie', out of shot of the bank; the Kinzies remembered that 'the combatants moved south and west over considerable space'. A Potawatomi leader called Black Bird comes out, shakes his hand and asks him to surrender, 'promising to spare the lives of all the prisoners.' Heald consents, and his tally reads: 'twenty-six regulars and all the militia were killed in the action, with two women and twelve children.' Among them are Ensign Ronan, the young surgeon's mate Isaac Van Voorhis, and Captain Wells. Twenty-three years later a boy named Fernando Jones was shown this ground by people 'who were children at the time'. The survivors are conducted northward, although at the Kinzie house the danger has not concluded.

== stop kinzie-house
15 August, sunset, at the house of John Kinzie, the trader who supplied the garrison, whose family is spared and brought home under guard: 'At their own mansion the family of Mr. Kinzie were closely guarded by their Indian friends.' Mrs. Heald, badly wounded, is ransomed by Kinzie's clerk for a mule, and Kinzie takes a ball from her arm 'with his penknife.' The gate of the green stands open; across the river the fort lies deserted, and in the morning it will be burned.

== stop the-burning
16 August, the following morning, before the main gate, through which the garrison marched yesterday, never to return. All night the empty fort has been stripped and the plunder shared out: 'shawls, ribbons, and feathers fluttered about in all directions', and one young man struts in 'a muslin gown and the bonnet of one of the ladies'. Now young men arrive from the encampments carrying brands from their fires. 'The following morning, the work of plunder being completed, the Indians set fire to the fort.' Barracks, blockhouses and the double row of pickets, nine summers of Whistler's squared logs, ignite successively. Heald's report gives it one line: 'The next morning, they set fire to the fort and left the place, taking the prisoners with them.' The little brick magazine 'alone defied the fire'. Take a brand and ignite the logs yourself.

== stop war-party
16 August, morning, back at the Kinzie house, while the fort burns across the river. Black Partridge, Waubansee and Keepotah stand sentinel on the porch. Presently a party from the Wabash, who have 'blackened their faces', enter the parlor and 'seated themselves upon the floor in silence'. Billy Caldwell, the Sauganash, 'entered the parlor with a calm step' and greets them as friends, and, 'thus taken by surprise', they profess to have come only for 'some white cotton in which to wrap their dead before interring them.' Several doors westward, a wounded woman is concealed, while searchers methodically examine the houses.

== stop ouilmette
16 August, morning, while the fort is burning. Margaret Helm, Kinzie's stepdaughter and Lieutenant Helm's wife, wounded at the wagons, has been brought here to Ouilmette's house by Black Partridge in 'the ordinary dress of a Frenchwoman of the country'. When searchers come, the family 'raised a large feather bed and placed her under the edge of it, upon the bedstead, with her face to the wall', and Mrs. Bisson sits deliberately sewing on the front of the bed. 'I can but die,' said she; 'let them put an end to my misery at once.' The searchers look around 'and left the house.' Quaife questions the romantic embellishment, though not the authenticity of the rescue itself.

== leg 0
Up the South Branch, shortly before sunset, eleven armed men approach an isolated farmhouse.

== leg 1
The two canoes come ashore beneath the haystacks on the opposite bank.

== leg 2
The soldier and the boy flee desperately downriver through the gathering dusk.

== leg 3
War with Britain is formally declared in June, and in August a messenger arrives from Detroit carrying instructions.

== leg 4
Out through the gate to the unenclosed ground immediately west of the stockade, where the council will convene.

== leg 5
Westward past the agency house toward the government's trading establishment.

== leg 6
Around to the river side of the fortification, where the covered way descends toward the water.

== leg 7
Back around the stockade to the principal gate on its southern elevation.

== leg 8
Through the open gate and across the parade toward the commanding officer's quarters.

== leg 9
Down along the riverbank, following the identical route the departing column marched.

== leg 10
Southward along the shoreline, precisely as Heald described it, with 'the lake on our left, and a high sand bank on our right'.

== leg 11
Retreating from the bank across the unprotected prairie, toward a position 'out of shot of the bank or any other cover'.

== leg 12
The prisoners are escorted back toward the fort, and the Kinzies are returned to their own residence.

== leg 13
Morning over the river, with the Kinzies confined to their residence under protective guard.

== leg 14
Behind you the fort is burning; Black Partridge and his companions guard the Kinzies' porch.

== leg 15
Several doors westward along the bank, to Antoine Ouilmette's house, toward which the searchers are heading.

== ending burning
Still 16 August 1812, and the fort you watched them ignite is burning to the ground. Heald's report concludes the account there: they 'set fire to the fort and left the place, taking the prisoners with them.' Only the brick magazine 'alone defied the fire'. On the third day after the battle, the Kinzies were placed in a boat for St. Joseph, where Heald and his wife were also taken, and the scattered prisoners were held in the villages 'until the following spring, when they were, for the most part, carried to Detroit and ransomed'. The army returned in 1816 and constructed the second Fort Dearborn on the identical ground. Quaife's caution regarding Wau-Bun, the family's memoir that shaped this narrative for a century: its accounts do Heald 'grave injustice'.
