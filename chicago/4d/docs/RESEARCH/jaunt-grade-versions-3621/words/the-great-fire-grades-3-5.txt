== premise
Sunday night, 8 October 1871. A fire starts in a barn and burns across Chicago for a night and a day. Follow it, stop by stop and hour by hour, until the rain comes.

== opening
JAUNT: THE GREAT CHICAGO FIRE. It is Sunday night, 8 October 1871. The summer has been very dry. Since July almost no rain has come. Most houses on the West Side are made of wood. A strong wind blows from the south-west. The firemen are tired. Last night they fought a big fire, and they worked until this afternoon.

There are twelve stops. You will follow the fire hour by hour, over two rivers, all the way to the north end of the city. Long rides go Instantly. Every place keeps its stop.

It starts in a barn on DeKoven Street, at about nine o'clock.

== stop oleary-barn
It is about nine at night on DeKoven Street. Behind this little house is a barn. It belongs to Patrick and Catherine O'Leary. They keep cows and sell the milk to their neighbours. The loft is full of new hay. According to legend, Mrs. O'Leary was milking, and her cow kicked over a lamp. But the O'Learys said they were in bed, and no one could prove that anyone was in the barn that night. Daniel Sullivan, a neighbour who drives a cart, saw the flames from across the street. He has a wooden leg, but he ran to the barn anyway. He pulled out a calf. Another neighbour, William Lee, ran to the drug store to ring the fire alarm. The store keeper would not give him the key. Far away, a watchman in a tall tower has not seen the fire yet.

== caption oleary-barn
Neighbours come out into the street to watch the barn burn. Families carry things out of the houses behind it.

== stop st-pauls
It is half past ten. The fire alarm came late, and it sent the fire engines to the wrong place. The watchman in the Court House tower saw the glow at 9:28. He called a fire box almost a mile away from the fire. One engine, the Little Giant, fought the fire alone for more than half an hour. Then the wind picked up big pieces of fire and threw them over the firemen. A man on a pile of wood saw one fly more than three blocks. It landed on the top of this church, St. Paul's. Now the church has fallen. Behind it are the mills of William Bateham, with huge piles of dry wood. They are burning too. Mr. Bateham says this fire will reach the South Side, and nobody can stop it now.

== caption st-pauls
Families hurry north with bundles and a mattress. A few men stop at the corner to watch the mills burn.

== stop parmelee
It is half past eleven, on Jackson Street. You have crossed the river. The South Branch of the Chicago River did not stop the fire. Sparks and burning bits of wood have been falling on this side for two hours. Now one has caught. This is a brand new stable for horses and buses. It cost $80,000. It is the best stable in the whole country, and it was going to open on Wednesday. The men at the stable had to run for their lives. By midnight the gas works are burning, and the little wooden houses next to them. Past them are the big stone buildings of LaSalle Street. People call them fireproof. Will they stand? Next comes the heart of the city.

== caption parmelee
Men run from the burning stable. Others stand at the corner and look at the red sky.

== stop court-house
It is half past one on Monday morning. This is the Court House, in the middle of the city. Mathias Schaffer, the watchman, is still up in the tower. Sparks have been landing on the roof for hours. He has put them out again and again. In the basement, the mayor, Roswell Mason, is giving orders. He tells men to blow up buildings to stop the fire. The jail is under the Court House. A police captain opens the doors, and the prisoners run free into the street. Now the tower is on fire. The watchmen ring the huge bell one more time, and run down the stairs. The bell weighs more than ten thousand pounds. Listen. It is still ringing.

== caption court-house
Freed prisoners run out of the square. Men stand back across the street to watch the tower burn. A runaway team runs past, and people jump out of its way. A wagon goes by with its load on fire.

== stop sherman-house
It is five past two, at the corner of Clark and Randolph. Behind you, the great Court House bell crashes down. The mayor stayed until it fell. Now he comes out, and the street next door is like a furnace. Across Clark Street is the Sherman House, a big hotel. It has seven floors and more than three hundred guests. For almost an hour the hotel held. Then flames burst from a window on the third floor. Only two hotel workers are left to wake everyone up. Five ladies are sick in bed. North of the hotel is the main river. It is the last thing between the fire and the North Side. The wind is carrying the fire over it right now.

== caption sherman-house
Hotel guests come out with bags and hurry away. A few stop on the corner and look back. A wagon goes up the street with its load on fire.

== stop crosbys
It is twenty past two, on Washington Street. The fire has turned east. This is Crosby's Opera House, a beautiful theatre with seats for 2,500 people. All summer it was closed so that workers could make it even more beautiful. Its owner spent $80,000 on new decorations and paintings. A famous orchestra was going to play here tomorrow night. Tonight, just an hour or two before the fire, all its gas lamps were lit for the first time, to see how lovely it looked. Now its roof catches fire. In a few minutes the grand theatre is gone. The hotel next door falls with it. And across the river, the North Side is burning.

== caption crosbys
People from the hotels and stores hurry east toward the lake. Two men stand in the road and watch the roof.

== stop north-side
It is half past two, north of the main river. The river did not stop the fire. The wind carried it over the water, and it started just north of the State Street bridge. Now a big stable on this corner is burning. An hour ago a judge, Lambert Tree, walked over the bridge while it was burning in spots. The mayor and his son are trying to get home to the North Side. They will find the fire here before them. North of this corner, most people are asleep. Many will wake up to find fire all around them. Half a mile away, on the lake shore, is the one building that pumps water for the whole city.

== caption north-side
People woken by the glow come out half dressed. Some start north up State Street, carrying what they can.

== stop water-works
It is twenty past three, at the lake shore. These are the Water Works. Big engines pump lake water up a tall stone tower and out to the whole city. The engineer, Frank Trautman, has the pumps running hard. A worker named D. W. Fuller sees a long piece of burning wood fly through the air. It is about twelve feet long! It hits the corner of the engine house. In an instant the roof is on fire, because the roof is made of wood. Mr. Fuller looks at his watch. It is twenty minutes past three. Before four o'clock the workers have to leave. The stone tower will stand, but the engines will stop. Now the firemen can only get water from the river.

== caption water-works
Workers come out to watch the engine house roof burn. Families walk by toward the lake.

== stop tribune
It is half past seven on Monday morning. The sun is up behind the smoke. On this corner is the Tribune building, home of a big newspaper. Its owners believe it is fireproof. At dawn William Bross, one of the owners, rode here and found it safe. Fire crept under the sidewalk into the barber shop in the basement. Mr. Bross and the other owners, Joseph Medill and Horace White, put it out with water and a fire extinguisher. They even got ready to print the morning paper. Then the fire came back from the south. It burned the theatre next door, just across a narrow alley. The iron shutters bent in the heat. The Tribune burns. And without the Water Works, nothing can stop what comes next.

== caption tribune
Printers and clerks come out with armfuls of papers and hurry away. A few men stand on the burnt corner across the street.

== stop state-street
It is eight o'clock, on State Street. This big store is made of white marble. Potter Palmer owns it. He rents it to a store company called Field, Leiter and Co. At first light their workers sprayed the whole building with water from their own pumps. Now the water has stopped, because the Water Works are gone. Men are carrying cloth and goods out of the store into wagons. The fire comes at the store from behind, and soon the store gives way. Down the street, the Palmer House hotel is already gone. On the North Side, the fire has been burning since half past two, and it has not stopped.

== caption state-street
Wagons wait by the store while men carry out rolls of cloth and hurry them away up State Street.

== stop ogden-house
It is noon on Monday, on the North Side. This is the house of Mahlon Ogden. His brother, William Ogden, was the first mayor of Chicago. The house is made of wood, and it is still standing! In front of it is a small park. In the night, families brought their furniture here to keep it safe. Then the fire came through the park and burned it all. A few men stayed inside the house. They pulled up the carpets and hung them over the walls. They kept the carpets wet. When the water ran out, they used barrels of cider! Now this house is the only building left for a long way around. A mile north, the fire is heading for St. Michael's church. Its steeple will burn. People are running for the lake and the old cemetery.

== caption ogden-house
The men who saved the house stand in the burnt park and look at it. A family looks at what is left of their furniture.

== stop old-cemetery
It is evening on Monday, at the old City Cemetery, near the edge of the city. Long ago most of the graves were moved. Only some old headstones are left, in the grass under small oak trees. By nightfall there are about thirty thousand people here! Men, women and children have come with beds, chairs and bundles. Many have watched their things burn. Along the lake shore, rich and poor sit side by side on the sand. Some ladies have even carried chairs into the water. They sit up high on the chair backs, with their feet on the seats. Thousands more have gone west to the open prairie. Everyone is tired, hungry and thirsty, and the smoke is everywhere. When will the fire stop?

== caption old-cemetery
Families sit among the old graves with their bundles and chairs. Others are still coming in from the burning streets.

== ending the-rain
It is Monday night, 9 October 1871. At about half past ten, the fire reaches the north end of the city. It burns one last house, on Fullerton Avenue, about four miles from the O'Leary barn. Then it burns itself out. At about eleven o'clock, rain begins to fall. It pours down. In the cemetery and on the prairie, the people stand in it, soaking wet. The fire burned for about twenty-five hours. It burned 17,450 buildings, and 98,500 people lost their homes. More than 250 people died. The mayor promised help for everyone who was hurt and hungry. He wrote that the fire was over, and that all would soon be well.

== keepsake text
Mayor Mason's first notice after the fire, as two Chicago newspapermen printed it that autumn: public order will be kept.
