== premise
Be the new helper at the town's newspaper on the day it comes out, 1 July 1835. Pick where the papers go first and where you stop to look, and bring your own story back to the shop.

== opening
JAUNT: APPRENTICE AT THE DEMOCRAT. It is Wednesday, 1 July 1835, and you are thirteen. In May the town's newspaper, the Chicago Democrat, put a notice in the paper. It said: 'Wanted immediately, an apprentice at this office to the printing business'. An apprentice is a young helper who learns a job by doing it. You asked for the job, and you got it. Now you sweep the floor, put ink on the letters and fold the big sheets. On Wednesdays the paper comes out, and you carry it all over town.

Today's paper is folded under your arm. You pick where it goes first, and where you stop to look. The places and the papers are real. The helper is you.

== stop old-corner
This is the corner of South Water and Clark streets. John Calhoun printed the very first Democrat here, on 26 November 1833. The shop has moved since then. By May it was upstairs over a hardware store, a little way down South Water Street. That is where you work now. The paper comes out every Wednesday. And now it has a rival! Since 8 June the town has a second paper, the Chicago American. It comes out on Saturday mornings.

== narrative old-corner
Go west to the river, or east to the rival?

== label old-corner west-first
West to the Sauganash

== consequence old-corner west-first
The river end of town gets the paper first.

== label old-corner east-first
Peek at the other paper

== consequence old-corner east-first
You will look at their paper before anyone sees yours.

== stop sauganash
Go west along the river to the Sauganash, where the two parts of the river meet. It is a hotel. A woman named Juliette Kinzie remembered it as a white building, two stories tall, with bright blue wooden shutters. Your own paper visited it this June. It said the hotel had been fixed up and furnished very neatly. Mark Beaubien, the town's best fiddle player, is part of its story. Long ago, before the town had a paper, news came in the mail. Someone stood on a box and read it out loud. Now the news comes under your arm.

== narrative sauganash
This is where travelers read the news.

== stop rival
Go east along South Water Street, near the bridge. Here is the office of the Chicago American. A man named T. O. Davis started it on 8 June, so it is only three weeks old. Its last paper had an ad from a drug seller. He was selling a medicine and promised it would stop a terrible sickness called cholera. Your paper wrote about cholera that same week. It said Chicago was very healthy. Two papers in one small town, and each one reads the other every week.

== narrative rival
This is where the other paper is made.

== stop the-wharf
Now go on to the river and Newberry and Dole's warehouse. They were in your paper's very first issue, and they are in today's paper too, on page four. They store goods, send goods on, and sell food and cloth. On 12 July 1834 a big sailing ship named the Illinois came up the new channel from the lake. People cheered for her at this firm's dock. Nobody is sure which side of the river the warehouse stood on. You have a few papers left, and the morning is going fast.

== narrative the-wharf
Stay by the river, or finish your route?

== label the-wharf watch-sails
Wait for a ship

== consequence the-wharf watch-sails
You will see what comes up the river and remember it.

== label the-wharf finish-round
Run to the Tremont

== consequence the-wharf finish-round
The people at the hotel get their paper before noon.

== stop the-draw
Go east to the end of Dearborn Street and the town's first drawbridge. A ship builder named Nelson Norton started building it in March 1834. It is about three hundred feet long. The middle part opens up so ships can sail through. Ships kept bumping into it, so it was fixed again and again. Every ship going up the river to the docks must pass through this gap. A newspaper wants to know about each one. Stay on the south side of the river.

== narrative the-draw
This is where people cross the river and ships pass by.

== stop the-tremont
Go up to Lake and Dearborn streets and the Tremont House. It is a wooden hotel, built in 1833. Ira Couch has run it since 1834. Years later a giant brick hotel took its place. It was so big that people made fun of it. Last week your paper said a new auction house was opening on South Water Street. At an auction, things are sold to whoever offers the most money. Its first sale is on the first day of July. That is today! The people at the hotel will want to know.

== narrative the-tremont
This is where the last papers go.

== leg 0
The river end of town comes first.

== note 0
Walk west along South Water Street to where the river splits. Then go a little south.

== leg 1
Keep your papers tucked in. None of them are for the other paper!

== note 1
Walk one block east along South Water Street, toward the Dearborn Street bridge.

== note 2
Walk back north and east to the river side of South Water Street.

== leg 3
Run back past your own shop.

== note 3
Walk west along South Water Street, past the old corner, to the river.

== note 4
Walk east along the river bank to the end of Dearborn Street.

== note 5
Walk east along South Water Street. Then go one block south to Lake and Dearborn.

== ending two-papers
You climb back up the stairs with ink on your fingers. Your head is full of news from two papers. You know what the other paper is selling, and you saw what sailed through the bridge. Nobody asked you to look. A good helper notices anyway. Maybe one day you will be the one who writes the news.

== ending the-river-news
You walk back to the shop with empty arms. Your head is full of the river. The Sauganash got the first papers, and you watched ships at the bridge last of all. The paper comes out again next Wednesday. Everything that came up the river this week will need telling.

== ending sale-day
Every paper is delivered, and you read both papers before noon. You saw the medicine ad at the start of the morning and the auction news at the end. Back upstairs at the shop, there are letters to clean and a whole week of news to gather before next Wednesday.

== ending the-round-done
Your route is done! The Sauganash got the first papers and the Tremont got the last. Every paper went where it belongs, all before noon. Back at the shop, next week's letters are waiting to be set. Six weeks ago you answered a notice in the paper. Today the whole town read the paper you carried.

== keepsake title
An Apprentice's Round

== keepsake text
A made-up note of one young helper's route with the newspaper of 1 July 1835: where the first papers went, and where the last. No real helper, pay or delivery is written on it.
