== premise
Mansard roofs perforated by dormers: six Second Empire residences from 1620 to 2115 Prairie, representing the avenue's predominant architectural idiom.

== opening
JAUNT: SECOND EMPIRE STYLE. A spring sunset, 1904. The mansard roof originated in Napoleonic Paris: a precipitous slope concealing an entire additional story, crowned with ornamental iron cresting and perforated by dormers. By the 1880s it dominated the avenue, and the Glessner House Museum identifies it as Prairie Avenue's most popular style.

Six residences on foot, from 1620 to 2115 Prairie, proceeding south: a pioneer's roof that swells into a crown, Pullman's palace, a residence that purchased a mansard to maintain parity with its neighbors, the avenue's sole survivor, the wealthiest man in Chicago, and the meatpacker's widow. Observe the sunset illuminating the slate.

Commence at 1620, with a roof engraved for a periodical in 1874.

== stop law
1620 Prairie, Robert Law's residence, engraved in 1874 for a series documenting the homes of Chicago: two stories above an elevated basement beneath a slate mansard with round-headed dormers, its northern section ascending into a taller curvilinear crown. Compare the engraving's roofline with the structure before you, then proceed one block south to the most magnificent mansard on the avenue.

== stop pullman
1729 Prairie, George Pullman's mansion of 1870 to 1876, characterized by the district register as a brownstone block beneath a mansard with dormers and iron cresting, approached through a portico on Prairie Avenue. The interior accommodates a theater of approximately two hundred seats, a pipe organ and a bowling alley, subsequently supplemented by a palm room and a spacious conservatory designed by Solon S. Beman. Pullman died in 1897, and his widow maintains the residence. It will be demolished in 1922. Across Eighteenth Street stands a modest residence that declined to be left behind.

== stop jones
1834 Prairie, Fernando Jones's residence, constructed in 1866 as an Italianate composition with a low roof. Two decades later his neighbors uniformly possessed mansards, so in 1886 the residence "received a mansard addition to bring it into fashion with many of its neighbors": an entirely new upper story, acquired exclusively to maintain social parity. That is precisely how an architectural fashion colonizes a street. The hat is removed permanently in 1942, upon the residence's demolition. Two doors south stands the solitary Second Empire residence destined to outlive them all.

== stop e-keith
1900 Prairie, Elbridge G. Keith's residence of 1870, designed by John W. Roberts. The district marker describes it as "the last surviving example of the popular Second Empire style, which dominated the street by the 1880s". Keith presided over a bank, and his residence will endure long after the surrounding palaces have disappeared, an unlikely survivor whose modesty perhaps protected it. Locate the slate mansard above the corner bay, then cross the avenue to the greatest fortune of all.

== stop field
1905 Prairie, Marshall Field's mansion, designed by Richard Morris Hunt and characterized by the district register as a broad brick and stone residence beneath a mansard, with an angular corner bay, dormers and a central portico. Field, "the wealthiest and most powerful businessman in Chicago", resided here. Envision his carriage turning in at sunset; the mansion will be demolished in 1955. One additional mansard awaits at the row's southern extremity.

== stop armour
2115 Prairie, the Kelley-Armour residence of 1871, which the district marker designates a stately Second Empire residence: a patterned slate mansard with cresting above a central arched doorway beneath a pediment. Commissioned by the lumberman David Kelley, it subsequently became the home of the meatpacker Philip D. Armour, who boasted of utilizing "everything but the squeal". His widow remains in residence and will be among the avenue's final inhabitants. The residence will be demolished a decade after her death, in 1937.

== ending finish
The Second Empire walk concludes here. Count the mansards you encountered: by the 1880s they lined the avenue, and even older residences acquired them in emulation.

Subsequently taste evolved, the families migrated northward, and most of these roofs were demolished. Only Elbridge Keith's survives. Return and contemplate it once more before the illumination fades.
