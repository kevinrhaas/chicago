== premise
At twenty-six, Frances Glessner Lee has an attorney husband, two children and an elegant new townhouse her parents built. Her hands crave something else, and one block south her mother has just discovered it.

== opening
JAUNT: THE DAUGHTER WHO MADE THINGS. A grey Monday morning, 21 November 1904, at the northern edge of the Glessner family's world.

Four stops, from the twin townhouses the Glessners built for their two children down to the massive stone house at Eighteenth Street where the daughter was raised. Frances Glessner Lee is twenty-six. She was educated at home, married at nineteen, and she is the last person on this avenue anyone would expect to transform the way suspicious deaths are investigated. Pay attention to her hands.

Begin at 1700 Prairie, at the door of the young Lees.

== stop lees
1700 Prairie, the northern half of a pair of twin townhouses. John and Frances Glessner built them for their two children, and the young families moved in during 1902. This half belongs to their daughter Frances and her husband Blewett Lee, an attorney. She was born in 1878, the youngest child and only daughter, "tutored at home" in the stone house down the street, and she spent fourteen months touring Europe with her aunt. On 9 February 1898, at nineteen, she married Lee in her parents' house, and the family cook baked the wedding cake. Now there is a son, John, who is six, and a daughter born last year. Observe the front door: her husband is departing for his office, and she remains on the step. Her brother lives next door.

== caption lees
Blewett Lee descends his steps and sets off for his office, while his wife and their son remain at the door.

== stop george
1706 Prairie, the other twin, sharing a wall with the Lees. Here lives her brother George Glessner, his wife Alice and their four children, Elizabeth, Frances, John and the newborn Emily. George pursues photography with lifelong devotion; he photographed these very houses under construction in 1901, and in 1902 he was appointed a manager at International Harvester. His is precisely the life this avenue understands: a respectable position, a hobby, a nursery full of children. His sister's life is expected to look identical from the sidewalk, and it does. Yet her son will later explain that what undermined her marriage was her "creative urge coupled with high manual dexterity—the desire to make things—which [Lee] did not share". Walk one block south to discover where that urge originated.

== caption george
George and Alice Glessner emerge with their children for a morning stroll.

== stop mother
1800 Prairie, the house she grew up in, constructed like a fortress at Eighteenth Street. Her mother, Frances Glessner, is fifty-six, and this year she has done something few ladies of Prairie Avenue would consider. In 1904 she installed a workbench in the basement and began crafting silver with her own hands, studying with Annibale Fogliata, a master jewelry craftsman of Hull House, and with Madeline Yale Wynne, a respected Chicago silversmith. Every piece carries her hallmark, "a G encircling a honeybee", because she also keeps bees. She has maintained a journal since 1879, and nothing escapes her notice. The daughter arrives at the door. Inside is a mother who has decided, at fifty-six, to master a craft.

== caption mother
Frances Glessner Lee walks up to her mother's door, and her mother opens it herself.

== stop court
The courtyard, the sunlit private center of the fortress. The mother shows the daughter what her hands have produced. It is easy to imagine what the daughter perceives: a woman of fifty-six who asked nobody's permission, performing exact, patient, beautiful work at a bench. Frances Glessner Lee is intelligent, restless and unfulfilled, and her marriage is "not a happy one". Her son will later describe the "creative urge coupled with high manual dexterity" that her husband never shared. Everything that follows in her life begins with what she observes now: small objects, crafted with precision, by hand, inside a house where every detail functions as a clue. She will have one more child. Then she will leave, and she will build something nobody on this avenue could have imagined.

== ending nutshell
The walk ends here. Martha was born in 1906, and in 1914 the Lees divorced. Decades later Frances Glessner Lee constructed the Nutshell Studies of Unexplained Death: miniature, meticulously accurate rooms in which a death has occurred, built at a scale of one inch to a foot, with every clue in its place. She confessed to an adviser, "I found myself constantly tempted to add more clues and details…stop me when I go too far". In 1931 she gave Harvard $250,000 to establish legal medicine, and in 1943 New Hampshire appointed her the first woman state police captain in the nation. Detectives still train on her rooms. The girl who was tutored at home taught the police how to observe.
