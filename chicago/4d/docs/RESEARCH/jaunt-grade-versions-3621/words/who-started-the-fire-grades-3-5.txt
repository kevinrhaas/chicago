== premise
Sunday night, 8 October 1871: a fire starts in the O'Leary barn, and the cow gets the blame. Hear what everyone said, and decide for yourself who started it. Eight stops.

== opening
JAUNT: WHO STARTED THE FIRE? It is Sunday night, 8 October 1871, on DeKoven Street on the West Side. Everyone knows the story. Mrs. O'Leary's cow kicked over a lamp and burned Chicago down. But the people who looked into it found no proof. A newspaper reporter later said he and his friends made the story up. And in 1997 the city said the cow did not do it.

So who did? There are eight stops: the barn, the yard, the party next door, the watchman in the tower, the hearing, the cow story, the other suspects, and the city's answer. You will hear each person in their own words. Start across the street from the barn, just as the fire is found.

== stop the-barn
This is DeKoven Street, at about half past nine on Sunday night. Flames are coming out of the hayloft of the barn behind house No. 137. Patrick and Catherine O'Leary live in that house. Daniel Sullivan, a wagon driver from across the street, later called 'Peg Leg', has just run over. He shouted 'Fire, fire,' and went into the barn. The hay in the loft was burning. He tried to set the animals free, but he saved only a calf. While the city was still burning, people said Mrs. O'Leary had been milking, and her cow kicked over a lamp. That story went all around the world. Was it true? Start with the woman who owned the cow.

== stop catherine
This is the yard behind the house. Catherine O'Leary milks five cows and sells the milk. Now she is watching everything she owns burn. Later she said: 'I was in bed myself and my husband and five children when this fire commenced.' Two men came to the door. Her husband Patrick ran back to the bedroom and said, 'Kate the barn is afire.' A neighbor, Dennis Rogan, said the same thing. At half past eight he had found them in bed already. She said her foot was sore. A week later the O'Learys swore she milked the cows at 4:30, fed the horse at about seven, and had no lamp lit that evening. But in the front of this house, there was a party.

== stop the-party
This is the front of the house, on DeKoven Street. The McLaughlin family rents the two front rooms. Tonight they are having a party for a cousin who just came from Ireland. Mr. McLaughlin plays the fiddle. Through the wall, Catherine O'Leary could hear the music. Later, a neighbor named Mrs. White told her that someone from the party went to the barn to milk her cows. Why? 'Some said it was for oysters.' Catharine McLaughlin said no one from her family went to the barn. She said they were all inside when the fire started. More than a mile away, in the Court House tower, the watchman has seen a glow.

== stop the-alarm
This is the top of the Court House tower, at 9:28. Mathias Schaffer, the fire watchman, sees the light of a fire to the southwest. He looks through his spyglass. He thinks the fire is near Canalport Avenue and Halsted Street, so he asks the man below, William J. Brown, to ring for Box 342. But that box is a mile past the real fire. Soon Schaffer sees the fire is closer, but no new alarm is sent. Some fire engines that could have come stay home. The historian A. T. Andreas called it 'a fatal mistake'. And something else is strange. No one near the barn rang an alarm at all.

== stop the-inquiry
Now it is weeks later, back on DeKoven Street. On 23 November 1871, the men in charge of the police and firemen began a hearing about how the fire started. About fifty people came to answer questions. Catherine O'Leary was one of the most important. She had lost her cows, her horse and her wagon, and she had no insurance to pay her back. The board decided the fire started in the O'Leary barn. But they said no one could prove anyone had been in the barn after dark. Was it a spark from a chimney, or a person? They could not tell. On paper, Mrs. O'Leary was cleared. But the story did not go away.

== stop the-cow
This is the alley behind the barn. While the city still burned, someone said the cow kicked over a lamp. Artists drew Mrs. O'Leary as a silly, clumsy woman. Later painters named the cow Daisy. Historians say the story stuck because some people already looked down on poor immigrant families like hers, and it was easy to blame her. Then a reporter, Michael Ahern, said that he and two friends had made up the cow story. The Chicago History Museum says he said it on the fire's fortieth birthday. Other books say it was in 1893. Another reporter later said he wrote Ahern's story for him. If not the cow, who was it?

== stop other-suspects
This is across the street, a few houses east, where Daniel Sullivan said he was sitting. In 1997 a lawyer named Richard Bales checked Sullivan's story against old land records. He said another house would have blocked Sullivan's view from here. He also said a man with a wooden leg could not have gotten to the barn as fast as Sullivan said. Bales thought Sullivan started the fire himself. A newspaper writer, Anthony DeBartolo, pointed to someone else, Louis M. Cohn. Cohn said that he, Mrs. O'Leary's son and other boys were playing dice in the hayloft when one of them knocked over a lantern. Some people even blame a meteor. The historian Carl Smith says none of these ideas can be proved.

== stop the-council
This is the corner of Jefferson and DeKoven, today. The O'Leary house was gone by the 1880s. Since 1961 the Chicago Fire Academy, where firefighters learn their job, has stood here. In 1971, a group of Chicago business leaders said Mrs. O'Leary was not to blame. They blamed Sullivan instead. Here, in October 1997, a group from the City Council held a hearing, half serious and half fun. They decided that Mrs. O'Leary and her cow had been treated unfairly. Then the whole City Council voted to clear them of all blame. Her great-great-granddaughter said, 'We always knew that she was innocent'. The city had made up its mind. But the facts had not changed.

== ending your-verdict
So who started the fire? Here is what we know.

Catherine O'Leary said she was in bed. A neighbor found her there at half past eight with a sore foot. The hearing believed her. In 1997 the city cleared her and her cow.

Someone from the McLaughlin party might have gone for milk, as Mrs. White heard. Catharine McLaughlin said no one did.

Daniel Sullivan was the first one in the barn. Richard Bales says his story does not fit the land records. Sullivan said he saw the fire from across the street and ran to save the animals.

Louis Cohn's dice story only came out after he died.

A spark from a chimney, on a windy night, could have done it too.

No one has ever been sure. Who do you think it was? And what would you need to know to be sure?
