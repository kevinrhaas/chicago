== premise
Ride from Wolf Point to the fort past the places of six women whom sources identify in the town of 1835: an author, two hotel and boarding-house proprietors, an educator and the women of two founding families.

== opening
RIDE: WOMEN OF 1835. A summer afternoon, traveling west to east, from the forks to the fort. Most of what was documented about this town in 1835 was written by men, concerning men: land sales, elections, harbors. Yet read attentively and women appear everywhere. They operated the hotels, managed the boarding houses, taught the earliest classes, authored the memoir that preserves half these buildings, and sustained families across two cultures.

Seven stops, seven lives. Each woman here is identified by a source, and each one remains significant.

== stop robinson
Wolf Point. Across the water stand the log cabins that Wau-Bun locates here in 1831 as the residence of Alexander Robinson and his wife's family. Catherine Chevalier Robinson was Potawatomi, granddaughter of the leader Naunongee. Her sister Archange married Antoine Ouilmette, and the village of Wilmette perpetuates their name. Robinson descendants retained their Des Plaines land into the mid-twentieth century.

== stop kinzie
John H. Kinzie's store. His wife Juliette Kinzie subsequently authored Wau-Bun (1856), and its recollections of 1831 constitute the evidence for several buildings in this scene. On 18 June 1835, thirteen days before this date, she gave birth in Chicago to Eleanor, whose daughter Juliette Gordon Low founded the Girl Scouts in 1912.

== stop murphy
The Exchange Coffee House, Mark Beaubien's second hotel. Andreas records: "Mr. and Mrs. Murphy took charge of this new hotel, which they christened the Exchange Coffee House". Operating under the Murphys from August 1834, it was a partnership of husband and wife, and the history identifies Mrs. Murphy as its keeper alongside her husband.

== stop brown
Mrs. Rufus Brown's boarding house, behind Peck's store. "Mrs. Rufus Brown kept one of the first-class boarding houses", Andreas writes, in a town whose houses "were always full; and full meant three in a bed sometimes, with the floor covered besides". She managed the enterprise; the documentary record preserves only her husband's name.

== stop chappel
Eliza Chappel's school. She conducted Chicago's first infant school, established in 1833 in a small log house formerly used as a store; one account describes pupils paddling canoes across the river to attend. Andreas declared that Miss Eliza Chappel "is entitled to especial distinction in this work".

== stop church
First Presbyterian Church. Eliza Chappel was among its twenty-six founding members in June 1833, and on 15 June 1835 she married its pastor, Jeremiah Porter. During the Civil War, as Eliza Chappel Porter, she directed the Chicago Sanitary Commission and nursed Union wounded alongside Mary Ann Bickerdyke at Chattanooga.

== stop beaubien
Fort Dearborn, from the south-west; the Beaubien homestead stands immediately south. Josette LaFramboise Beaubien, wife of Jean Baptiste Beaubien, was the daughter of the trader Francois LaFramboise and Shawenoqua, a Potawatomi woman. In July 1834 a Josette Beaubien served as sponsor at a baptism at St Mary's, the town's first Catholic parish.

== ending finish
The ride concludes at the fort. These are the women whose places this scene preserves. Others in the town's newspapers operated shops and schools of their own; their rooms await discovery.
