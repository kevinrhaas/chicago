== premise
Circle Richardson's final house in summer 1904, from the street it confronted to the sunny courtyard it concealed, in ten stops on foot.

== opening
JAUNT: MEET THE GLESSNER HOUSE. A summer morning, 1904, at 1800 Prairie Avenue. Morning light from the lake falls across the granite façade. Behind it live John J. Glessner, a farm-machinery executive from Ohio; his wife Frances, who has kept a journal since 1879; and the house that scandalized the avenue.

Ten stops on foot, completely encircling a single house: the corner, the Eighteenth Street elevation, the alley, the front entrance, the carriage passage and the interior courtyard. The narrative is largely the owner's own, drawn from the account he wrote for his children in 1923. Exteriors only.

Begin at the corner, where the neighbors first encountered the house and disliked what they saw.

== stop corner
An old Chicago saying described Prairie Avenue as "the sunny street that holds the sifted few". Pullman's mansion stands diagonally across the corner, and Kimball's French chateau faces it across the avenue. Then there is this one: rough granite, small recessed windows, steep red roofs. It was Henry Hobson Richardson's final house design, and he never saw it completed.

== stop north-wall
This is the elevation people criticized when the house was new. Passers-by remarked on the narrow windows, "just enough to light the narrow corridors". Richardson maintained that the windows of a city house were "not to look out of", since residents only smother them in shades and curtains. The house deliberately turns its back to the street.

== stop arch
The great arch is the service entrance, where a small corridor leads to the kitchen. It is also how the orchestra sneaks inside. On New Year's Day 1903 a double quartet of horns slipped in through the Eighteenth Street door and up the back stairs, and at four o'clock began performing in the upper hall.

== stop stable
The west wing contains the stable and carriage house, with a wide carriage doorway opening onto Eighteenth Street and interior walls of white glazed brick. The furnace room lies beneath it, so the dust, dirt and noise of coal and ashes never penetrate the house itself.

== stop gate
Around the corner, these alley gates open into the courtyard, and they allowed the Glessners a clever maneuver. On 1 December 1887 the family moved everything through here, "before our neighbors suspected even that we were about to move". That night they kindled the new hearth with live fire carried from the old one.

== stop door
The front door: heavy oak strapped with iron, with a square window behind an iron grille. Richardson refused to have the ironwork painted; instead it was Bower-Barffed against rust, a process Glessner never encountered elsewhere. The wall appears to be solid rock, but it is brick faced with granite slabs, with deep stones at every opening to sustain the illusion.

== stop porte
Not every visitor uses the front door. Here a carriage could leave Prairie Avenue and roll directly beneath the house, through two paneled oak doors hung on massive iron straps, and emerge into a private courtyard. Follow it through.

== stop court-north
Inside, the house reverses its character. From the street it appeared sealed; here it opens generously. Richardson placed large windows along this sunny courtyard side, admitting, Glessner wrote, "a flood of sunlight when the sun shines in Chicago". The family rooms face inward toward this quiet yard rather than outward toward the street.

== stop hall-bow
The rounded bay above the terrace steps is the entrance hall. Its courtyard door is glazed with yellow glass beneath chipped white glass, so the hall feels sunlit even on grey days. The vines here were transplanted from the family's previous house; one, fifty-five feet long, was replanted whole, and it flourished.

== stop family
Behind these walls, 1904 is an eventful year. Frances Glessner, a journal keeper since 1879, has installed a silversmith's bench in the basement, and every winter and spring her Monday Morning Reading Class meets in the library. Years earlier her son George and his friends operated a telegraph company from his schoolroom, wired to seven houses.

== leg 0
In March 1885 Glessner paid $50,000 for this corner: two lots plus the northern seventeen feet of a third.

== leg 1
Norcross Brothers of Worcester, Massachusetts, built the house for the contract price, without a single bill for extras.

== leg 2
Three weeks after the horns, the full orchestra came to dinner, and the musicians performed a Beethoven movement on pots, pans and dishes.

== leg 3
Glessner recalled his architect weighing 370 pounds: "big-minded, big-bodied, big-hearted".

== leg 4
The house has hosted receptions for more than four hundred and dinners for more than a hundred, all prepared in its own kitchen.

== leg 5
By special agreement the neighboring house used the same pinkish brick, so every wall of this courtyard matches.

== ending finish
Richardson never saw any of this. About three weeks before his death in April 1886, too ill to leave his bedroom, he marked on the drawings where the lights would go and told Glessner, "There, Mr. Glessner if I were to live five years longer, that is the last thing I would do on your house, my part is finished". Excavation began that June, and George and Frances turned the first soil. Glessner remained convinced it was the house Richardson would most have liked to inhabit himself.
