== premise
Spend a spring morning in 1904 around 1800 Prairie Avenue with the people who live and work there: the Glessners, their cook and maids, the stable helper, and the former coachman returned for the morning, each waiting at a different door.

== opening
JAUNT: THE GLESSNER HOUSEHOLD. A spring morning, April 1904, at 1800 Prairie Avenue.

Seven stops around the exterior of the house, and at each one someone who lives or works here waits at their own door: John and Frances Glessner, their cook, the parlor maid and the house maid, the stable helper, and the veteran coachman, who left the Glessners' service in 1902 and has returned this morning to show you his stable. Each explains their role in the household and directs you to the next.

The people and the facts come from the house's own records: John Glessner's 1923 account of the house, the museum's research into Frances Glessner's journals, and the census of 1900. Their spoken words are written for this walk.

Begin the morning with Mr. Glessner, out on his front walk.

== stop john
Good morning, and welcome to 1800 Prairie Avenue; I'm John Glessner, and I moved to Chicago from Springfield, Ohio, in 1870, a few weeks married, to be closer to the farmers who purchase our machines. We sold 800 of them in 1871 and 25,000 in 1884, and two years ago our firm merged into the new International Harvester Company. Mr. Richardson designed this house, his final commission, and we first slept here on 1 December 1887. An elderly lady once remarked that my standing in this city came from my wife and my house. I accepted it as a compliment, since I selected the one and constructed the other. The entire household is occupied this morning, and Mrs. Glessner is in the courtyard: enter beneath the house through the carriage passage, immediately to your left.

== stop frances
You found us! I'm Frances Glessner, and here the house reveals its sunny face: notice the rounded bay of the hall, and the door whose yellow glass makes the hall feel sunlit even on an overcast Chicago day. The creeper on these walls was transplanted from our previous house, one vine fifty-five feet long, and it flourished. Every Monday morning through winter and spring my Reading Class gathers in the library, the ladies sewing while a reader performs. This year I installed a silversmith's bench in the basement, and I also keep bees. I have maintained a journal since 1879, recording every servant we engage, their wages and their reasons for leaving. One August evening in 1891 our entire staff of five resigned after dinner. Consequently I sent for Mattie; meet her on the kitchen side of the court.

== stop mattie
Hello! I'm Mattie Williamson, and this household employs a single cook: me. I was born in Quebec, and my brother Alex manages the Glessners' farm in New Hampshire; every summer I travel a week ahead of the family to open the house. In the winter of 1891 Mrs. Glessner exhausted seven cooks in seven weeks, all defeated by her new gas stove, and in February 1892 she wrote, "Mattie Williamson is going to try my cooking. I shall have her taught". My kitchen lies through this wall, where a board of bells announces which room requires us. I produce eight-course dinners and feed the staff besides, and we eat at three so the kitchen is clear for the family. Last January the entire orchestra performed Beethoven on my pots and pans! Christine has returned from market, around the corner.

== stop christine
Hej! I'm Christine Nelson, the parlor maid, and I emigrated from Sweden in 1892. Mattie and the waitress feed the household, and the ladies' maid attends to the ladies' wardrobes; the cleaning is my responsibility, and in this city it is endless. The smoke is so dense that the drawings in the hall had to be placed under glass. Passers-by mock those narrow windows along Eighteenth Street, but they merely illuminate the corridors, and those corridors belong to us: every room has two or more doors, and the passages are arranged so that servants rarely are in evidence. I can clean an entire floor without encountering the family. Even the iceman remains outside, pushing the ice through a small half-door into the cold closet. Mary maintains the rooms with me; she'll be at our door beneath the great arch.

== stop mary
Hello, I'm Mary Reynolds, the house maid, born here in Illinois. Welcome to our side of the house, where beneath the great arch is our own front door, and we even possess our own address, 35 Eighteenth Street, so our correspondence never mingles with the family's. We women sleep above the kitchen wing, four bedrooms along one hall, with a small porch overlooking the street. The men live above the coach house, with no passage connecting their wing to ours. This door is also how music gets smuggled inside. On the twenty-fifth wedding anniversary Mr. Thomas brought his entire orchestra through here and up the back stairs, and nobody upstairs suspected anything until they began playing. Last New Year's Day a double quartet of horns crept up the same route. Charles, our coachman until 1902, is back at the stable doors.

== stop charles
Good morning to you; I'm Charles Nelson, and I'll be candid: I'm not the coachman here in 1904. I drove for Mr. Glessner from 1878, beginning at the old house on Washington Street, until 1902, and I've returned this morning to show you my former stable. I emigrated from Sweden in 1871, and by 1900 Martha and I were raising five children in the rear building here. Six stalls, and space in the carriage house for as many carriages, all in white glazed brick so it can be washed down like a dairy. The furnace room lies underneath, so coal and ashes never approach the house. When they broke ground in June 1886, young George drove his father's horse and buggy to the site and turned the first soil with his sister. Who holds the reins today, the records don't reveal. August is around the corner at the courtyard gates.

== stop august
Hallo! I'm August Billings, the stable helper, and I emigrated from Sweden in 1892, the same year as Christine, and I sleep in the rear building with the other men. The coachman assigns me the harness and the washing of the carriage, and I tend the doves as well: the dovecotes hold nearly sixty, and their eggs supply the family's table. These gates are the rear entrance to the court, and there's a story about them from before my time. In December 1887 the family carried every possession they owned through here and into the house before the neighbors even suspected they were moving. That night they kindled the new hearth with fire carried from the old one. That completes the household, and I thank you for coming around!

== leg 0
Mr. Glessner paid $50,000 for this corner in March 1885: two lots plus the northern seventeen feet of a third.

== leg 1
Out on Prairie Avenue, the Pullman mansion stands diagonally across the intersection.

== leg 2
The walls are Wellesley granite facing the streets and hollow brick within, leaving nowhere for a mouse to nest.

== ending finish
Two years after this morning, in 1906, the Glessners exchanged their horses for motor cars, and the wall between the stable and the carriage house was removed. Mattie cooked here until 1912, when she departed to marry in California; Frances Glessner wrote that she was "one of the finest people that ever lived". John and Frances remained in this house for the rest of their lives, and in 1924 he signed a deed of gift for it. The rooms these people maintained have become a museum, where their labor is finally on display.
