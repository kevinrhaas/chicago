== premise
Compose an imagined letter home, read the newspaper's list of uncalled letters and work out where to post your own.

== opening
JAUNT: A LETTER HOME. You have spent a few days in Chicago, and you owe the people you left behind a letter, because mail is the town's lifeline. In winter, Moses and Kirkland recall, "There was nothing coming in, and there was no means of sending anything out of the town", and whenever the mail did arrive the whole town gathered to hear it read aloud from "a dry goods box".

Decide what purpose your letter serves, obtain paper and ink, learn how the town handles its correspondence, and determine where to post it.

== stop purpose
Begin at Mrs Rufus Brown's log boarding house, which Andreas places behind Peck's store. Sit down at the common table, if you can find a corner of it, and decide first what the letter is meant to accomplish: a brief word that you arrived safely, a lengthy description for anyone considering following you west, or a page of questions that will require an answer to find you here.

== label purpose safe
Tell them you arrived safely

== consequence purpose safe
A brief letter describing the journey, the town and your good health.

== label purpose prospects
Describe the town's prospects

== consequence purpose prospects
A longer letter, intended for anyone at home considering following you.

== label purpose news
Ask for news from home

== consequence purpose news
A letter of questions, which will require an answer capable of finding you.

== stop paper
In front stands Peck's store, whose printed cards advertise dry goods, hardware, groceries and crockery. For paper, however, the Chicago American of 20 June 1835 points elsewhere: Frederick Thomas's new drug store on Water Street advertised letter paper, wafers, wax, and black and red ink. A wafer or a drop of wax seals the folded sheet, since nobody here uses envelopes yet.

== narrative paper
Paper from the druggist, then.

== stop notice
At the corner where the Democrat originated, read its notices; by May its office had moved above Jones and King's hardware store. On 27 May 1835 it printed a List of Letters remaining in the Chicago post office on 31 March, consisting of names nobody had called for. Every name represents somebody who moved on, or never arrived, or never thought to inquire.

== narrative notice
Ask your family to address their reply plainly and legibly.

== stop mail-corner
Finish at the old mail corner, where Lake and South Water meet. John S. C. Hogan was appointed postmaster in November 1832 and distributed the mail from "a room partitioned off" in his log store, forty-five feet by eighteen. Andreas places the office here until about July 1834. This spring Hogan's card offers goods "one door below the Post Office", and the Democrat still identifies him as Postmaster.

== narrative mail-corner
The counter is one door along, so where will you look for it?

== label mail-corner south-water
Look for it beside Hogan's store

== consequence mail-corner south-water
You will inquire along South Water Street, one door below Hogan's establishment.

== label mail-corner keep
Keep the letter until someone shows you

== consequence mail-corner keep
You fold the draft away until a neighbor can direct you to the counter.

== leg 0
Carry your purpose around to the street side of the block.

== ending safe-and-well
Your letter reports that you arrived safely and remain well. It is folded, sealed with a wafer and ready for the counter on South Water Street.

== ending come-west
Your letter describes a busy, muddy, booming town and its considerable prospects. It will be read aloud at home, and somebody will certainly be on the next boat.

== ending write-back
Your letter requests news from home, and now you understand what happens to a letter nobody calls for. It is ready to send.

== keepsake text
A fictional folded letter, written in town and not yet posted.
