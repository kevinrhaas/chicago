== opening
JAUNT: THE SISTERS OF THE LEVEE. Dusk on a Monday in November 1904, at Eighteenth Street and Prairie Avenue.

Four stops, walking west from Millionaire's Row toward the Levee, the city's red-light district, and ending at a church on its edge. Prairie Avenue is three blocks from the most famous address in Chicago, and it is not the Glessner House. It is a brothel. Two sisters who called themselves Everleigh opened it on Dearborn Street in 1900, and the richest men in the world have found their way there, some of them from a few blocks away.

Start at the corner. Watch which way the cabs turn.

== stop corner
Eighteenth and Prairie, as the lamps come on. Eighteenth Street is a border. To the north and east: the mansions. To the south and west, "several blocks away from Prairie Avenue", begins the Levee, the district that runs from Eighteenth Street to Twenty-second, Wabash to Clark, where sex is for sale on every block. The ladies of Prairie Avenue never mention it. And yet one of the two great brothels of Chicago, Vic Shaw's, kept house for a time on this very avenue, "with Shaw's business existing on Prairie Avenue for a time." The two gentlemen hurrying round this corner are not going home to their wives. Follow them west.

== stop sisters
Indiana Avenue, a block west. Three more blocks and there is Dearborn Street, and at 2131 and 2133 Dearborn, the Everleigh Club. Its owners are two sisters, Ada and Minna. The Kentucky childhood they claim is "another story concocted by the sisters": they were born Simms, in Virginia, in 1864 and 1866. With an inheritance of some $35,000 they ran a house in Omaha, then came to Chicago and bought Effie Hankins's brothel on Dearborn. They took a new name from their grandmother, who signed her letters "Everly yours". The club opened on 1 February 1900, and on the first night the sisters turned many callers away. In their trade, saying no is how you set a price.

== stop rooms
Further south along Indiana, with the Levee two blocks west. Inside the Everleigh Club are fifty rooms across two mansions: a Japanese Throne Room, a Mirror Room, a Silver Parlor, "a $15,000 gold-leafed piano for the Music Room" and "$650 gold cuspidors". A bottle of champagne costs $12, "in an era when an entire meal could be had for fifty cents", and the entrance fee alone is $50, before a man has so much as gone upstairs. Ada keeps the books and hires the women. Minna meets the guests. The women are called "butterflies", and behind them work 15 to 25 cooks and maids. In March 1902 Prince Henry of Prussia came, and when a slipper flew off during a dance and spilled champagne, one of his party drank from it. So began the custom of champagne from a woman's shoe.

== stop church
First Presbyterian Church, at Indiana and Twenty-first Street, on the Levee's eastern edge, close enough to hear it on a summer night. The club's famed clients, the stories go, include the actor John Barrymore, the authors Ring Lardner and Theodore Dreiser, and the young Marshall Field Jr., who lives a few blocks from here. When young Field is shot, the rumour will put the gun in the Everleigh Club for years: "A rival madam maliciously accused the Everleigh sisters of murdering Field." The minister's door is lit. So is the club's. Tonight, as every night, the club has more callers.

== ending close
End of the walk. The Everleigh Club lasted eleven years. On 24 October 1911 the mayor, Carter Harrison Jr., ordered it closed, and Minna took it the way she took everything: "If the Mayor says we must close, that settles it...I'll close up shop and walk out with a smile on my face." The building was razed in July 1933.

Vic Shaw, the madam who once kept a house on Prairie Avenue, lived until 1952 in a crumbling mansion at 2906 Prairie with a dog, a cat and a parrot. Prairie Avenue never once admitted that it knew the way to Dearborn Street. It only ever knew the way back.
