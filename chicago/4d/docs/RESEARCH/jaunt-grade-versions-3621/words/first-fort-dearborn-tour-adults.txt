== opening
JAUNT: FORT DEARBORN, BUILDING BY BUILDING. Friday, 14 August 1812, the last full day of the first Fort Dearborn. The United States built this post in 1803, when Captain John Whistler's company marched in from Detroit and put it up in logs on the south bank of the Chicago River. In 1808 Whistler drew every building for the War Department. Since 1810 the commander has been Captain Nathan Heald, with 'fifty-four regulars and twelve militia'. On 18 June the United States declared war on Britain; the news reached here in July. Five days ago an order came from the general at Detroit: abandon the post. Tomorrow at nine the garrison marches out of the gate, with its women and children, along the beach.

Today everyone is packing, giving away and destroying what cannot be carried, and waiting, and some of them already suspect what tomorrow holds. Twelve stops, daybreak to sunset. By tomorrow night many of the people in these buildings will be dead, and every building but one will be ash.

== stop the-fort
14 August 1812, daybreak. This is the first Fort Dearborn, seen from outside. Captain John Whistler's company reached this bend of the river in 1803, 'at two o'clock on the afternoon of August 17', where the bank stood 'eight feet high', and built the whole post in logs with their own hands. A visitor from Fort Wayne in 1809 called it 'the neatest and best wooden garrison in the United States'. Barracks round a square, two gun blockhouses at opposite corners, and a double ring of pickets. It was built to be held. Nine years on, the order has come to give it up, and tomorrow morning everyone inside will walk out of it into open country, with the enemy watching. Today is their last full day here. It starts, like every day in this fort, at the gate.

== stop the-gate
Six in the morning, at the main gate, in the middle of the south side. A girl who lived here, Victoire Porthier, remembered that sunset was 'when they used to shut the gates of the fort', and remembered one sunset in particular: two months ago, in June, John Kinzie and Jean Lalime 'came out together', a pistol went off, 'they fell down together', and Lalime did not get up. 'Blood was running from his shoulder', she said of Kinzie, who got home 'pretty quick'. Notice the two rows of pickets: an attacker who got over the first 'would only find himself within a narrow inclosure', swept by fire from the blockhouses. Whistler built this fort to stand a siege. No one is planning to stand one now.

== stop soldiers
Seven in the morning, just inside the gate. The soldiers' barracks flank it on both sides, two storeys with 'shingled Roofs and Galliaries fronting the parade'. The first company here built the fort themselves, and when their enlistments ran out 'they had all re-enlisted'. Whistler's letters show how far this post was from anything: he had 'sixty-six men to supply' with fifty-six suits of clothes, 'Watch coats are needed very badly', and he had 'two fifers but the only fife has been lost.' The garrison now is 'fifty-four regulars and twelve militia', and this morning they are packing for the long march to Fort Wayne, with their families in tow. The man who will lead them lives across the parade.

== stop commandant
Half past eight, on the parade. In the middle stands the 'lofty flagstaff'; on the east side, the commanding officer's quarters. Captain Nathan Heald has commanded here since 1810. In June 1811 he brought his bride Rebekah, 'the niece of Captain William Wells' of Fort Wayne. In May this year, Heald wrote, 'we had a son born dead for the want of a skilful Midwife.' Three months later Rebekah Heald is packing to march. General Hull's order to abandon the post arrived on 9 August and 'was read next morning upon parade', here. The officers' 'unanimous advice was, to remain where they were, and fortify themselves as strongly as possible.' Heald chose to obey. Whether that was duty or a fatal mistake is still argued: 'Obedience to orders is the primary duty of a soldier', his defender wrote.

== stop officers
Ten o'clock, the officers' barracks, facing the commandant. Three men live here. Lieutenant Linai Helm, Heald's second in command, came from Detroit at his own request, for his 'straitened financial circumstances and the cheaper cost of living'; his wife Margaret is the 'step-daughter of old John Kinzie'. Ensign George Ronan is 'a young cadet direct from West Point'. And the surgeon's mate, Dr. Isaac Van Voorhis, twenty-two, wrote last October, on his 'solitary walks', that this place would one day be 'the seat of millions yet unborn'. By tomorrow noon Ronan and Van Voorhis will be dead on the beach, and Helm a prisoner. The young doctor was right about the millions. He will not see any of them.

== stop magazine
Eleven o'clock, the north side. Ahead is the magazine, where powder is kept: 'a small structure made of brick', in a fort built of logs. Beside it, the north-west blockhouse and its cannon, and 'A small wicket gate' through the pickets. To the right, the north range, part barracks and part 'the contractor's store of supplies'. When the fort burns, this little building 'alone defied the fire'. Seven months from now a British agent, Robert Dickson, will find 'two brass cannon, one dismounted, the other on wheels but in the river', and 'The powder magazine was in a good state of preservation'. But the fort's spare ammunition is no longer anywhere in these buildings. Last night it was carried out in secret, to the north-east corner, and down a well.

== stop covered-way
Noon, the north-east corner. From here a covered way ran down to the river, so the garrison could reach water 'in time of attack'. Whistler listed it on his drawing but left it off: 'Omited in their places'. Wau-Bun calls it a sally-port, with a well dug inside. Last night the ammunition and liquor 'were carried, part into the sally-port, and thrown into a well'. The muskets 'were broken up and thrown into the well' after them, and the whiskey barrels were knocked in and poured into the river. Heald feared they 'would make bad use of it'. It was no secret: the noise betrayed it, and by this morning 'the taste of the water' was 'strong grog'. Everyone outside the pickets now knows the powder, the guns and the whiskey are gone, the day before the garrison walks out among them.

== stop merchants
Half past one, outside the pickets on the south-east. Here stood the merchant's shop, the sutler's garden, and the armourer's shop, where the garrison's guns were kept in repair. The sutler is the private trader licensed to sell to the soldiers; since January that has been 'Messrs. Kinzie & Forsyth', John Kinzie's firm. Behind you rises the south-east blockhouse, the fort's second gun corner. Whistler's drawing puts the blockhouses north-west and south-east; Wau-Bun, written forty years later, remembered both 'on the southern side'. Memory moves buildings. South of here, the gardens are full of a summer's growing that the garrison will not stay to eat.

== stop gardens
Three in the afternoon, the commanding officer's gardens, south of the fort, where in the early years 'melons and other small fruit and vegetables were raised'. Whistler had the brush cleared 'all within one Fourth of a Mile of the Garrison', so nobody could creep up unseen. Beyond lies what he called 'a large parraria', open grass, and the line of woods along the South Branch. Tomorrow the column will go the other way: east to the lake, then south along the beach, 'with the lake on our left, and a high sand bank on our right, at about 100 yards distance'. A high bank a hundred yards off on one side, the water on the other, and nowhere to go between them.

== stop agency
Half past four, west of the fort, among the government houses: the factor's trading house, the agent's house, the interpreter's, the bake house and the stables. The factor runs the government's own store. Heald's report for today: 'On the 14th, I delivered the Indians all the goods in the factory store, and a considerable quantity of provisions which we could not take away with us.' Out went 'blankets, broadcloths, calicoes, paints'. In April the townspeople had fortified the agency house, 'an old-fashioned log building, with a hall running through the centre', and its piazzas 'were planked up, for greater security, port-holes were cut, and sentinels posted at night.' People here have been afraid for months. Now the shelves are bare. The day's last light is falling on the river, below the fort.

== stop wharf
Six in the evening, at the wharf, below the bank on the river side, where boats tied up and the fort's stores came ashore. In 1808 Whistler judged the garrison 'perfectly secure from any ambuscade', except for one thing: the houses across the river, 'somewhat in the way'. He had already pulled up a picket fence over there that 'might serve as a Barrier against the Garrison'. The biggest of those houses, straight across the water, belongs to John Kinzie. His family will not march with the soldiers tomorrow; they are going by boat, and a boat on the river is a long way from a beach. Walk along the bank and face it as the sun goes down on the fort's last night.

== stop across-the-river
Sunset. Across the river, 'John Kinzie Esq. House on the opisite side River', and three more houses to the west. Kinzie sells to the garrison and lives within shouting distance of the gate. Behind you, the gates are shutting at sunset, for the last time. Tomorrow 'at 9 o'clock in the morning' they will open, the band will strike up 'the Dead March', and Captain Wells, who 'had blackened his face before leaving the garrison, in token of his impending fate', will lead them out. The morning after, the fort is set on fire, and only the brick magazine comes through. The army will come back in 1816 and build a second fort on this ground.

== ending burned
On 15 August 1812 the garrison marched out of the gate and down the beach, and there it was attacked. Heald reported 'Ensign George Ronan and Doctor Isaac V. Van Voorhis of my company, with Captain Wells of Fort Wayne' among the killed. Afterwards, he wrote, the warriors 'set fire to the fort and left the place, taking the prisoners with them.' Seven months later Robert Dickson found the brick magazine standing and 'The powder magazine was in a good state of preservation'. Everything else Whistler built was ash, and the dead lay on the sand. For four years 'over Chicago silence and desolation reigned supreme', until the army came back in 1816 and built again on the same ground. To follow the garrison out of the gate on the morning of the 15th, take the jaunt The Battle of Fort Dearborn.
