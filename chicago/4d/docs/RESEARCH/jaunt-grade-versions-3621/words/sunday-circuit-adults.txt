== opening
JAUNT: A SUNDAY CIRCUIT. A spring Sunday morning, and the town is nursing its Saturday night. Three years ago this town had no church at all. Then, in a single season, 1833, they came all at once: a Catholic priest said mass in one of Mark Beaubien's log cabins, a Presbyterian minister preached in the fort's carpenter's shop, and the Methodists met in a log room at Wolf Point. Soon the town's trustees will ban 'gaming houses, and the sale of liquors on Sunday', which tells you what the town was doing on Sundays before.

A neighbour expects you at the Sauganash, a tavern, on the Sabbath. On the way you pass every place the town worships. Look from the road and see how far three years have come, and how far they have yet to go.

== stop st-marys
St. Mary's, on Lake Street west of State, the town's first Catholic church. Father St. Cyr 'celebrated his first mass in a log cabin belonging to Mark Beaubien' in May 1833; that August Augustine D. Taylor raised this frame, twenty-five by thirty-five feet, its lumber brought by scow across the lake from St. Joseph at $12 a thousand. It cost about $400. Inside, rough benches for pews and no plaster; outside, no paint, no steeple and no tower. God gets the leftovers in a land boom.

== stop presbyterian
Two blocks west, at Lake and Clark, the Presbyterian church, dedicated on 4 January 1834. Its pastor, Jeremiah Porter, 'the pioneer Protestant minister of Chicago', came with the troops from Fort Brady and 'preached a sermon in the carpenter's shop of the garrison on the 19th of May, 1833', to soldiers among the sawdust. Until the church went up he lived and studied upstairs in Peck's store, a minister lodging above a merchant's stock. It is a meeting room too: School District No. 4 is called here for 7 July. Church and politics share the same benches.

== stop walker
Over the South Branch to Wolf Point, where Wau-Bun remembered 'a small square log building, originally designed for a school-house, but occasionally used as a place of worship whenever any itinerant minister presented himself'. A pioneer account credits Father Jesse Walker, the Methodist missionary, with building it, and his own log house 'served the purpose of a parsonage, parlor, kitchen and audience-room'. A frontier preacher, poor and travelling, made one room do everything.

== stop the-call
Back over the bridge to Lake and Market and the Sauganash, white with blue shutters in Wau-Bun, newly fitted up by the Democrat of 10 June 1835. Your neighbour is waiting. On a Sunday the landlord's fiddle ought to be quiet, though Moses and Kirkland remember Mark Beaubien as 'an ardent, if not always devout, Catholic', and 'a liberal contributor' to St. Mary's: a tavern-keeper's money builds the church, and the church forgives the tavern-keeper.

== ending among-neighbors
You reach the Sauganash's door with the whole of the town's religion behind you: a priest's plain frame, a minister's church that doubles as a meeting hall, and a log room at the forks that served for school and worship both. Three years ago there was none of it. The tavern came first.
