== premise
Round-headed fenestration, bracketed cornices and elevated stoops: five Italianate residences from 1612 to 2031 Prairie, representing the avenue's earliest architectural generation.

== opening
JAUNT: ITALIANATE STYLE. An autumn morning, 1904. Before the mansards and the granite, Prairie Avenue was constructed in the Italianate idiom: residences costumed as villas on a Tuscan hillside, the prevailing fashion of the 1860s, when the first substantial houses appeared along this avenue.

Identify three characteristic features: tall round-headed windows, deep cornices supported on carved brackets, and elevated stoops lifting the principal entrance above the street. Five residences on foot, from 1612 to 2031 Prairie, all belonging to the avenue's original generation.

Commence at 1612, with the most accomplished Italianate façade on the avenue.

== stop goodman
1612 Prairie, Joseph Goodman's residence of 1869, constructed two years before the Great Fire. Its photograph documents the Italianate formula realized in stone: three bays, every window round-headed beneath an elongated keystone, a belt course articulating the principal floor, and an arched portico on columns surmounting an elevated stoop. The stoop is fundamental to the composition: a visitor ascends considerably above the street, and above the dust of its traffic, before the door opens, a spatial expression of social distance. The residence will survive until its demolition around 1960. Walk one block south, where the identical year's style acquires a celebrated architect.

== stop hibbard
1701 Prairie, William G. Hibbard's residence, likewise from 1869, designed by William Le Baron Jenney for the president of a substantial wholesale hardware enterprise. The district register classifies it as Italianate: a low hipped roof on bracketed eaves surmounting a stone-based body, with a rear range appended in 1874. Jenney would subsequently engineer the first skyscraper, an irony for an architect who began with domestic villas; this residence will be demolished in 1932. Two doors south stands something considerably rarer on this avenue: an Italianate residence constructed of timber.

== stop walker
1720 Prairie, the Tyrrell and Walker residence, which the district register identifies as an Italianate building of timber: a low hip on deep bracketed eaves, a central gable, a canted bay and a porch spanning the entire façade. The brick and stone fronts surrounding it demonstrate the direction the avenue ultimately pursued. In 1910 wreckers will make it the first of numerous Prairie Avenue residences demolished, as domestic architecture yields to offices, warehouses and factories. Compare its timber brackets with the masonry examples nearby. Cross Eighteenth Street to a residence that revised its appearance.

== stop jones
1834 Prairie, Fernando Jones's residence, designed by John M. Van Osdel and constructed in 1866 for "one of Chicago's earliest pioneers - Fernando Jones". It was originally Italianate. Two decades later the entire avenue had embraced the mansard, so in 1886 Jones appended a mansard addition "to bring it into fashion with many of its neighbors". Here, therefore, is an Italianate residence wearing a more fashionable hat, retained until its demolition in 1942. Prairie Avenue was an intensely competitive environment, where architectural currency signified social standing. One concluding stop lies beyond Twentieth.

== stop tolman
2031 Prairie, the northern residence of a row of three constructed in 1869 with pale Lemont limestone façades. The district register classifies it as Italianate: a flat roof concealed behind a bracketed cornice, surmounting three stories. The three residences share a continuous cornice line, simulating a single unified palazzo, although each preserves an independent entrance. Identify where the brackets interrupt and recommence.

== ending finish
The Italianate walk concludes here. Count the brackets you encountered: these constitute the avenue's oldest façades, constructed before the fire and before the extraordinary fortunes that succeeded it.

By 1904 they already appear antiquated beside the mansards and the granite. Walk the Second Empire or Romanesque routes to examine what followed.
