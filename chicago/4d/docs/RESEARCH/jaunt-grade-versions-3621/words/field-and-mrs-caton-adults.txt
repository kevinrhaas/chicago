== opening
JAUNT: THE MERCHANT AND MRS. CATON. Monday morning, 21 November 1904. A telegram has come from New York, and the blinds are down in a house on Calumet Avenue. A husband is dead, and the man his wife is said to have loved for thirty years lives just across the alley.

Four stops, on foot, from the widow's door, through the back yards the servants share, to the front steps of Marshall Field, the richest man in Chicago. Everybody on Prairie Avenue knows this story. Nobody says it out loud in the parlour. In the kitchens and the stable yards, where the people who carry the coal and change the sheets see everything, they have been saying it for years.

Start at Calumet Avenue, at the house with the drawn blinds.

== stop widow
Calumet Avenue, at the side of the Caton house, blinds drawn. On Friday Arthur J. Caton died at New York's Waldorf-Astoria, and nobody can quite say of what. "Some said he succumbed to liver failure. Others ascribed the demise to peritonitis." A downstate paper will suggest he took his own life. He was a judge's son, a lawyer who loved golf and polo and bred horses and dogs. His wife Delia, a hardware heiress he married in 1876, gave the best parties in town: "nobody ever gave such wonderful parties as Delia Caton". There were no children. Whatever killed him, he died far from home, in a hotel. In the side yard the news is going from the house to the stable. Go round the back.

== stop yards
The back yards, where Calumet meets Prairie at the rear fence. Turn round and the Field house is right there, its coach house on the alley. The servants of the two houses have shared this ground for years, and they have seen who comes and goes. Arthur Meeker wrote it down: "The gossip of the day cast the worst light on the affair; it was even absurdly asserted that an underground tunnel connected the Caton house on Calumet with the Field house on Prairie". There was no tunnel. There was no need of one. There were rumours that the two were lovers for nearly thirty years, and "Delia sometimes traveled to Europe with Marshall Field without Arthur accompanying them." Now Arthur is dead, Field's wife eight years dead, and nothing stands between them but decency and a funeral.

== stop field
1905 Prairie, the Marshall Field house, built in 1876 for some $2 million. Field is the richest merchant in the city. He once proposed to his first wife by jumping aboard her train; by one account "Mrs. Field led Marshall Field a hell-on-earth life", and Nannie Field took to the South of France and died there in 1896. Meeker put the rest plainly: "he fell in love with his friend's wife years before he was able to marry her." And: "In the Nineties, divorce was unthinkable: they had to wait for him (Arthur Caton) to die, which, distressingly, he didn't do until 1904." Distressingly: that is how a society memoirist wrote of a man's death. Thomas Benson, the butler, has the door. The house will be razed in 1955.

== stop talk
From up the street the Field house looks like any other on the row. It is not. Field never liked making speeches, and one night at one of the Catons' grand dinners he was called on to say a few words. Delia sprang to her feet and spoke for him: Mr. Field just wants me to remind you that the White Sale starts next Monday. A joke about his store, made by another man's wife, at her husband's table: the whole room saw how close those two were. The ladies passing the Field house this morning are not looking at it, and they are talking about nothing else. Arthur Caton is not yet buried, and everyone is already counting the months.

== ending london
End of the walk. They waited less than a year. In 1905 they married in London, at St. Margaret's, Westminster; the licence gave Delia as 46 and Field as 70. "The ceremony was quiet, and attended only by a few friends." In November 1905 Field's son, Marshall Field Jr., was shot. In January 1906 Field himself was dead of pneumonia, caught on a New Year's golf course with Robert Todd Lincoln. Thirty years of waiting, a husband's lonely death in a hotel, and in the end they had a few months. Delia got $1 million and the house, moved to Washington, where her mansion was called the Pink Palace, and lived until 1937.
