== premise
Frances Glessner Lee is 26. She has a husband, two children and a fine new house. But her hands want to make things, and a block away her mother has just shown her how.

== opening
JAUNT: THE DAUGHTER WHO MADE THINGS. It is a grey Monday morning, 21 November 1904, at the north end of the Glessner family's street.

There are four stops. You start at the twin houses the Glessners built for their two children. You end at the big stone house where the daughter grew up. Frances Glessner Lee is 26. She was taught at home, and she married at 19. No one on this street would guess that one day she will change the way police solve crimes. Watch her hands.

Start at 1700 Prairie, at the door of the young Lees.

== stop lees
This is 1700 Prairie, one half of a pair of twin houses. John and Frances Glessner built them for their son and daughter. The young families moved in in 1902. This half belongs to their daughter, Frances, and her husband, Blewett Lee, a lawyer. Frances was born in 1878. She was the youngest child and the only girl. She had lessons at home, not at school. At 19 she married Mr. Lee in her parents' house, and the cook baked the wedding cake. Now they have a boy, John, who is six, and a baby girl. Her husband is leaving for work. She stays on the step. Her brother lives next door.

== caption lees
Blewett Lee comes down his steps and leaves for his office. His wife and son stay at the door.

== stop george
This is 1706 Prairie, the other twin house. It shares a wall with the Lees. Here lives her brother, George Glessner, with his wife, Alice, and their four children. The baby, Emily, was born this year. George loves taking photographs. He took pictures of these very houses while they were being built. He has a good job at a big farm machine company. From the sidewalk, his sister's life looks just like his. But her son will say, years later, that she had a strong wish to make things with her hands, and her husband did not share it. Walk south one block to see where that wish came from.

== caption george
George and Alice Glessner come out with their children for a morning walk.

== stop mother
This is 1800 Prairie, the house where Frances grew up. It is built of stone, like a castle. Her mother, also named Frances Glessner, is 56. This year she did something few rich ladies on this street would do. She set up a workbench in the basement and began to make silver things by hand. She takes lessons from two expert silver makers. Every piece she makes has her mark on it: a G around a honeybee, because she keeps bees too. She has kept a diary since 1879. She notices everything. The daughter comes to the door. Inside is a mother who decided, at 56, to learn a new skill.

== caption mother
Frances Glessner Lee comes up the walk to her mother's door. Her mother opens it herself.

== stop court
This is the courtyard, the sunny, private middle of the stone house. The mother shows her daughter what she has made. The daughter sees a woman of 56 doing careful, patient, beautiful work at a bench. Frances Glessner Lee is clever, and she is bored. Her marriage is not a happy one. Everything that happens to her next starts with what she sees now: small things, made exactly right, by hand. She will have one more child. Then she will leave her marriage, and she will build something no one on this street could ever have imagined.

== ending nutshell
The walk ends here. Her daughter Martha was born in 1906. In 1914 the Lees divorced. Years later Frances Glessner Lee built tiny rooms, like dollhouses, each showing a crime scene with every clue in place. She called them the Nutshell Studies. She said she was always tempted to add more clues. She gave money to Harvard to teach doctors and police how to study a death. In 1943 she became the first woman police captain of any state. Detectives still learn from her tiny rooms. The girl who was taught at home taught the police how to look.
