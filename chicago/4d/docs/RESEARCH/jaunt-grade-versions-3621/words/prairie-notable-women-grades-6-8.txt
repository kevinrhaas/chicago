== premise
A spring morning walk past six houses linked to women who changed Chicago: nursing, crime science, a forty-year journal, the vote, a famous fountain and the street's last great lady.

== opening
JAUNT: WOMEN OF PRAIRIE AVENUE. A spring morning, 1904. The men of this street have gone downtown to their stores, banks and packing houses, and their names are on everything. Their wives, sisters and daughters are not allowed to vote for president, and they won't be for another nine years.

They are busy anyway. Six houses on foot, from 1608 to 2115 Prairie, north to south. At each one is a woman the sources connect to the house: a champion of nurses, a future detective, a diarist with a silversmith's bench in her basement, a fighter for the vote, a fountain builder and the street's last great lady.

Begin at the north end, with two sisters who walked into a club that had never let anyone like them in.

== stop frank
1608 Prairie, the Frank household. Henrietta Greenebaum Frank worked year after year for better training for nurses at the Illinois Training School for Nurses. In 1876 she and her sister Hannah Greenebaum Solomon became the first Jewish members of the important Chicago Woman's Club. Hannah went on to found the National Council of Jewish Women and became its first president. Two sisters, one door opened. A block south, a young mother lives in a house her parents built for her.

== stop lee
1700 Prairie, built by John and Frances Glessner for their daughter Frances, who married Blewett Lee in 1898. Right now she is a society wife. But wait. Decades from now she will build eighteen tiny, perfect rooms, each one a death scene in miniature for students to solve. They are called the Nutshell Studies of Unexplained Death, and they still train investigators today. She will help found Harvard's Department of Legal Medicine, and in 1943 she will become the first woman police captain in the United States. Her mother lives a block south, and is probably writing about her right now.

== stop glessner
1800 Prairie, Glessner House. Frances Macbeth Glessner started a journal in 1879 and kept it for forty years, and the house museum still tells the family's story from it. She kept bees. Since 1893 her Monday Morning Reading Class has met in the library, where women read together every week through the winter and spring. And this year, 1904, she has set up a silversmith's workbench in the basement and started making silver with her own hands. Across Eighteenth Street and down the west side, a neighbor is fighting for something bigger.

== stop jones
1834 Prairie, the Fernando Jones house. Jane Grahame Jones was, in the words of the city's encyclopedia, a longtime and outspoken fighter for women's right to vote. Her husband's scrapbook holds newspaper clippings about the Illinois Woman Suffrage Association from as early as 1875. She has been at it for almost thirty years and still cannot vote. In 1913 Illinois will become the first state east of the Mississippi to let women vote for president. The house will be torn down in 1942. Keep walking south, past Twentieth Street, to a family that will give the city a fountain.

== stop buckingham
2036 Prairie, the Buckingham house, where the 1904 Blue Book lists Clarence Buckingham, a businessman who gives to good causes. His sister was Kate Sturges Buckingham, a giver to the arts. In 1927, after his death, she will give Chicago the Buckingham Fountain in his memory. It is twice the size of the fountain at Versailles that it copies, and its center jet throws water 150 feet into the air. One more house, and the woman who stayed after everyone else had left.

== stop armour
2115 Prairie, the Kelley-Armour house. The 1904 Blue Book lists Mrs. Philip D. Armour here; the census of 1900 gives her name as Belle, born in Ohio. Her husband, the meatpacker, died in 1901. As the great families died or moved away to suburbs like Lake Forest, she stayed on and on. The district marker says Armour's widow was one of the last people living on the street, and she will die here in 1927, long after most of her neighbors' houses were sold or torn down. Hers will be torn down in 1937.

== ending finish
The walk ends at 2115 Prairie. Six women, six houses: a champion of nurses, a detective, a diarist, a fighter for the vote, a fountain builder and a widow who would not leave.

They are only the ones the sources name. Behind every one of these fronts lived maids, cooks, nurses and laundresses whose names appear only in the census. Their stories are waiting too.
