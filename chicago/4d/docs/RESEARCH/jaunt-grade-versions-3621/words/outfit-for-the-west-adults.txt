== opening
JAUNT: OUTFIT FOR THE WEST. Daybreak in spring, and the roads west are drying out. Chicago is not the end of your journey; for thousands it is a door, and not all of them come back through it. 'Many immigrants passed through the town with a view to locating farther west', arriving 'by land and by water', in prairie schooners and other wagons, 'on horseback and on foot', and the town makes its living selling them what they will need and what they think they need.

You have fifteen dollars and a long way to go, and every merchant on these streets knows it. Make your list at the Green Tree, see to harness and iron at Lake and Canal, then cross the river for food and tools. Spend wisely: the prairie has no stores, no credit and no second chances.

== stop the-list
Start outside the Green Tree, the public house at Lake and West Water, 'so called from an oak tree standing close by'. Old settlers recalled Chester Ingersoll keeping it from 1834 to 1837. Travellers bound west gather in houses like this one, trading news of the roads and the land offices ahead, some of it true, some of it the boasting of men who have never been there, and some of it told by men with land of their own to sell. Listen to all of it and believe a little.

== stop carrying
A block west along Lake Street, at Canal, Goss and Cobb advertised saddles, harness, bridles and trunks in 1833, the year Silas B. Cobb arrived. The partners parted in February 1835; Cobb's card says he will continue the business alone. Everything you own has to travel somehow, on your back or behind a team, and the choice decides how far you can go, how fast, and how much you will have to leave by the trail when it gets too heavy.

== stop ironwork
On the same crossing stands Asahel Pierce's smithy, opened in October 1833. Andreas records him ironing the first Chicago-to-St Louis stage line and making 'Bull' ploughs from 1834, for men who meant to break the prairie sod. A cracked tire or a worn chain a hundred miles out is not a repair. It is a disaster.

== stop provisions
Over the river on South Water, a Jones grocery and provision store advertised in 1833, and Andreas adds forwarding and commission by 1834. The Democrat's first numbers that year quoted flour at $6 a barrel, coffee at 17 cents and tea up to $1.25 a pound, and 'whiskey 40 cents a gallon': the cheapest comfort in town, and the one that travels worst. Even Jones's initial is disputed.

== stop tools
A block east, Peck's store holds the corner of South Water and LaSalle. His own advertisements, from December 1833 through 1834, list dry goods, hardware, groceries, crockery, boots, shoes and hats. An axe here is a house, a fence and firewood out there. It may be the last thing you buy for a long time.

== ending prepared
Your list is crossed off and the load is packed. You take the western road prepared, with what is left of the purse and a town shrinking behind you on the flat horizon. Ahead is land, work and whatever fever and weather the year brings, and nobody to sell you anything you forgot.

== ending light
You set out light, with money in hand and gaps in the kit. The road will have to supply the rest, and the road is not famous for its stores, or its mercy.
