== premise
Draw up a builder's list for one small roof: what arrives by lake at Newberry and Dole's, nails or nail rods at Peck's corner, brick or none at the town's first brickyard, and a look at the Lake House site.

== opening
JAUNT: MATERIALS FOR A ROOF. You are erecting a small frame house according to the new Chicago method, because the old frame of heavy beams "was found too slow", so the town builds with "the balloon frame, originated by George W. Snow", using light timbers "two by four inches", nailed together. Skeptics predicted these houses would "be blown away by the first high wind", but they were mistaken.

With the walls standing, the roof comes next, so investigate what arrives by lake, choose the ironwork at Peck's corner, look in at the first brickyard, and finish at the Lake House, the grandest building in town, still under construction.

== stop the-wharf
Begin at Newberry and Dole's warehouse on the river. In the Democrat of 1 July 1835 they advertise storage, forwarding and commission services as agents for the Merchants' Line. A balloon frame requires nails by the keg, and nails, glass and iron do not grow on the prairie: whatever the town cannot manufacture arrives by lake and lands at warehouses like this one.

== narrative the-wharf
What the town cannot manufacture, the lake delivers.

== stop the-ironwork
Along South Water Street to Peck's corner at LaSalle. In September 1834 he advertised nails, nail rods, bar iron and English blister and cast steel, his stock "just received from New York". A roof requires nails, and a balloon frame requires considerably more of them. Choose whether you will purchase finished nails or the iron to make them.

== narrative the-ironwork
Settle the question of ironwork before considering any brick.

== label the-ironwork cut-nails
A keg of nails, ready to drive

== consequence the-ironwork cut-nails
Manufactured nails are added to the list, ready for immediate use.

== label the-ironwork nail-rods
Nail rods, for a smith to head

== consequence the-ironwork nail-rods
Rods are added to the list, followed by a blacksmith's labor.

== stop the-brickyard
Across the river on the North Side, Andreas places Tyler K. Blodgett's brickyard of spring 1833 near the bank, with Henry S. Lampman, "undoubtedly the first brick-maker". The first brick building outside the fort was erected that same year for John Noble, on the lot adjacent to where the Lake House is rising. In a town of 165 new frame buildings, a single brick house was genuinely newsworthy. A single shed represents the brickyard in this reconstruction.

== narrative the-brickyard
Decide between brick or none, and the list is complete.

== label the-brickyard brick-chimney
Brick for a chimney

== consequence the-brickyard brick-chimney
Brick for the chimney is added to the list, to be collected from the yard.

== label the-brickyard no-brick
No brick this season

== consequence the-brickyard no-brick
The chimney can wait for another summer, when money permits.

== stop the-lake-house
Continue east along the northern riverbank to Rush Street and the Lake House. Andreas describes the completed hotel as brick, three stories with a basement, opened in autumn 1836, and it "aimed to cater for only the highest class of guests". Before it was even finished, the first Unitarian service in Chicago was held inside, with the English writer Harriet Martineau among the congregation. Observe from the street as the brick walls gradually rise.

== narrative the-lake-house
Whose brickyard supplied its walls, nobody recorded.

== leg 0
With the lake freight in mind, continue along the river to the ironwork.

== leg 2
With the brick settled, observe what an enormous quantity of it becomes.

== ending nails-and-brick
Nails to drive and brick for a chimney: the list is short and complete, although the Lake House will require a great deal more of both.

== ending rods-and-brick
Nail rods for a smith and brick for a chimney: the list is complete, although some of the work remains to be hammered out.

== ending a-plain-roof
Ironwork for the shingles and no brick this season: a plain roof first, and the chimney another year. Despite every prediction, it will withstand the first high wind.

== keepsake text
A fictional list of what one small roof required in July 1835. No real order, price or sale by any merchant or brickmaker is written on it.
