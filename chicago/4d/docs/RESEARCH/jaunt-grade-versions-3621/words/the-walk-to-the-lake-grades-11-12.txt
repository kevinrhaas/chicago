== premise
At daybreak a widow rises at 1906 Prairie, eight years after her husband, sleepless and deteriorating for a decade, walked out of this door at six in the morning and never returned. Retrace the final route of a prosperous, gentle and profoundly exhausted man.

== opening
JAUNT: THE WALK TO THE LAKE. Daybreak on a Monday in November 1904, at the southern extremity of Prairie Avenue.

Four stops, proceeding north along Prairie toward the lake. By every external measure the Keiths are the most stable family on the avenue: three brothers, three residences, one formidable wholesale enterprise. Nevertheless, on a Monday morning eight years ago, at approximately this hour, the gentlest of them rose from yet another sleepless night, dressed in his hat and overcoat, and departed through his own front door, walking deliberately toward the water.

Begin at his door, where his widow continues to live.

== stop door
1906 Prairie, at daybreak. The widow occupying this house is Mrs. Edson Keith. On Monday, 21 September 1896, her husband, a wholesale merchant, "disappeared from his home at 1906 Prairie avenue about 6 o'clock in the morning". He was sixty-three and had settled in Chicago in 1854. For at least a decade he had suffered from chronic insomnia and debilitating illness, so that on his final night he once again paced the corridors, and "his butler found him in a state of deliriousness at 2 AM". By six o'clock he had vanished, and the census of 1900 records his wife as widowed. Hilda Johnson, the ladies' maid, is the first awake; Paul Rasmussen, the Danish-born coachman, crosses the yard. Mrs. Keith appears at the door to assess the weather. Proceed north to the adjoining residence of his brother.

== caption door
Mrs. Edson Keith opens her door at first light and contemplates the street toward the lake, her maid positioned behind her.

== stop brother
1900 Prairie, the residence of his brother Elbridge, designed in 1870 by the architect John W. Roberts in the Second Empire style that once dominated this avenue, and which survives here as that style's final example. Elbridge and his wife recognized the approaching catastrophe. Several weeks before that Monday they consulted Edson's wife, who confided, "I … suspect that he was losing control of his mind through long suffering". His personal secretary subsequently testified that he had never heard Edson utter anything malicious or unkind about anyone, a reputation that defined him as the considerate brother, the one who never raised his voice. Elbridge is awake early this morning as well, and he continues to pass his brother's door each day. Walk north along Prairie, following Edson's route.

== caption brother
Elbridge Keith descends his front steps in the half light while his wife observes his departure from the doorway.

== stop firm
1808 Prairie, the Osborn R. Keith residence. Osborn Keith and Albert Faxon established the family enterprise on Lake Street in 1858, and by the mid-1880s Edson Keith and Company generated annual sales of $4.5 million, employing more than two hundred people in Chicago and approximately five hundred in a Milwaukee factory. Three Keith residences occupied a single avenue, underwritten by considerable wealth, although by 1904 the Blue Book identifies Stanley Field as the occupant here, and neighbors nonetheless continue to regard it as a Keith house. Edson passed it in darkness that Monday, proceeding north toward the water, an extraordinarily long and deliberate walk for an exhausted and ailing man. The residence will be demolished in 1968, excepting its north wall, preserved to shelter the Glessner courtyard. Continue north to Sixteenth Street.

== caption firm
A milkman's boy hurries along the morning delivery route past 1808, the only other figure visible on the avenue.

== stop lake
Sixteenth Street, the northern terminus of the avenue. East of here, beyond the railroad embankment, lies the lake. The street terminates here, but Edson Keith continued; on the beach that Monday morning someone discovered a hat and an overcoat, and fishermen described what they had witnessed. Contemporary newspapers could not reconcile even the location, one specifying the foot of Sixteenth Street and another the foot of Thirteenth, while reporting that he had taken his own life by drowning in Lake Michigan. The search continued throughout Monday, and the household staff comprehended what had occurred only on Tuesday, when they encountered it in the newspaper. Stand here and contemplate the eastern horizon at first light: the lake is grey, motionless and immense, utterly indifferent to the private grief of the affluent avenue behind you.

== caption lake
A solitary gentleman in hat and overcoat proceeds east along Sixteenth Street toward the lake without once looking back.

== leg 0
North two doors, following his documented route, to his brother Elbridge's residence.

== leg 1
North along Prairie Avenue in the half light, past the residences that the family enterprise financed.

== leg 2
North the entire remaining length of the avenue to Sixteenth Street, the long, deliberate walk toward the lake.

== ending dragged
The walk concludes here. The intelligence reached the family "with paralyzing suddenness", and a dispatch reported that "the lake is now being dragged for the recovery of the remains". Edson Keith had executed his will the previous year, on 13 March 1895, bequeathing $300,000 apiece to his wife Susan and to their two sons, Edson Junior and Walter. His widow remained at 1906 Prairie and his brother remained beside her, while the avenue preserved his memory as the gentle brother, the man incapable of an unkind remark, who departed through his own front door at six one Monday morning and walked to the lake. His history illustrates, with uncomfortable clarity, that neither wealth nor social standing nor an impeccable address could insulate anyone from prolonged and essentially private suffering, which the respectable society of 1896 scarcely possessed the vocabulary to acknowledge.
