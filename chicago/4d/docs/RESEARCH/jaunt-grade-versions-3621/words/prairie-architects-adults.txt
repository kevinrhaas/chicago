== opening
JAUNT: ARCHITECTS. An autumn afternoon on Prairie Avenue, 1904, the low sun raking across stone. Thirty-three years ago Chicago burned; it rebuilt itself as a daring new city, and many of the architects who did it built houses on this street, for clients who could pay for anything and expected to get it. Architecture on Prairie Avenue was a trade in vanity, rivalry and very large cheques, and the architects knew it.

Seven houses on foot, 1701 to 2100 Prairie: the man who built the first skyscraper, a dying giant from Boston, the firm of Cobb and Frost, the young Burnham and Root, the designer of a company town, the architect of the Statue of Liberty's pedestal, and the house where Daniel Burnham married the client's daughter and got a street of commissions as her dowry.

Begin at 1701, with a modest Italianate front by a very immodest engineer.

== stop jenney
1701 Prairie, William G. Hibbard's house of 1869, for the president of a big wholesale hardware firm. Its architect, William Le Baron Jenney, trained as an engineer, and fifteen years after this house he built the Home Insurance Building downtown: 'The building was the first fully metal-framed building and is considered the first skyscraper'. Louis Sullivan, Daniel Burnham, William Holabird and Martin Roche 'performed their architectural apprenticeships on Jenney's staff'. The house will end sadly. From 1921 a terra cotta company gives Sullivan, the greatest of them, free office space here, a kindness, until his death in 1924. In 1932 it is razed. South to the corner, and a very different kind of genius.

== stop richardson
1800 Prairie, Henry Hobson Richardson's house for John J. Glessner. Glessner remembered his architect weighing 370 pounds: 'big-minded, big-bodied, big-hearted'. He was a dying man. Three weeks before the end, too ill to leave his bedroom, Richardson marked where the lights would go and said, 'There, Mr. Glessner if I were to live five years longer, that is the last thing I would do on your house, my part is finished.' He died on 26 April 1886 and never saw a stone of it laid; building began on 1 June, and Norcross Brothers built it for the contract price, with no bill of extras, which on this street was the real miracle. George Pullman grumbled about 'that thing staring me in the face'. Now look next door.

== stop cobb-frost
1808 Prairie, next door, by Cobb and Frost for Osborn R. Keith. The notice of 1886 reads like a price tag, because it is one: a three-storey dwelling, 38 by 80 feet, with a granite front, at a cost of forty thousand dollars. This house went up the same year Richardson's drawings next door were being built, granite answering granite, neighbours outspending each other in stone. When it is razed in 1968, its north wall will be left standing to keep the Glessner courtyard private. Two doors south is the firm that would outbuild them all.

== stop burnham-root
1812 Prairie, George H. Wheeler's house of 1884 by Burnham and Root: a picturesque brick row house in the Dutch manner, its front carried up as a tall shaped gable over a stone first storey. It will be razed in 1968. Within a decade Burnham would run the building of the 1893 World's Fair, the 'White City'. His motto: 'Make no little plans. They have no magic to stir men's blood and probably will not themselves be realized.' It is the motto of a man who never had to worry about who paid for the big ones. Cross the avenue to a chateau by a man who once planned an entire town.

== stop beman
1801 Prairie, Solon S. Beman's chateau of 1890 for the piano maker William W. Kimball, French Chateauesque, steep slate and a round tower. Beman, born in 1853, was in his twenties when he 'received a commission from George Pullman to design what would become the nation's first planned company town', Pullman, south of the city: a whole town designed so that its workers lived in their employer's houses, on their employer's terms. He also added the palm room and conservatory to Pullman's own house up the street. Walk south to the street's most famous name, and the most famous architect.

== stop hunt
1905 Prairie, Marshall Field's house, by Richard Morris Hunt of New York. 'Hunt was the first American to be admitted to the school of architecture at the École des Beaux-Arts' in Paris, and he designed 'the pedestal of the Statue of Liberty' and Biltmore, 'America's largest private house'. He was the architect the very rich hired to tell the world how rich they were. For Chicago's richest man he built a broad brick and stone mansion under a mansard; it ends as an aeronautical university and is razed in 1955. One more stop, at 2100, where a young architect married into business.

== stop sherman
2100 Prairie, John B. Sherman's house of 1874, the first of nearly a dozen Burnham and Root commissions on this street. Sherman ran the Union Stock Yards, the city's great slaughterhouse. While the house was going up, his daughter Margaret married the young architect Daniel Burnham, in 1876, and Sherman started telling his neighbours whom to hire. That is how a firm gets a street: the client becomes your father-in-law, and the father-in-law becomes your salesman. Burnham and Root became the most prolific architects on Prairie Avenue, on a fortune made from killing cattle. The house where it started is razed in 1936.

== ending finish
End of the walk at 2100 Prairie. These seven houses are a sketchbook of the architects who rebuilt Chicago after the fire, and a ledger of who paid them. A few years from now Burnham will take the lesson from this street to the whole city: his 1909 Plan of Chicago, 'the first comprehensive plan for the controlled growth of an American city'. Five of the seven houses on this walk will fall to the wrecker. Stone, it turns out, is not forever either.
