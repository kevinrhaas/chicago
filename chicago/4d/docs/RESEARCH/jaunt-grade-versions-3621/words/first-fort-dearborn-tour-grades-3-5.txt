== premise
The last full day of the first Fort Dearborn, 14 August 1812, from sunrise to sunset. See every building on Captain Whistler's 1808 drawing while the soldiers pack to leave. Twelve stops.

== opening
JAUNT: FORT DEARBORN, BUILDING BY BUILDING. It is Friday, 14 August 1812. This is the last full day of the first Fort Dearborn. The United States built this fort in 1803. Captain John Whistler and his soldiers marched here from Detroit. They built it out of logs on the south bank of the Chicago River. In 1808 Whistler drew a picture of every building. Since 1810 the fort's leader has been Captain Nathan Heald. He has more than sixty soldiers.

In June the United States went to war with Britain. Five days ago, a general sent an order: leave the fort. Tomorrow morning the soldiers will march out of the gate. Today everyone is packing up.

There are twelve stops, from sunrise to sunset. By tomorrow night, all but one of these buildings will be burned. Watch for the one that lasts.

== stop the-fort
It is 14 August 1812, at sunrise. This is the first Fort Dearborn, seen from outside. Captain Whistler's soldiers came to this bend in the river in 1803. They built the whole fort out of logs with their own hands. A visitor in 1809 called it the neatest and best wooden fort in the country. There are soldiers' houses around a square. There are two tall gun towers at opposite corners. Two rows of log walls go all the way around. Now the order has come to leave. Tomorrow everyone inside will go. Today is their last full day here.

== stop the-gate
It is six in the morning, at the main gate on the south side. A girl who lived here remembered that the gates shut at sunset. Look at the two rows of log walls. If an enemy climbed over the first wall, he would be stuck in a narrow space. Soldiers in the towers could fire at him there. Whistler built this fort to hold off an attack. But nobody plans to stay and fight now. The order is to march out, and this is the gate they will use. Walk through the gate and step inside to where the soldiers live.

== stop soldiers
It is seven in the morning, just inside the gate. The soldiers' houses stand on both sides. They are two stories tall, with porches facing the open square. The first soldiers here built the fort themselves. When their time in the army ended, they all signed up again. This fort was very far from anywhere. Whistler wrote that he had sixty-six men but not enough clothes for all of them. He even said the only flute for the band had been lost. This morning the soldiers are packing for the long march to Fort Wayne.

== stop commandant
It is half past eight, on the open square. In the middle stands a tall flagpole. On the east side is the house of the fort's leader, Captain Nathan Heald. In 1811 he brought his new wife, Rebekah, to live here. A book says she and Mrs. Helm played a game like badminton here last spring. In May, the Healds' baby boy was born but did not live. The order to leave came on 9 August. It was read out loud the next morning, right here. The officers asked Heald to stay and make the fort stronger. But Heald chose to obey the order.

== stop officers
It is ten o'clock, at the officers' house, across from the leader's house. Three men live here. Lieutenant Linai Helm is second in charge. His wife, Margaret, is the stepdaughter of John Kinzie, the trader across the river. Ensign George Ronan is the youngest officer. He came straight from army school. Dr. Isaac Van Voorhis is the fort's young doctor, only twenty-two. Last fall he wrote that one day millions of people would live here. He was right. Sadly, Ronan and the doctor will not live through tomorrow, and Helm will be taken prisoner.

== stop magazine
It is eleven o'clock, on the north side. Ahead is the magazine, where the gunpowder is kept. It is a small building made of brick, in a fort made of logs. Next to it is a gun tower and a small side gate. When the fort burns, this little brick building will be the only one left standing. But the fort's extra gunpowder is not here anymore. Last night it was carried out in secret to the north-east corner. Then it was dropped down a well.

== stop covered-way
It is noon, at the north-east corner. From here a covered path once ran down to the river. It let soldiers get water safely during an attack. A well was dug inside it. Last night the soldiers threw the extra gunpowder and broken guns down the well. They poured the whiskey into the river. Captain Heald did not want anyone to use those things against them. It was no secret. By morning, people said, the river water tasted like whiskey. Now everyone outside the fort knows the extra guns are gone.

== stop merchants
It is half past one, outside the log walls on the south-east side. Here stood a store, a garden, and a shop where the soldiers' guns were fixed. One trader was allowed to sell things to the soldiers. Since January, that was John Kinzie and his partner. Behind you rises the south-east gun tower. Whistler's drawing put the towers on two opposite corners. A book written forty years later remembered them both on the south side. People do not always remember things the same way. South of here, the gardens are full of food nobody will stay to eat.

== stop gardens
It is three in the afternoon, in the leader's gardens, south of the fort. In the early years, people grew melons, fruit and vegetables here. Whistler had all the bushes cleared for a long way around the fort, so nobody could sneak up. Past the gardens is a big open field of grass and a line of trees by the river. Tomorrow the soldiers and families will go the other way. They will walk east to the lake, then south along the beach.

== stop agency
It is half past four, west of the fort, among the government buildings. There is a trading house, a few homes, a bakery and the stables. The trading house is the government's own store. Today Captain Heald gave away all the goods in the store. His report says he "delivered the Indians all the goods in the factory store". He also gave away food the soldiers could not carry. Blankets, cloth and paints went out the door. In April, the townspeople made one log house stronger. They boarded up its porches and cut holes to look out, and guards watched at night. Now the store shelves are empty.

== stop wharf
It is six in the evening, at the dock below the river bank. Here boats tied up and the fort's supplies were unloaded. In 1808 Whistler said the fort was safe from a surprise attack. He said only one thing was in the way: the houses across the river. The biggest of those houses belongs to John Kinzie. His family will not march with the soldiers tomorrow. They will leave by boat. Walk along the bank and look at the house as the sun goes down on the fort's last night.

== stop across-the-river
It is sunset. Across the river is John Kinzie's house, with three more houses to the west. Kinzie sells to the soldiers and lives close enough to shout to the gate. Behind you, the gates are shutting at sunset, for the last time. Tomorrow at nine in the morning they will open. The band will play slow, sad music, and the soldiers will walk out. The next day the fort will be set on fire. Only the brick magazine will last. The army will come back in 1816 and build a new fort here.

== ending burned
On 15 August 1812 the soldiers and families marched out of the gate. Captain Heald wrote later that the warriors set fire to the fort and left, taking prisoners with them. Seven months later, a visitor found the brick magazine still standing. Everything else Whistler built had burned down. For four years the place was quiet and empty. Then the army came back in 1816 and built a new fort on the same ground. To follow the soldiers out of the gate on the morning of the 15th, take the jaunt The Battle of Fort Dearborn.
