== premise
Ride from Wolf Point to the fort past the places of six women named by sources in the town of 1835: an author, two women who kept a hotel and a boarding house, a teacher, and the women of two founding families.

== opening
RIDE: WOMEN OF 1835. It is a summer afternoon, and you ride west to east, from the forks to the fort. Most of what was written about this town in 1835 was written by men, about men: land sales, votes and harbors. But read closely and you will find women everywhere. They ran the hotels and kept the boarding houses. They taught the first classes. One of them wrote the book that remembers half these buildings. Others held families together across two worlds.

Seven stops, seven lives. A source names each woman here, and each one still matters.

== stop robinson
Wolf Point. Across the water stand log cabins. A book called Wau-Bun says Alexander Robinson and his wife's family lived here in 1831. Catherine Chevalier Robinson was Potawatomi, the granddaughter of a leader named Naunongee. Her sister Archange married Antoine Ouilmette, and the village of Wilmette carries their name. Robinson's family kept their land on the Des Plaines River into the mid-1900s.

== stop kinzie
John H. Kinzie's store. His wife, Juliette Kinzie, later wrote Wau-Bun, which came out in 1856. Her memories of 1831 are the evidence for several buildings in this scene. On 18 June 1835, just thirteen days ago, she gave birth here to a girl named Eleanor. Eleanor's own daughter, Juliette Gordon Low, started the Girl Scouts in 1912.

== stop murphy
The Exchange Coffee House, Mark Beaubien's second hotel. The historian Andreas wrote that "Mr. and Mrs. Murphy took charge of this new hotel", and it was they who named it. The Murphys have run it since August 1834. It was a couple's business, and the history names Mrs. Murphy as its keeper, right beside her husband.

== stop brown
Mrs. Rufus Brown's boarding house, behind Peck's store. Andreas writes that she "kept one of the first-class boarding houses". The town's houses "were always full", he adds, "and full meant three in a bed sometimes, with the floor covered besides". She ran the business, but the record keeps only her husband's name.

== stop chappel
Eliza Chappel's school. She ran Chicago's first infant school, a school for very young children. It opened in 1833 in a small log house that had once been a store. One account says some pupils paddled canoes across the river to get to class. Andreas wrote that Miss Chappel deserves "especial distinction in this work".

== stop church
First Presbyterian Church. Eliza Chappel was one of its twenty-six founding members in June 1833. On 15 June 1835 she married its pastor, Jeremiah Porter. Years later, in the Civil War, she led the Chicago Sanitary Commission, which helped soldiers. She also nursed wounded Union soldiers at Chattanooga, working beside the famous nurse Mary Ann Bickerdyke.

== stop beaubien
Fort Dearborn, seen from the south-west, with the Beaubien home just to the south. Josette Beaubien was the wife of Jean Baptiste Beaubien. Her father was the trader Francois LaFramboise, and her mother, Shawenoqua, was a Potawatomi woman. In July 1834 a Josette Beaubien stood as sponsor at a baptism at St. Mary's, the town's first Catholic church.

== ending finish
The ride ends at the fort. These are the women whose places this scene holds. Other women in the town's papers kept shops and schools of their own. Their rooms are still waiting to be found.
