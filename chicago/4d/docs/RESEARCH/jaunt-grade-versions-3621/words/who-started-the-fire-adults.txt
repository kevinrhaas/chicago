== opening
JAUNT: WHO STARTED THE FIRE? Sunday night, 8 October 1871, DeKoven Street, a West Side block of wooden cottages, sheds and shavings. Before the fire is out, the blame will already have settled on one poor Irish woman and her cow. The inquiry found no proof of it. A reporter later bragged that he had made it up. In 1997 the City Council cleared her, far too late to help her.

So who did it? Eight stops: the barn, the yard, the party, the watchman, the inquiry, the legend, the other suspects, and the verdict of 1997. The witnesses speak in their own words. None of them is disinterested. Start across the street from the barn, as the fire is found.

== stop the-barn
DeKoven Street, about half past nine on Sunday night. The hayloft of the barn behind No. 137, the O'Leary place, is on fire. Daniel Sullivan, a drayman from across the street with a wooden leg, the man later called 'Peg Leg', has crossed over, cried 'Fire, fire,' and gone in, where he found 'the hay in the loft on fire'. He tried to cut the animals loose and came out with 'a half-burned calf'. Note who is first on the scene; it will matter. While the city was still burning, someone said Mrs. O'Leary had been milking when her cow 'kicked over a lighted lamp'. Nobody ever found out who said it first. It did not matter. The story was too good, and she was too easy to blame. Start with her.

== stop catherine
The yard behind the cottage. Catherine O'Leary milks five cows and sells the milk door to door; the cows, the horse, the wagon and two tons of hay are the family's whole capital, and none of it insured. She told the inquiry: 'I was in bed myself and my husband and five children when this fire commenced.' Patrick ran back to the bedroom and said 'Kate the barn is afire.' A neighbour, Dennis Rogan, had found them in bed at half past eight, and she told him it was a 'sore foot'. A week later they swore she milked at 4:30, fed the horse at about seven, and 'She had no lighted lamp in the house or barn that evening.' Of course she would say so. So would anyone, guilty or not. But there was a party in the front of the house.

== stop the-party
The front of No. 137. The O'Learys let the two front rooms to the McLaughlins, and tonight there is drink, a fiddle and young people celebrating 'the arrival of a cousin from Ireland'. Catherine O'Leary heard it through the wall: 'There was a little music there.' Afterwards Mrs. White from across the street told her that someone from the party had gone out to the barn to milk her cows. For what? 'Some said it was for oysters.' Catharine McLaughlin swore that nobody from her rooms went to the barn for milk for punch and everyone was inside when it started; Andreas recorded that they drank beer 'but had no whisky or milk punch'. Neighbours do not always tell the truth about neighbours, and tenants do not always tell it about their landlords. Up in the Court House tower, the watchman has seen a glow.

== stop the-alarm
The Court House cupola, 9:28 by the watchman's own account. Mathias Schaffer, on duty, sees fire to the southwest, puts it near Canalport and Halsted through his spy-glass, and has the operator, William J. Brown, strike Box 342: 'a point in the direction of the fire, but a mile beyond it.' He soon sees his mistake. No corrected alarm goes out, and the engines that a correct box would have called stay in their houses. Andreas called it 'a fatal mistake'. The Board noticed something else: nobody near the barn pulled a box: 'If any person set the fire, either by accident or design, he was careful not to give the alarm.'

== stop the-inquiry
Back on DeKoven Street, weeks later. On 23 November 1871 the Board of Police and Fire Commissioners opened its inquiry: about fifty witnesses, several days, a great deal of beside-the-point testimony. Catherine O'Leary, the star witness, mostly wanted them to understand what she had lost: 'Never had five cents insurance' ... 'and upon my word I worked so hard for them.' The Board found that the fire began in her barn, and stopped there: 'There is no proof that any person had been in the barn after nightfall that evening.' Spark or person? 'we are unable to determine.' The report was attacked, not for clearing her but for covering for the Department; Andreas thought it 'little more than an attempt to quiet' the critics. She was cleared on paper. On paper was not where it mattered.

== stop the-cow
The alley behind the barn. While the city burned, 'some now-forgotten tongue' put the lamp and the cow in the story, and the cartoons followed, the cow later named Daisy. The Chicago History Museum is blunt about why it stuck. 'As a poor clumsy Irishwoman and not a sworn enemy of the social order, she was a disempowered comic stereotype', made to stand for 'careless building, sloppy conduct, and a shiftless immigrant underclass', and blaming her fed 'anti-Catholic, anti-immigrant, and possibly anti-female' prejudice. Years later Michael Ahern, a reporter at the fire, boasted in the Tribune that he and two cronies made the cow up; the museum dates it to the fortieth anniversary, other accounts to 1893. A colleague said he had ghost-written Ahern's piece, adding: 'The booze got him many years ago, and he has not been able to do any newspaper work.'

== stop other-suspects
A few lots east, across DeKoven Street, where Daniel Sullivan said he sat. In 1997 Richard Bales, a lawyer, set his testimony against the land records: another house would have blocked his view, and a man with a wooden leg could not have covered the 193 feet to the barn as fast as he claimed. Bales concluded that Sullivan started it himself, perhaps while feeding his own cow on the O'Leary hay, and lied. Then there is Louis M. Cohn, who left $35,000 to Northwestern and a story that surfaced in 1944: boys 'shooting dice in the hayloft', a lantern knocked over, and Cohn, who 'never denied that when the other boys fled, he stopped long enough to scoop up the money.' And there is the meteor, which science does not support. Carl Smith's verdict on them all: 'interesting, but ultimately unprovable theory'.

== stop the-council
Jefferson and DeKoven, today. The cottage was gone by the 1880s, and since 1961 the Chicago Fire Academy, where the city trains its firefighters, has stood on the site, a flame sculpture by its door. In 1971 the city's business association cleared Mrs. O'Leary and blamed Sullivan instead. In October 1997 a City Council committee held a hearing here, half serious and half civic theatre; Aldermen William Beavers and Edward Burke found she and her cow 'had been unfairly maligned', and the full council cleared them of 'all blame'. Her great-great-granddaughter, Nancy Knight Connolly: 'We always knew that she was innocent'. A Tribune writer noted that the pardon would only 'shift the mantle of guilt from a poor, immigrant Irish woman to a poor, immigrant (as well as disabled) Irish man'. The city had a verdict. It had no new evidence.

== ending your-verdict
So who started the fire? The case as it stands.

Catherine O'Leary swore she was in bed, a neighbour found her there at half past eight, and the inquiry believed her.

Someone from the McLaughlin party may have gone out for milk. Catharine McLaughlin swore no one did.

Daniel Sullivan was first into the barn. Bales says his story does not fit the ground; Sullivan swore he saw the fire from across the street and ran in to save the animals.

Louis Cohn's dice game came out only after he died, and his will does not mention it.

A spark from a chimney, on 'that windy night', is still possible. So is a careless boy, a thief, or no one at all.

The Board could not decide, and neither could Andreas. 'It is highly unlikely that we will ever have definitive knowledge of the cause of the fire.' Who do you think it was, and why do you believe it?
