== opening
JAUNT: FAMOUS NAMES. A summer dusk on Prairie Avenue, 1904. The lamplighters have been round, the carriages are coming home from the Loop, and behind these walls sit some of the richest families in America. People call the street Millionaire's Row: at one point 'sixteen of the 60 members of the Commercial Club of Chicago lived on Prairie Avenue'.

Six houses on foot, 1729 to 2115 Prairie, north to south: the man who put America to sleep on trains and cut his workers' wages, a farm-machine maker in a granite fortress, the piano king, the merchant prince and his doomed son, and the meatpacker who used everything but the squeal. Every one of these fortunes was made in Chicago in a single lifetime, and every one was made off the work of thousands of people who will never live on this street.

Start at the corner of Eighteenth Street, with the biggest house on the block.

== stop pullman
1729 Prairie, the Pullman house, built 1870 to 1876: inside, a theatre of about two hundred seats, a pipe organ and a bowling alley, and later a palm room and a great conservatory. George M. Pullman got rich young by lifting Chicago out of its mud, jacking up whole buildings 'on average six feet without causing them any damage'. Then he built sleeping cars. In 1894 he cut his workers' wages, but 'he didn't reduce the rent in Pullman', his company town, and they struck. He 'died of a heart attack' in 1897, and his family buried him under steel rails and concrete for fear 'some of the employees weren't quite done getting revenge'. His widow still keeps this house, razed in 1922. Behind you is the house he hated.

== stop glessner
1800 Prairie, John J. Glessner's house. Pullman complained, 'I do not know what I have ever done to have that thing staring me in the face every time I go out of my door'. Glessner made his money in farm machinery for the prairie farms, and two years ago his firm merged with four others into the giant International Harvester, with him as vice president. His architect, Henry Hobson Richardson, died weeks before building began in 1886; the family moved in on 1 December 1887 and are still here. Sixty years from now 'the house was threatened with demolition', and it will be saved in 1966 'for just $35,000'. Across the avenue, listen for pianos.

== stop kimball
1801 Prairie, the Kimball house of 1890, a French chateau by Solon S. Beman with a very steep slate roof and a round tower on Eighteenth Street. William W. Kimball came to Chicago in 1857, 'beginning with only four pianos', and by 1881 his factory was 'churning out around 100 pianos and organs every week'. This is his last summer: he 'died at his home in Chicago on December 16, 1904'. Now walk a block south to the richest man in the city.

== stop field
1905 Prairie, Marshall Field's house, designed by Richard Morris Hunt of New York. Field came from Massachusetts and became 'the wealthiest and most powerful businessman in Chicago', worth $125 million by 1905. His store's rule was 'Give the lady what she wants', and the ladies made him rich. He and George Pullman both bought land on this street, and the rest of Chicago's rich followed them here. His house will stand until 1955, when it is razed. Next year his world will fall apart: his son shot, himself dead within two months. It starts two doors south.

== stop field-jr
1919 Prairie, Marshall Field Jr.'s house. He bought it in 1890 and kept growing it; in 1902 D. H. Burnham and Company built a huge addition, two years old tonight. Upstairs live his children, among them Marshall Field III, ten years old. On 22 November 1905 the father will be shot in this house. Within two months the grandfather will be dead too, of pneumonia caught on a New Year's golf course. The boy will grow up in England, without a father. For now, the lights are on. One more house, at the far end of the walk.

== stop armour
2115 Prairie, the Kelley-Armour house, built in 1871 and home to the Armour family for nearly fifty years. Philip D. Armour skipped the gold-rush shovels and 'started a business employing out-of-work miners to construct sluices': he made his first money from other men's failure. Back in Chicago he built Armour & Co., 'the world's largest food processing and chemical manufacturing enterprise', on the killing floors of the stockyards, boasting that he used 'everything but the squeal'. In 1893 he gave a million dollars to found the Armour Institute of Technology. He died of pneumonia in 1901; his widow still lives here, and will outlast nearly everyone on the street. The house itself is razed in 1937.

== ending finish
End of the walk at 2115 Prairie. Mrs. Armour stayed: the district marker says she was among the last residents of the street, dying here in 1927. Most of the others had already gone. After Bertha Palmer built her castle on Lake Shore Drive, 'the elite residents began to move north', and one by one these houses became boarding houses, warehouses and parking lots. The fortunes moved on; the houses were left to fall.

A few survived. Glessner's fortress is still standing, and so is Kimball's chateau. Come back after dark some night, when the street tells its ghost stories.
