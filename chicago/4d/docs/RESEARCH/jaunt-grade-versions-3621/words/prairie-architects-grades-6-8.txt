== premise
An autumn afternoon with the architects who rebuilt Chicago: Jenney, Richardson, Cobb and Frost, Burnham and Root, Beman and Hunt, ending where Burnham met his wife.

== opening
JAUNT: ARCHITECTS. An autumn afternoon on Prairie Avenue, 1904, with the low sun slanting across the stone. Thirty-three years ago Chicago burned. It rebuilt itself as a bold new city, and many of the architects who did it built houses on this street, for clients who could pay for anything.

Seven houses on foot, from 1701 to 2100 Prairie: the man who built the first skyscraper, a giant from Boston, the firm of Cobb and Frost, the young Burnham and Root, the man who designed a whole company town, the architect of the Statue of Liberty's base, and the house where Daniel Burnham got his start. He married the client's daughter.

Begin at 1701, with a plain front by a very bold engineer.

== stop jenney
1701 Prairie, William G. Hibbard's house of 1869, built for the head of a big hardware firm. Its architect, William Le Baron Jenney, was trained as an engineer. Fifteen years after this house he built the Home Insurance Building downtown, "the first fully metal-framed building", which people call the first skyscraper. His drafting room trained a generation: Louis Sullivan, Daniel Burnham, William Holabird and Martin Roche all learned the job on his staff. For Hibbard he drew a plain Italianate house, with a low roof on bracketed eaves. The house will have a fitting last tenant: from 1921 its owners give Sullivan a free office here until he dies in 1924. In 1932 it is torn down. Walk south to the corner and a very different kind of genius.

== stop richardson
1800 Prairie, Henry Hobson Richardson's house for John J. Glessner. Glessner remembered his architect weighing 370 pounds: "big-minded, big-bodied, big-hearted". Three weeks before he died, too ill to leave his bedroom, Richardson marked where the lights would go and said, "There, Mr. Glessner if I were to live five years longer, that is the last thing I would do on your house, my part is finished". He died on 26 April 1886. Building began on 1 June, and Norcross Brothers built it for the contract price, not a dollar more. The neighbors were shocked. George Pullman grumbled about "that thing staring me in the face". Now look next door.

== stop cobb-frost
1808 Prairie, next door, by Cobb and Frost for Osborn R. Keith. A notice from 1886 reads like a price tag: a three-story house, 38 by 80 feet, with a granite front, costing forty thousand dollars. It went up the same year the Glessner house next door was being built, granite answering granite. When it is torn down in 1968, its north wall will be left standing to keep the Glessner courtyard private. Two doors south is the firm that would build more than all the others.

== stop burnham-root
1812 Prairie, George H. Wheeler's house of 1884 by Burnham and Root: a brick row house in the Dutch manner, its front rising into a tall, shaped gable over a stone first floor. It will be torn down in 1968. Within ten years Burnham would run the building of the 1893 World's Fair, the "White City". His motto: "Make no little plans. They have no magic to stir men's blood". Cross the avenue to a chateau by a man who once planned an entire town.

== stop beman
1801 Prairie, Solon S. Beman's chateau of 1890 for the piano maker William W. Kimball, in the French style, with steep slate and a round tower. Beman was still in his twenties when George Pullman hired him to design "what would become the nation's first planned company town", the town of Pullman, south of the city. He also added the palm room and conservatory to Pullman's own house up the street. Walk south to the street's most famous name, and its most famous architect.

== stop hunt
1905 Prairie, Marshall Field's house, by Richard Morris Hunt of New York. Hunt was the first American admitted to the architecture school of the École des Beaux-Arts in Paris. He designed the pedestal of the Statue of Liberty, and Biltmore, "America's largest private house". For Chicago's richest man he built a broad brick and stone mansion under a mansard roof. It will end up as a flying school, and it will be torn down in 1955. One more stop, at 2100, where a young architect met his wife.

== stop sherman
2100 Prairie, John B. Sherman's house of 1874, the first of nearly a dozen Burnham and Root jobs on this street. Sherman ran the Union Stock Yards. While the house was going up, his daughter Margaret married the young architect Daniel Burnham, in 1876, and Sherman began telling his neighbors whom to hire. That is how a firm wins a street: your client becomes your father-in-law, and your father-in-law becomes your salesman. Burnham and Root became the busiest architects on Prairie Avenue. The house where it began will be torn down in 1936.

== ending finish
The walk ends at 2100 Prairie. These seven houses are a sketchbook of the architects who rebuilt Chicago after the fire. In a few years Burnham will take the lesson of this street to the whole city: his 1909 Plan of Chicago, the first full plan for how an American city should grow.

Look for their other fronts as you explore. Every one of them is signed in stone.
