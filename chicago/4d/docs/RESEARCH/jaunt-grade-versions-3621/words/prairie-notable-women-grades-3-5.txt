== premise
A spring morning walk past six houses of women who changed Chicago: nursing, crime solving, a forty-year diary, the right to vote, a famous fountain and the street's last great lady.

== opening
JAUNT: WOMEN OF PRAIRIE AVENUE. It is a spring morning in 1904. The men of this street have gone downtown to their stores, banks and meat packing plants. Their names are on everything. Their wives, sisters and daughters are not allowed to vote for president. They won't be for nine more years.

But the women are busy anyway. There are six houses on foot, from 1608 to 2115 Prairie. At each one you will meet a woman linked to that house. There is a friend of nurses, a future detective, and a woman who kept a diary and made silver. There is a woman who fought for the vote, a woman who gave a fountain, and the street's last great lady.

Start at the north end, with two sisters who joined a club that had never let anyone like them in.

== stop frank
This is 1608 Prairie, the Frank house. Henrietta Frank worked year after year to give nurses better training. In 1876 she and her sister Hannah became the first Jewish members of an important women's club in Chicago. Hannah went on to start a big group for Jewish women all over the country. She was its first leader. Two sisters opened one door. A block south, a young mother lives in a house her parents built for her.

== stop lee
This is 1700 Prairie. John and Frances Glessner built it for their daughter, also named Frances. She married Blewett Lee in 1898. Right now she is a rich young wife. But wait. Many years from now she will build eighteen tiny, perfect rooms. Each one shows a mystery death for students to solve. Police still use them to learn. She will help start a new kind of medical school at Harvard. In 1943 she will become the first woman police captain in the United States. Her mother lives one block south.

== stop glessner
This is 1800 Prairie, the Glessner House. Frances Glessner, the mother, began a diary in 1879. She kept it for forty years. The house museum still tells the family's story from it. She kept bees. Since 1893 her Monday Reading Class has met in the library. The women read together every week through the winter and spring. And this year, 1904, she set up a workbench in the basement. She has started making silver things with her own hands. Across Eighteenth Street, a neighbor is fighting for something bigger.

== stop jones
This is 1834 Prairie, the Jones house. Jane Jones spoke out for many years for a woman's right to vote. Her husband saved newspaper stories about the fight, from as early as 1875. She has worked at it for almost thirty years, and she still cannot vote. In 1913 Illinois will let women vote for president, the first state east of the Mississippi River to do so. This house will be torn down in 1942. Keep walking south, past Twentieth Street, to a family that will give the city a fountain.

== stop buckingham
This is 2036 Prairie, the Buckingham house. Clarence Buckingham lives here. He is a businessman who gives money to good causes. His sister was Kate Buckingham, who loved art. In 1927, after he died, she gave Chicago the Buckingham Fountain to remember him. It is twice as big as the famous fountain in France it copies. Its middle jet shoots water 150 feet into the air! One more house is left, with the woman who stayed after everyone else had gone.

== stop armour
This is 2115 Prairie, the Armour house. Mrs. Philip Armour lives here. Her first name is Belle, and she was born in Ohio. Her husband, the meat packer, died in 1901. Other rich families died or moved away to towns outside the city. But she stayed on and on. She was one of the last rich people on the street. She will die here in 1927, long after most of her neighbors' houses were sold or torn down. Her house will be torn down in 1937.

== ending finish
The walk ends here, at 2115 Prairie. You met six women in six houses. There was a friend of nurses, a detective, a diary writer, a fighter for the vote, a fountain giver, and a widow who would not leave.

They are only the ones the records name. Behind every one of these doors lived maids, cooks, nurses and washerwomen. Their names are only in the census. Their stories are waiting too.
