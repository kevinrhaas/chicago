== premise
Every Monday from October to May the women of Chicago assemble in the granite fortress at Eighteenth and Prairie to sew and listen. This Monday, the mansion across the intersection awaits a death.

== opening
JAUNT: MONDAY AT THE FORTRESS. Monday morning, 21 November 1904, at the intersection of Eighteenth Street and Prairie Avenue.

Five stops within and surrounding the Glessner House. Today is Monday, which signifies the Reading Class: the wives of University of Chicago faculty and the ladies of the South Side, convening in the library at Frances Glessner's invitation. Within these walls everything proceeds with mechanical precision, and the domestic staff remain almost invisible. Across Eighteenth Street, in the mansion of the piano manufacturer, a different clock is gradually running down.

Begin at the intersection, where the neighbors first confronted Richardson's design.

== stop fortress
The intersection of Eighteenth and Prairie. The residence is granite, low and austere, its street-facing windows scarcely wide enough to illuminate a corridor. Its architect, H. H. Richardson, died in April 1886, before construction commenced; the house rose between June 1886 and December 1887. Its reception was not universally favorable. George Pullman, diagonally opposite, protested: "I do not know what I have ever done to have that thing staring me in the face every time I go out of my door". Pullman has been deceased for seven years, and the facade continues its unblinking scrutiny. What passers-by never perceive is the reverse: generous windows overlooking a sunlit courtyard, and a household operating with industrial efficiency. The ladies are arriving on foot.

== stop hostess
The principal entrance. In 1893 William Rainey Harper, president of the newly established University of Chicago, consulted Frances Glessner about how faculty wives might become acquainted with the women of the city. Her solution was the Monday Morning Reading Class, convening in the library for two hours every Monday from October to May. During the first hour the members sew while a professional reader performs aloud; the second hour accommodates lighter reading or a lecture. Admission cannot be requested: "Membership was by invitation only", and for a professor's wife recently arrived in Chicago, that invitation constitutes the essential introduction to society. Mrs. Glessner receives her guests personally, while Christine Nelson, the parlor maid, waits to collect their wraps. Then the door closes, and the residence fulfills its intended purpose.

== caption hostess
Frances Glessner receives her guests at the entrance while Christine Nelson waits behind her.

== stop hall
Inside, the residence turns its back on the street and opens toward the courtyard. The courtyard door incorporates yellow glass beneath chipped white glass, imparting "a feeling of sunlight even in cloudy weather", and today is overcast. The plan operates like an elaborate puzzle: every principal room possesses two or more entrances, and the passages are "so planned that servants rarely are in evidence". Consequently, while needles pass through fabric in the library, an entire secondary household circulates invisibly around them: the cook, the maids, the man maintaining the fires. Frances Glessner desired a residence in which domestic labor remained concealed, and she obtained precisely that. The ladies sew, the reader performs, and the staff remain hidden within the court, where only you can observe them.

== caption hall
Beneath the hall bay, the household labors unseen: a maid crosses the court with a tray while the cook waits at the service entrance.

== stop bench
The service stair, descending to the basement. Below lies the residence's newest room: the workbench Frances Glessner installed this year, where she shapes silver with hammer and torch and stamps every piece with "a G encircling a honeybee". Upstairs she is the most impeccably correct hostess in Chicago; downstairs she is an apprentice craftsman receiving instruction from a Hull House jeweler. Both identities are authentic, and she has administered this household, and that class, according to an exacting schedule for eleven years. Across Eighteenth Street, however, a neighbor has endured a protracted illness, and the physician is calling again this morning. Look through the gates toward the corner mansion of William W. Kimball, the piano manufacturer.

== caption bench
August Billings hauls coal from the cellar and pauses on the stair to look toward the street.

== stop kimball
1801 Prairie, across Eighteenth Street: the Kimball residence. William Wallace Kimball arrived in Chicago in 1857 and exchanged his Iowa property for four pianos, which he sold from a second-floor office. Since 1881 his factory has manufactured approximately a hundred pianos and organs weekly, distributing his name across America. He and his wife Evaline have no children, and he is dying. He will die "at home after a lingering illness" on 16 December, and his funeral will be conducted in this house. Observe the physician ascending the steps and the steward who admits him. Among the honorary pallbearers will be "Marshall Field, Robert T. Lincoln" and, representing the household of the Reading Class, "J. J. Glessner".

== ending clock
The walk concludes here. W. W. Kimball died on 16 December 1904, and John Glessner crossed Eighteenth Street to stand among his honorary pallbearers. The Reading Class persisted, convening every Monday from October to May "until 1930". When Frances Glessner died, her husband attempted to articulate her character: "She had a clean and wholesome and orderly mind, a heart overflowing with love for family and friends and for all in any need". That sentence characterizes the residence equally well: immaculate, wholesome, meticulously ordered, and populated by individuals whose labor remained deliberately invisible.
