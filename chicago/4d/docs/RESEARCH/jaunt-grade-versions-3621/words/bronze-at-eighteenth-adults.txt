== opening
JAUNT: THE BRONZE AT EIGHTEENTH STREET. A Monday afternoon in November 1904, at the corner of Eighteenth Street and Prairie Avenue, the richest street in Chicago.

Four stops round one street corner. There is a battlefield under the pavement where the dead were left to rot in the sand, a bronze that turns that day into a romance, the house of the man who paid for the bronze, and the house that stared back at him. Every stop is a question about what really happened, who profited from the telling, and whose blood paid for the version on the plaque.

Start where the street starts: the ground where the battle was fought.

== stop ground
Prairie Avenue at Eighteenth Street. Before the houses, this was sand hills by the lake. On 15 August 1812 the Battle of Fort Dearborn was fought across them, and the dead "lay in the sand, half buried where they were killed, until 1816": four years of bones in the dunes before anyone buried them. For a century a big cottonwood tree marked the spot. In 1900 a Tribune writer swore that the Pullman house in front of this corner "stands about where the massacre took place". A millionaire's parlour on a killing ground. Now look at the street in 1904: brick, granite, gas lamps, a schoolteacher with her class. Walk east along Eighteenth Street to the bronze.

== stop bronze
The monument. George Pullman paid for it in 1893, near his own front door: Carl Rohl-Smith's bronze of the Potawatomi chief Black Partridge saving Margaret Helm. Noble, and perhaps untrue. "Some historians regard the incident as the product of romantic imagination instead of eyewitness testimony", and say "Mrs. Helm rode her horse to the shore of Lake Michigan where she found refuge." The sculptor's models came from Fort Sheridan, survivors of Wounded Knee, whom he called "Indians of the most untamed sort". His figures "may have owed more to Buffalo Bill Cody's Wild West Show than to actual research." Now look at the house the bronze guards.

== stop strike
1729 Prairie, the Pullman house. The man who paid for the bronze built sleeping cars, and a company town south of the city where his workers paid him rent. In 1894 he cut their wages, but "he didn't reduce the rent in Pullman", so the men who built his cars paid him the same rent out of smaller wages, and they struck. Then "a year after the statue's dedication", the Seventh Cavalry, the regiment of Wounded Knee, "came to Chicago to quell the Pullman riots of 1894." A regiment that 'had fought Indians so fiercely at Little Big Horn and Wounded Knee' was turned on Chicago working men, in a strike against the man whose bronze stood here. Pullman died in 1897. The house will be razed in 1922. Cross Prairie to the house that stared at his.

== stop grave
The Glessner corner, diagonally across from Pullman. Pullman hated this house: "I do not know what I have ever done to have that thing staring me in the face every time I go out of my door". When he died, his family feared his body would be dug up, by robbers, or because "some of the employees weren't quite done getting revenge". So they buried him in a "lead-lined mahogany coffin, which was then sealed inside a block of concrete", under steel rails "bolted together at right angles". His family feared his own workers would come for his corpse. One historian is honest about the legends: "Whether it's true or not, we don't really know." Bronze, granite and concrete, and under all of it, the sand.

== ending sand
End of the walk. In 1904 the bronze still stands at Eighteenth Street, telling its story one way, the way the man who paid for it preferred. Some historians doubt the rescue it shows ever happened. Pullman lies at Graceland under his concrete, the strike he broke is in every history book, and the ground under this corner still holds the sand of 1812, where the dead lay half buried for four years. A monument shows who paid for it. The rest you have to dig for.
