== premise
Come in off the open shore south of the fort. Start at an old trading house below Fort Dearborn and pass the fort's log fence. Find a store on South Water Street and end at an inn's front door.

== opening
JAUNT: FROM PRAIRIE TO TOWN. It is sunset, and you have come up the open shore from the south. This is how many people first saw Chicago. Philo Carpenter landed on this shore in July 1832. A man named Joe Ellis carried him to the fort in a cart pulled by oxen. Looking inland, Philo saw only flat grassland covered with tall wild grass.

Three years later the grassland is filling up. Since the land office opened, buyers have filled the streets. Some camp out on the open grass around town. Ride north past the fort. Find a store on the river street. Reach an inn before dark.

== stop factors-house
Start at the old government trading house below the fort's fence. A man who knew it in 1818 remembered a two-story house of squared logs. A map from 1830 shows it here. Past the army's land, the shore is open. An 1834 map shows that nobody had bought that land yet. This is the same shore where Philo Carpenter came ashore in 1832.

== narrative factors-house
Look around. The town is ahead, and the grassland is behind.

== stop the-stockade
Ride north to Fort Dearborn's tall log fence where the river meets the lake. Juliette Kinzie lived inside it in 1831. She wrote about its tall log walls and its big gates on the north and south sides. Soldiers have kept the fort since June 1832, so today it is a working army post. Look from outside. The gates are shut.

== narrative the-stockade
These are the town's first buildings.

== stop the-store
Ride west along the river to South Water and LaSalle Streets and Peck's store. It was the town's first two-story wooden building. Its boards were brought from Detroit by boat. His ad in the paper offered cloth, tools, food, dishes, boots, shoes and hats. In May 1833 a new minister slept in its unfinished attic.

== narrative the-store
A place to buy what the trip used up.

== stop the-public-house
Last, ride on to Lake and Market Streets and the Sauganash, on the east bank of the river's South Branch. A woman who knew it remembered it as white and two stories tall, with bright blue shutters. On 10 June 1835 the paper said it was fixed up new. A history book calls it the nicest inn the city had. Nobody knows if a bed is free tonight.

== narrative the-public-house
An inn at the end of the ride, and a fiddle playing somewhere inside.

== leg 0
Ride from the last house to the town's first buildings.

== leg 1
Ride into the trading street.

== leg 2
Ride the last few blocks to a door.

== ending in-town
You reach the Sauganash's door after coming in from the south. You passed the last house below the fort, rode along the river street to a store, and on to an inn. Three years ago this was tall grass and a cart pulled by oxen. Tonight it is a town.
