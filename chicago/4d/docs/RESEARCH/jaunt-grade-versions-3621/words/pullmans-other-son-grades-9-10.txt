== premise
A German sergeant insisted George Pullman was his father. For four years he pursued the family, to the office, to the funeral, to the front steps, and finally to the wrong end of a judge's revolver.

== opening
JAUNT: PULLMAN'S OTHER SON. A Monday afternoon in November 1904, outside the Pullman mansion at Eighteenth Street.

Four stops, around the Pullman house and down the avenue to the residence of the family's son-in-law. George Pullman had twin sons, and the entire city knew their names. A third young man claimed the name as well. His mother revealed on her deathbed who his father was, and he crossed an ocean to declare it to Pullman's face. The family dismissed him as a lunatic, yet the doctors repeatedly concluded he was sane.

Begin at the front of the mansion, where his story starts.

== stop mother
1729 Prairie, the Pullman mansion. Imagine a German Army sergeant named Gustave Behring, twenty-eight years old, at his dying mother's bedside. She confessed that his real father was George M. Pullman. She had worked as a governess in Chicago "for a house on the west side", she said, and when she became pregnant, Pullman allegedly shipped her back to Germany with $500. Behring traveled to Chicago, where Pullman ejected him from his office, and he was subsequently confined for a time in an asylum and arrested six more times for lurking outside the Pullman Building. Four days before Pullman's death, their exchange was recorded: "BEHRING: You are my father! PULLMAN: You are a crazy man!" The mansion will be demolished in 1922. Walk around to the front steps.

== caption mother
A stranger in a dark coat pauses at the Pullman gate, studies the house, and moves on.

== stop steps
The front steps. Pullman died of a heart attack in October 1897, and his family buried him beneath concrete and steel. "Less than 12 hours after the funeral, Behring showed up at the family's residence on Prairie Avenue, demanding to speak to Mrs. Harriett Pullman". He never reached the door. On these steps he was confronted by Pullman's twin sons and the family's attorneys, and he was arrested once again. On 29 October 1897 an expert on insanity examined him and declared him sane. He was not a madman, then, but simply a determined man who refused to disappear. The Pullmans' housekeeper, Emma Scott, still answers this door. Walk around the corner to the conservatory.

== caption steps
Emma Scott, the housekeeper, opens the front door, surveys the empty steps, and closes it again.

== stop ship
The conservatory, the family's glass parlor. Behind this glass the Pullmans waited for their problem to depart, and in September 1898 it did: detectives escorted Behring up the gangway of the Cunard liner Campania, bound for Bremen. Europe could keep him, the family evidently hoped; yet he returned, because a man who has heard from his dying mother who his father was does not remain deported. By 1901 the twins who had blocked the steps faced scandals of their own, and George Jr. would die of pneumonia that November. Florence Pullman's husband, Frank Lowden, was an attorney, so when Behring returned, he went to Lowden. Walk south down Prairie to the Lowden residence.

== caption ship
The groom, Samuel Whatter Jr., emerges from the stable yard beside the conservatory and stands gazing down the street.

== stop gun
1912 Prairie, where Frank Lowden lives with Florence and their children. Lowden is a cautious, deliberate man, but even cautious men experience dramatic afternoons. "On June 25, 1901, Behring was again arrested at the law offices of Frank Lowden (Florence Pullman's husband) demanding $5,000 in cash and $10,000 a month for 6 months". Lowden's partner did not wait for the police to negotiate: "Judge Davis, Lowden's partner, held Behring at gunpoint until the police arrived". The house will be demolished in 1939. Watch Lowden return home this afternoon, glancing back down the street before he steps inside.

== ending name
The walk ends here. Gustave Behring was arrested, institutionalized, examined, declared sane, deported and arrested again, and as the newspapers reported it, his story concludes at the point of a judge's revolver. "You are my father!" "You are a crazy man!" Those two sentences are all that survive of the only conversation the two men ever had. Weigh the evidence, consider who had the power to be believed, and decide for yourself which of them was telling the truth.
