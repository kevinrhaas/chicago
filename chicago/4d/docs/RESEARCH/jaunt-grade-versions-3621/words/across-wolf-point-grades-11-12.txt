== premise
Undertake a brief crossing between the settlement's divisions and comprehend why the confluence of the river's branches was so consequential.

== opening
JAUNT: ACROSS WOLF POINT. Daybreak in spring, with the river running high. This is where Chicago originated: at the forks, where the North and South Branches converge and flow eastward to the lake as a unified river. Before there were surveyed streets there were taverns here, along with the earliest bridges, and a ferryman who simultaneously served as the community's fiddler.

Cross the river as the settlement did: from the tavern that gave Wolf Point its name, along the western bank past the Green Tree, over the South Branch bridge, and onward to the Sauganash. Four stops, nothing to purchase, and the entire nascent settlement visible.

== stop the-fork
Commence on the western bank at the tavern that named Wolf Point, constructed partially of logs and partially of sawn frame. Its sign was improvised by an army officer, Lieutenant Allen, from a fragment of a packing box, decorated with a painted wolf. Whether it continues to accommodate travelers is contested: one account has William Walters operating it until 1836, whereas another maintains it ceased in 1834. Look eastward: the two branches converge immediately off this bank and continue to the lake as a single channel.

== narrative the-fork
Interpret the confluence before you cross it.

== stop west-bank
South along the bank to the Green Tree, at Lake and West Water, which James Kinzie constructed in 1833 as the first building here erected specifically for hotel purposes, "so called from an oak tree standing close by". Early settlers recalled Chester Ingersoll managing it from 1834 to 1837. It is the extraordinary survivor among the settlement's taverns: six decades later it still stands, relocated to Milwaukee Avenue, "the oldest memento of early taverns".

== narrative west-bank
Face eastward: the south side constitutes your destination.

== stop the-crossing
Several steps south lies the South Branch bridge between Lake and Randolph. Moses and Kirkland report that it "at first consisted of floating logs tied together"; four early settlers attested that it rested upon log abutments and two bents, approximately ten feet wide and six feet above the water, sufficiently elevated for teams to pass beneath on the winter ice. It was financed by public subscription, totaling $486.20, "of which the Indians of the vicinity contributed $200".

== narrative the-crossing
This is the crossing: the deck, never the water.

== stop south-side
Off the bridge and north to Lake and Market stands the Sauganash, white and two stories tall, with bright-blue wooden shutters, which the Democrat of 10 June 1835 reported newly refurbished. Moses and Kirkland characterize it as "the most attractive hostlery the city possessed". Its proprietor, Mark Beaubien, was "fiddler, landlord, merchant, ferryman and a patron of horse racing", and during winter the settlement raced its horses upon the frozen river you have just traversed.

== narrative south-side
Along the western bank, over the bridge, north to this corner: the route is now familiar.

== leg 0
Observe the two branches initially, then proceed along the one you will cross.

== leg 1
The South Branch constitutes the water separating you from the south side.

== leg 2
Arrive on the south side at the settlement's most celebrated establishment.

== ending crossed
You have crossed between the divisions precisely as the settlement did, by the bridge rather than the water, from the oldest tavern corner to the most renowned. Return when the river freezes, for the races.
