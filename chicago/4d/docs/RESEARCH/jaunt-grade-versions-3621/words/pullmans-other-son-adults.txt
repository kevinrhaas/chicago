== opening
JAUNT: PULLMAN'S OTHER SON. A Monday afternoon in November 1904, outside the Pullman house at Eighteenth Street.

Four stops, around the Pullman house and down the street to the house of the family's son-in-law. George Pullman had twin sons, and the whole city knew their names. A third young man claimed the name too. He crossed an ocean on his dying mother's word to say it to Pullman's face. The family called him a madman. The doctors kept finding him sane.

This is a story about a rich man, a governess and $500, and about how far a fortune will go to make a problem disappear. Start at the front of the house, where his story begins.

== stop mother
1729 Prairie, the Pullman house. Begin at a deathbed. A German Army sergeant named Gustave Behring, 28 years old, sits by his dying mother, and with almost her last breath she names his real father: George M. Pullman. She had been a governess in Chicago "for a house on the west side". When she became pregnant, Pullman allegedly packed her off to Germany with $500, the price of keeping a scandal an ocean away. Behring came to Chicago. At the Pullman building he was thrown out of Pullman's office, confined for a time in a mental institution, and arrested six times more lurking outside. Four days before Pullman died, their conversation was recorded. "BEHRING: You are my father! PULLMAN: You are a crazy man!" The house will be razed in 1922. Walk round to the front steps.

== stop steps
The front steps. Pullman died of a heart attack in October 1897, three years after he cut his workers' wages while 'he didn't reduce the rent in Pullman', his company town. Afraid his body would be dug up, the family sealed him in a lead-lined coffin inside a block of concrete, under steel rails. And "Less than 12 hours after the funeral, Behring showed up at the family's residence on Prairie Avenue, demanding to speak to Mrs. Harriett Pullman." He never reached the door. On these steps he was met by Pullman's two sons, the twins, and by the family's lawyers. He was arrested again, and on 29 October 1897 an insanity expert pronounced him sane. Not a madman, then: a man who would not go away. Walk round the corner to the glasshouse.

== stop ship
The conservatory, the family's glass parlour. In September 1898 the family's problem went away, for a while: detectives walked Behring up the gangway of the Cunard liner Campania, bound for Bremen. Money had once sent his mother across the ocean; now it sent him. He came back anyway. By 1901 the twins who had blocked the steps had a scandal of their own. George Jr. secretly married Lynn Fernald, 'his brother's betrothed', and Frederick Bowers sued him for $100,000, 'eventually settling for $50,000.' George died of complications from pneumonia in November 1901. Florence Pullman's husband, Frank Lowden, practised law, so when Behring came back, he went to Lowden. Walk south down Prairie to the Lowden house.

== stop gun
1912 Prairie, where Frank Lowden lives with Florence and their children. "on June 25, 1901, Behring was again arrested at the law offices of Frank Lowden (Florence Pullman's husband) demanding $5,000 in cash and $10,000 a month for 6 months." The $500 that sent his mother away had become $65,000. And "Judge Davis, Lowden's partner, held Behring at gunpoint until the police arrived". If the sergeant's story was true, Lowden's partner was holding a gun on Lowden's own brother-in-law. The house will be razed in 1939. Watch Lowden come home this afternoon, and look back down the street before he goes in.

== ending name
End of the walk. Gustave Behring was thrown out, locked up, examined, found sane, shipped to Bremen and arrested again, and in the papers his story ends at the point of a judge's gun. Nobody proved him a liar, and nobody proved him a son.

"You are my father!" "You are a crazy man!" Those two sentences are all that is left of the only conversation the two men ever had. A rich family spent a great deal of money to make sure the second one was the last word.
