== opening
JAUNT: BOOTS, LEATHER AND THE ROAD. A spring afternoon, the mud drying at last, and you ride out tomorrow. The strangers pour through this town 'by land and by water', in prairie schooners and other wagons, 'on horseback and on foot', and most of them, like you, are 'locating farther west'. Before you join them you need an outfit, and an outfit is leather: boots for the rider and tack for the horse.

Here is the catch. In this town those are two different trades in two different shops on two sides of the river. Buy boots on South Water Street, look across the forks at the tannery where Chicago's leather began, visit the saddler at Lake and Canal, and lay it all out at the Green Tree tonight.

== stop ready-made
Start one door east of Dearborn on South Water Street. In June 1835 John Holbrook advertised hats, clothing, boots and shoes, wholesale and retail, as agent for the manufacturers. These are made goods, stitched in eastern workshops by hands you will never see, shipped in by the crate and sold off the shelf. In a town growing this fast nobody waits for a cobbler, and nobody asks who made the boots or what they were paid.

== stop tannery
Over the South Branch to Wolf Point. Across the North Branch, in 1831, John Miller built a log house on the point and used it as a tannery, Chicago's first recorded factory. Before the boats brought boots by the crate, leather here began as raw hides soaking in a pit. Every polished boot in town began as a dead animal. Whether hides were still worked this summer, no source says.

== stop saddler
South down the west bank to Lake and Canal. Goss & Cobb opened here in 1833, the year Silas B. Cobb arrived among the flood of that year's newcomers, making saddles, harness, bridles, martingales and trunks to order. The partners parted in February 1835, and Cobb's June card says he will continue alone. One partnership in this town, at least, did not last two years. Neither card names boots.

== stop road
East along Lake to West Water and the Green Tree, which James Kinzie built in 1833 as the first house here put up for hotel purposes, 'so called from an oak tree standing close by'. Old settlers recalled Chester Ingersoll keeping it from 1834 to 1837. Lay the outfit out on the floor for the morning, between the other travellers' packs, and keep one eye on your own.

== ending another-day
Another day in town, then, and another night's lodging out of a thin purse. What the outfit lacks is on a card somewhere on these streets, and the road will keep. A day late is not the same as unready, and the prairie has no shops.

== ending equipped
Boots from the clothier and a bridle from the saddler: leather for the rider and leather for the horse, from two trades that did not do each other's work. You ride out at first light, equipped to travel and committed now, with the town at your back and nothing ahead but road.

== ending trunk-bound
A trunk will not ride on a saddle. The morning begins with finding a wagon going your way, and paying whatever its driver asks: the baggage is ready and the way of carrying it is not.

== ending half-ready
You ride out with what you have. Boots for the rider and a bridle for the horse were the outfit's two leathers, one from each trade; whatever is missing, the road will ask for before long, and far from any shop.
