== premise
Steep mansard roofs with little windows: six Second Empire houses from 1620 to 2115 Prairie, the street's most popular style.

== opening
JAUNT: SECOND EMPIRE STYLE. It is a spring sunset in 1904. The mansard roof came from Paris. It is a steep roof that hides a whole extra floor inside. It has iron trim on top and little windows poking out of it. By the 1880s this style was everywhere on the street. The Glessner House Museum says it was Prairie Avenue's most popular style.

There are six houses on foot, from 1620 to 2115 Prairie, going south. You will see a roof that rises into a crown, and Pullman's palace. You will see a house that bought a new roof to keep up with its neighbors, and the last one still standing. Then come the richest man in Chicago and the meat packer's widow. Watch the sunset shine on the slate.

Start at 1620, with a roof drawn for a magazine in 1874.

== stop law
This is 1620 Prairie, Robert Law's house. A magazine printed a drawing of it in 1874. It has two floors over a raised basement. On top is a slate mansard roof with round-topped windows. The north part of the roof rises up into a taller, curved crown. Compare the drawing's roof with the real one in front of you. Then walk a block south to the grandest mansard on the street.

== stop pullman
This is 1729 Prairie, George Pullman's house, built from 1870 to 1876. It is made of brownstone, with a mansard roof, little windows and iron trim. Inside are a theater with about two hundred seats, a pipe organ and a bowling alley. Later he added a palm room and a big glass plant room. Pullman died in 1897. His wife still lives here. The house will be torn down in 1922. Across Eighteenth Street is a smaller house that did not want to be left behind.

== stop jones
This is 1834 Prairie, Fernando Jones's house. It was built in 1866 with a low roof. Twenty years later all his neighbors had mansards. So in 1886 he added a mansard too, to look in style. He bought a whole new top floor just to keep up! That is how a style takes over a street. The house will be torn down in 1942. Two doors south is the one Second Empire house that will outlive them all.

== stop e-keith
This is 1900 Prairie, Elbridge Keith's house, built in 1870. A sign here calls it the last one left of the Second Empire style, which ruled the street by the 1880s. Mr. Keith was a bank president. His house will still be standing long after the palaces around it are gone. Look for the slate mansard over the corner bay. Then cross the street to the biggest fortune of all.

== stop field
This is 1905 Prairie, Marshall Field's house. It is a wide brick and stone mansion with a mansard roof. It has a pointed corner bay, little roof windows and a porch in the middle. Mr. Field, the richest and most powerful businessman in Chicago, lives here. Picture his carriage turning in at sunset. The house will be torn down in 1955. One more mansard waits at the far end of the row.

== stop armour
This is 2115 Prairie, the Armour house, built in 1871. A sign calls it a grand Second Empire home. It has a patterned slate mansard with iron trim, over an arched door in the middle. It was built for a lumber man, David Kelley. Then it became the home of Philip Armour, the meat packer. He liked to say they used every part of the pig but the squeal. His wife still lives here. The house will be torn down in 1937.

== ending finish
That is the end of the Second Empire walk. Count the mansards you passed. By the 1880s they lined the street, and even old houses bought them.

Then people's tastes changed, and the families moved north. Most of these roofs came down. Only Elbridge Keith's is left. Go back and look at it again before the light goes.
