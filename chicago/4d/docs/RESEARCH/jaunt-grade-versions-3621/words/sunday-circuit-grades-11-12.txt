== premise
A contemplative excursion past the settlement's two churches and its log meeting house, concluding with a social call at the Sauganash.

== opening
JAUNT: A SUNDAY CIRCUIT. A spring Sunday morning. Three years ago this settlement possessed no church whatsoever; then, within a single season, 1833, organized religion arrived from several directions simultaneously: a Catholic priest celebrated mass in one of Mark Beaubien's log cabins, a Presbyterian minister preached in the fort's carpenter's shop, and the Methodists convened in a log room at Wolf Point. Soon the town's trustees will even prohibit "the sale of liquors on Sunday".

A neighbor anticipates your arrival at the Sauganash, and along the route you will pass every place the settlement worships. Observe from the road how dramatically three years have transformed it.

== stop st-marys
St. Mary's, on Lake Street west of State, is the settlement's first Catholic church. Father St. Cyr "celebrated his first mass in a log cabin belonging to Mark Beaubien" in May 1833, and that August Augustine D. Taylor erected this frame, twenty-five by thirty-five feet, its lumber transported by scow across the lake from St. Joseph at $12 a thousand. It cost approximately $400. Inside are rough benches serving as pews; outside there is no paint, no steeple and no tower.

== narrative st-marys
The most unadorned church in town.

== stop presbyterian
Two blocks west, at Lake and Clark, stands the Presbyterian church, dedicated on 4 January 1834. Its pastor, Jeremiah Porter, "the pioneer Protestant minister of Chicago", accompanied the troops from Fort Brady and "preached a sermon in the carpenter's shop of the garrison on the 19th of May, 1833". Until the church's completion he resided and studied upstairs in Peck's store. It also functions as a civic meeting room: School District No. 4 is summoned here for 7 July.

== narrative presbyterian
Sanctuary and civic assembly room combined.

== stop walker
Across the South Branch at Wolf Point, Wau-Bun recalled "a small square log building, originally designed for a school-house, but occasionally used as a place of worship whenever any itinerant minister presented himself". A pioneer account credits Father Jesse Walker, the Methodist missionary, with its construction, and his own log house "served the purpose of a parsonage, parlor, kitchen and audience-room". A frontier preacher compelled a single room to accomplish everything.

== narrative walker
One log room, wherever it actually stood.

== stop the-call
Return across the bridge to Lake and Market and the Sauganash, white with blue shutters in Wau-Bun and newly refurbished according to the Democrat of 10 June 1835. Your neighbor is waiting. On a Sunday the proprietor's fiddle ought to remain silent, although Moses and Kirkland remember Mark Beaubien as "an ardent, if not always devout, Catholic", and "a liberal contributor" to St. Mary's.

== narrative the-call
Arrive where your call is anticipated.

== leg 0
Two blocks west, from the priest's unadorned frame to the minister's church.

== leg 1
Depart the settlement's frame churches for its oldest place of worship.

== leg 2
Cross once more, to the social call for which you departed.

== ending among-neighbors
You reach the Sauganash's threshold with the entirety of the settlement's religious life behind you: a priest's unadorned frame, a minister's church that doubles as a civic hall, and a log room at the forks that accommodated both instruction and worship. Three years ago none of it existed.
