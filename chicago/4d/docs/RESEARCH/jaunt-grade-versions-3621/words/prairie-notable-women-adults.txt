== opening
JAUNT: WOMEN OF PRAIRIE AVENUE. A spring morning, 1904. The men of this street have gone downtown to their stores, banks and packing houses, and their names are on everything: the buildings, the companies, the street directory, the wills. Their wives, sisters and daughters cannot vote for president, and won't for another nine years.

They are busy anyway, and some of them are angry. Six houses on foot, 1608 to 2115 Prairie, north to south, and at each a woman the sources tie to the house: a nurse's champion who broke a club's religious bar, a future detective, a diarist with a silversmith's bench in her basement, a suffragist who has waited thirty years, a fountain-builder and the street's last great lady, left behind.

Begin at the north end, with two sisters who walked into a club that had never let anyone like them in.

== stop frank
1608 Prairie, the Frank household. Henrietta Greenebaum Frank worked year after year for better nursing education at the Illinois Training School for Nurses. In 1876 she and her sister Hannah Greenebaum Solomon became the first Jewish members of the prestigious Chicago Woman's Club. No Jewish woman had been let in before them. Hannah went on to found the National Council of Jewish Women and was its first president. Two sisters, one door forced open. A block south, a young mother is living in a house her parents built for her.

== stop lee
1700 Prairie, built by John and Frances Glessner for their daughter, Frances, who married Blewett Lee in 1898. Right now she is a society wife. Decades from now she will build eighteen tiny, perfect rooms, each one a crime scene in miniature, for students to solve: the Nutshell Studies of Unexplained Death, still used to train investigators. She will help found Harvard's Department of Legal Medicine, and in 1943 she will become the first woman police captain in the United States. The rich girl in the twin house will spend her old age teaching policemen how to read a corpse. Her mother lives one block south, and is probably writing about her right now.

== stop glessner
1800 Prairie, Glessner House. Frances Macbeth Glessner began a journal in 1879 and kept it for forty years, and the house museum still writes the family's story from it: a woman's private record became the family's history. She kept bees. Since 1893 her Monday Morning Reading Class has met in the library, women educating each other every week. And this year, 1904, she has set up a silversmith's workbench in the basement and begun making silver with her own hands, as if to prove a lady can work. Across Eighteenth Street and down the west walk, a neighbour is fighting for something bigger.

== stop jones
1834 Prairie, the Fernando Jones house. Jane Grahame Jones was, in the words of the city's encyclopedia, a longtime and outspoken activist for woman suffrage; her husband's scrapbook keeps clippings on the Illinois Woman Suffrage Association from as early as 1875. She has been at it for nearly thirty years, and she still cannot vote for the men who make the laws she lives under. In 1913 Illinois will become the first state east of the Mississippi to let women vote for president. The house is razed in 1942. Keep walking south, past Twentieth Street, to a family that will give the city a fountain.

== stop buckingham
2036 Prairie, the Buckingham house, where the 1904 Blue Book lists Clarence Buckingham, philanthropist and businessman. His sister was Kate Sturges Buckingham, philanthropist and art patron. In 1927, fourteen years after his death, she will give Chicago the Buckingham Fountain in his memory: twice the size of the Versailles fountain that inspired it, its centre jet throwing water 150 feet into the air. Her money, her brother's name on it. One more house, and the woman who stayed after everyone else had gone.

== stop armour
2115 Prairie, the Kelley-Armour house. The 1904 Blue Book lists Mrs. Philip D. Armour here; the census of 1900 names her Belle, born in Ohio, and the Blue Book does not even print her first name. Her husband, the meatpacker, died in 1901. As the great families died or moved away to suburbs like Lake Forest, she stayed on, and on. The district marker says Armour's widow was among the last residents of the street, dying here in 1927, long after most of her neighbours' houses had been sold or torn down. Her house follows her in 1937.

== ending finish
End of the walk at 2115 Prairie. Six women, six houses. A nurse's champion, a detective, a diarist, a suffragist, a fountain-builder and a widow who would not leave.

They are only the ones the sources name. Behind every one of these fronts lived maids, cooks, nurses and laundresses whose work kept these houses running, and whose names are only in the census. Their stories are waiting too.
