== premise
From a cow barn on Sunday night, 8 October 1871, to the rain on Monday night: twelve stops at their recorded and inferred hours, tracing the Great Chicago Fire across two rivers, through the commercial centre and over the North Side.

== opening
JAUNT: THE GREAT CHICAGO FIRE. Sunday night, 8 October 1871, at the close of a prolonged drought: since the third of July only two and a half inches of rain have fallen on Chicago, a city whose West Side consists overwhelmingly of wooden cottages, sheds, planing mills and lumber yards. A persistent south-westerly wind is blowing, and the fire department is exhausted, because its companies spent last night and much of this afternoon battling a considerable fire beside the river.

Twelve stops follow the conflagration hour by hour, from a cow barn on the West Side, across both branches of the river, to the northern limit of the city, through the night and the smoke-darkened day that followed. Long rides go Instantly; every place keeps its stop.

It begins in a barn on DeKoven Street, at about nine o'clock.

== stop oleary-barn
Sunday 8 October 1871, about nine in the evening, on DeKoven Street. Behind the cottage at No. 137 stands the barn of Patrick and Catherine O'Leary, who keep cows and supply milk to neighbouring families; its loft "had recently been filled with hay". According to legend, Mrs. O'Leary was milking when her cow kicked over a lamp, although the official inquiry, finding the O'Learys in bed, reported "no proof that any person had been in the barn after nightfall that evening." Daniel Sullivan, a drayman opposite, noticed the flames, and although "he has a wooden leg", he hurried across and rescued a calf. William Lee, who lives two doors along, hastens to the drug store for the key to the alarm box, which the druggist refuses to surrender. "The clock struck nine." Across the river, the watchman in the Court House tower has not yet observed the light.

== stop st-pauls
Half past ten, at Clinton and Mather Streets. The alarm was both delayed and misdirected: at 9:28 Mathias Schaffer, the watchman in the Court House tower, identified the glow through his spy-glass and signalled Box 342, which was "nearly a mile from the fire". Only one engine, the Little Giant, arrived promptly, and "For more than thirty minutes this engine battled alone." Then the gale carried the fire over the firemen's heads; Richard Riley, a railroad contractor positioned on a lumber pile, watched "A large mass of fire" go "whirling through the air for more than three blocks" before settling on the cupola of this church, St. Paul's. The church has collapsed, and behind it William B. Bateham's mills, with a thousand cords of kindling, are burning as well. Bateham, an alderman, concludes: "The materials from this mill will fire the South Side, and nothing can prevent it now."

== stop parmelee
Half past eleven, at Jackson and Franklin Streets, across the South Branch of the river. Since half past nine incandescent brands have been descending on this side, and now one has ignited the new stable of the Parmelee Omnibus and Stage Company, "the finest of its kind in the country", completed at a cost of $80,000 and scheduled to be "occupied for the first time on Wednesday". At the stable, the fire "swept down upon them with such rapidity and terrific force as to compel them to flee for their lives." By midnight the gas works on Adams Street are ablaze, together with the shanties of Conley's Patch beside them. Beyond rise the stone and marble blocks of LaSalle Street, a double row of supposedly "fire-proof" commercial buildings, and one observer wondered: "Would these succumb to the shower of brands and the triple-heated furnace which had been thrown about them?"

== stop court-house
Half past one on Monday morning, in the Court House square at the administrative centre of the city. Mathias Schaffer, the watchman whose erroneous alarm dispatched the engines astray, remains at his post in the tower; since ten o'clock sparks have been accumulating on the roof, which has ignited "five or six times", and he has extinguished it with two Babcock fire-extinguishers. In the basement, Mayor Roswell B. Mason has been issuing orders since midnight, requisitioning gunpowder to demolish buildings in the fire's path and directing that the jail be evacuated. Captain Hickey has had the cells unlocked, the prisoners "rushed into the street", and those charged with murder departed in handcuffs. Now the tower itself is burning. Schaffer and his relief, Dennis Denene, set the enormous bell swinging and descend the stairs; it weighs 10,849 pounds, and it is still ringing.

== stop sherman-house
Five past two, at the corner of Clark and Randolph. Behind you the Court House bell comes "crashing and clanging down", and Mayor Mason, who remained until it fell, emerges to discover LaSalle Street "like a furnace, all fire". Across Clark Street stands the Sherman House, seven stories high and accommodating more than three hundred guests, whose awnings men were tearing down hours earlier in an attempt to protect it. A correspondent for the Post recorded the outcome: "for nearly an hour the house held good. Suddenly, a spirit of flame came from a window in the third story on the southern face". Only the night clerk and a single assistant remain to rouse every room, while five ladies lie sick in bed. North of the hotel flows the main river, the final natural barrier between the conflagration and the North Side, and the wind is already transporting it across.

== stop crosbys
Twenty past two, on Washington Street between Dearborn and State. From the Court House the fire has veered eastward toward Crosby's Opera House, opened in 1865 and accommodating 2,500; throughout the summer it remained closed while $80,000 was expended on ornament, upholstery and frescoes. It was scheduled to reopen tomorrow evening with Theodore Thomas and his orchestra, and tonight, "only an hour or two before the fire, it was lit up for the first time, that its effects might be seen by gaslight." Now the roof ignites; "In almost the time it takes to write these lines," wrote James Sheahan and George Upton, two Chicago newspapermen, the most magnificent auditorium in the country was obliterated. The St. James Hotel adjoining it, which "had two or three times before stood the test of fire", collapses simultaneously, and on the far side of the main river the North Side has caught.

== stop north-side
Half past two, north of the main river, where the State Street viaduct descends to Kinzie Street. The river provided no protection: "About the time that the Sherman House was ablaze", the wind conveyed the fire across the water, and it "broke out just north of the bridge, at State street." Now Wright's stable, on this corner, is burning; an hour earlier Judge Lambert Tree crossed the bridge when it "was then burning in spots". Mayor Mason and his son, isolated on the South Side, are detouring by the Wells Street bridge in an attempt to outflank the fire, and will encounter it here before them. North of this corner most families are sleeping, and many will awaken to find themselves "surrounded by fire." Half a mile distant on the lake shore stands the solitary building that pumps the water for the entire city.

== stop water-works
Twenty past three, Chicago Avenue at the lake shore. These are the Water Works: the engine house and, a hundred and fifty feet west of it, the Water Tower, a stone enclosure surrounding an iron column 130 feet high, up which the pumps propel water for the whole city. The engineer, Frank Trautman, has his engines operating at thirty million gallons a day. D. W. Fuller observes "a fire-brand, apparently twelve feet in length," revolving through the air toward the tower until it strikes the corner of the engine house. "In an instant, the roof was aflame." The roof is constructed of wood, and Fuller consults his watch: "exactly twenty minutes past three." A few minutes before four, the intense heat expels the men. The tower will survive, but the engines will not, and henceforth the firemen can operate only where they can draw water from the river.

== stop tribune
Half past seven on Monday morning, at Dearborn and Madison Streets, in a dim and smoky daylight. On this corner stands the Tribune building, which its proprietors consider fireproof. At daybreak William Bross, a former lieutenant governor of Illinois and one of the newspaper's owners, rode here and "found everything safe", and when fire crept beneath the sidewalk into the barber's shop in the basement, Bross extinguished it with his partners, Joseph Medill, one of the principal stockholders, and Horace White, the editor, "with water and one of Babcock's fire-extinguishers." The printing forms descended for the morning edition. Then the fire returned from the south, past the immense Palmer House, to McVicker's Theatre across a narrow alley. "The iron shutters on the alley side, bent by the fire, sprung out of place". The Tribune is destroyed, and without the Water Works nothing can impede what follows.

== stop state-street
Eight o'clock, State Street at Washington. This store of white Massachusetts marble belongs to Potter Palmer, the developer responsible for the Palmer House hotel several blocks south, who leases it to Field, Leiter and Co., importers of dry goods, for $52,000 annually. At first light the company's employees were "showering that massive pile of pure marble and iron with water from their own pumping engines." Now the streams "had ceased to run", because the Water Works have failed, and men are transferring merchandise from the lower floor into waiting wagons. The fire advances "from the north and eastwardly in the rear, and it soon yielded." Farther along State Street the Palmer House is already a ruin, and on the North Side the conflagration has been advancing continuously since half past two.

== stop ogden-house
Midday on Monday, on the North Side, before the house of Mahlon D. Ogden, brother of William B. Ogden, Chicago's first mayor; it is constructed entirely of wood, and it is still standing. Before it lies Washington Park, which filled overnight with refugees and their possessions until the fire swept through and consumed "the accumulated furniture of fifty families." The men remaining inside defended it, tearing up the carpets, spreading them across the walls and keeping them saturated, and when the hydrant failed, "Several barrels of cider were used to saturate the carpets". Surrounding it now there is "nothing but this solitary building in an area of 2,600 acres." A mile north the fire is advancing on St. Michael's, the immense brick church of the German parish, whose steeple it will burn, while the population of the North Side retreats toward the lake and the old cemetery.

== stop old-cemetery
Nightfall on Monday, at the old City Cemetery on the southern edge of Lincoln Park, from which most of the dead were removed years ago, leaving "some old headstones scattered about" among the grass and small oaks. "By nightfall on Monday there could not have been less than thirty thousand men, women, and children, huddled within this ghostly inclosure." They carried in beds, chairs and bundles, and many have watched their possessions "burn up before their eyes." Farther south along the shore, "Along the beach were judges, merchants, doctors, workingmen, women and children", and "Ladies were seen moving their chairs into the water, and sitting on the top of the backs". Thousands more have escaped westward to the open prairie. Everywhere they are "blinded by smoke, weakened by hunger, and choking with thirst", and nobody can predict where the conflagration will finally terminate.

== ending the-rain
Monday night, 9 October 1871. At approximately half past ten the fire reaches the northern extremity of Lincoln Park and consumes the house of Dr. John H. Foster on Fullerton Avenue, "just twenty-five hours from the commencement of the fire, and about four miles distant from the place of its origin." There it finally "spent itself", and then "About eleven o'clock Monday night, the sky, as if in remorse at what the fiery element had done, poured down floods of rain." In the cemetery and on the prairie the refugees stand in it, drenched. The eventual accounting: 2,124 acres burned, 17,450 buildings destroyed, 98,500 people homeless, and "more than 250" dead. The mayor's first proclamation pledged the city's credit "for the necessary expenses for the relief of the suffering", and concluded: "It is believed the fire has spent its force and all will soon be well."
