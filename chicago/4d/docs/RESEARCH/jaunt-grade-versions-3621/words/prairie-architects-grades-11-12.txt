== premise
An autumn afternoon with the architects who reconstructed Chicago: Jenney, Richardson, Cobb and Frost, Burnham and Root, Beman and Hunt, concluding where Burnham encountered his future wife.

== opening
JAUNT: ARCHITECTS. An autumn afternoon on Prairie Avenue, 1904, the declining sun raking across masonry. Thirty-three years ago Chicago burned; it reconstructed itself as an audacious modern metropolis, and numerous architects responsible for that reconstruction designed residences along this avenue, for clients whose resources were effectively unlimited.

Seven residences on foot, from 1701 to 2100 Prairie: the engineer who constructed the first skyscraper, a colossus from Boston, the partnership of Cobb and Frost, the youthful Burnham and Root, the designer of an entire company town, the architect of the Statue of Liberty's pedestal, and the commission that launched Daniel Burnham's career. He married his client's daughter.

Commence at 1701, with a restrained Italianate façade by a profoundly unrestrained engineer.

== stop jenney
1701 Prairie, William G. Hibbard's residence of 1869, commissioned by the president of a substantial wholesale hardware enterprise. Its architect, William Le Baron Jenney, trained as an engineer, and fifteen years later he constructed the Home Insurance Building downtown: "The building was the first fully metal-framed building and is considered the first skyscraper". His drafting room educated a generation, as Louis Sullivan, Daniel Burnham, William Holabird and Martin Roche "performed their architectural apprenticeships on Jenney's staff". Here he provided Hibbard a sober Italianate composition, a low hipped roof on bracketed eaves. Appropriately, from 1921 its owners will grant Sullivan a complimentary office here until his death in 1924; in 1932 the residence will be demolished. Proceed to the corner, and a radically different variety of genius.

== stop richardson
1800 Prairie, Henry Hobson Richardson's residence for John J. Glessner. Glessner recalled his architect weighing 370 pounds: "big-minded, big-bodied, big-hearted". Three weeks before his death, too debilitated to leave his bedroom, Richardson indicated the positions of the light fixtures and declared, "There, Mr. Glessner if I were to live five years longer, that is the last thing I would do on your house, my part is finished". He died on 26 April 1886; construction commenced on 1 June, and Norcross Brothers completed it for the contracted price without a single additional dollar. The neighbors were appalled, and George Pullman complained about "that thing staring me in the face". Now examine the adjacent residence.

== stop cobb-frost
1808 Prairie, adjacent, designed by Cobb and Frost for Osborn R. Keith. The construction notice of 1886 reads like an invoice: a three-story dwelling, 38 by 80 feet, with a granite façade, costing forty thousand dollars. It rose during the same year that Richardson's drawings next door were being realized, granite responding to granite in an architectural dialogue. When it is demolished in 1968, its north wall will be preserved to maintain the privacy of the Glessner courtyard. Two doors south stands the work of the firm that would ultimately outbuild every competitor.

== stop burnham-root
1812 Prairie, George H. Wheeler's residence of 1884 by Burnham and Root: a picturesque brick row house in the Dutch manner, its façade extended upward as a tall shaped gable above a stone ground story. It will be demolished in 1968. Within a decade Burnham would direct the construction of the 1893 World's Columbian Exposition, the "White City". His celebrated maxim: "Make no little plans. They have no magic to stir men's blood and probably will not themselves be realized". Cross the avenue to a chateau by an architect who once planned an entire municipality.

== stop beman
1801 Prairie, Solon S. Beman's chateau of 1890 for the piano manufacturer William W. Kimball, executed in the French Chateauesque idiom with precipitous slate and a cylindrical tower. Beman was merely in his twenties when he "received a commission from George Pullman to design what would become the nation's first planned company town", Pullman, south of the city, an experiment in paternalistic urbanism. He additionally designed the palm room and conservatory for Pullman's own mansion farther up the avenue. Proceed south to the avenue's most renowned name and its most renowned architect.

== stop hunt
1905 Prairie, Marshall Field's mansion, designed by Richard Morris Hunt of New York. "Hunt was the first American to be admitted to the school of architecture at the École des Beaux-Arts" in Paris, and he designed "the pedestal of the Statue of Liberty" and Biltmore, "America's largest private house". For Chicago's wealthiest citizen he constructed a broad brick and stone mansion beneath a mansard; it will conclude its existence as an aeronautical university before being demolished in 1955. One final stop, at 2100, where a young architect encountered his wife.

== stop sherman
2100 Prairie, John B. Sherman's residence of 1874, the first of nearly a dozen Burnham and Root commissions along this avenue. Sherman administered the Union Stock Yards. During construction, his daughter Margaret married the young architect Daniel Burnham, in 1876, whereupon Sherman began advising his neighbors whom to engage. That is how a firm acquires an entire avenue: the client becomes one's father-in-law, and the father-in-law becomes one's most persuasive salesman. Burnham and Root consequently became the most prolific architects on Prairie Avenue. The residence where it originated will be demolished in 1936.

== ending finish
The walk concludes at 2100 Prairie. These seven residences constitute a sketchbook of the architects who reconstructed Chicago after the fire. Within several years Burnham will extend the lessons of this avenue to the entire metropolis through his 1909 Plan of Chicago, "the first comprehensive plan for the controlled growth of an American city".

Watch for their other façades as you explore. Every one is signed in stone.
