== opening
JAUNT: ACROSS WOLF POINT. Daybreak in spring, the river running high and brown, and the town still sleeping off last night. This is where Chicago began: the forks, where the North and South Branches meet and run east to the lake as one river. Before there were streets there were taverns here, and the first bridges, and a ferryman who also played the fiddle, kept the light, and was, as two historians put it, 'the fun of every funeral and the life of every wake'.

Cross the river the way the town did: from the tavern that named Wolf Point, down the west bank past the Green Tree, over the South Branch bridge, and up to the Sauganash. Four stops, nothing to buy, and every vice and virtue of the early town within a few hundred yards.

== stop the-fork
Start on the west bank at the tavern that named Wolf Point, partly log and partly frame, a bar and beds at the edge of the water. Its sign was made by an army officer, Lieutenant Allen, from a piece of a box, with a wolf painted on it. Whether it still keeps travellers is disputed: one account has William Walters keeping it until 1836, another says it stopped in 1834. Look east. The two branches meet off this bank and run to the lake as one river, Every cargo bound inland passed this point.

== stop west-bank
South along the bank to the Green Tree, at Lake and West Water, which James Kinzie built in 1833 as the first house here put up for hotel purposes, 'so called from an oak tree standing close by'. Old settlers recalled Chester Ingersoll keeping it from 1834 to 1837. In a town where buildings went up in a week and burned or fell in a year, it is the great survivor: sixty years on it still stands, moved to Milwaukee Avenue, 'the oldest memento of early taverns'. Everyone who drank in it is long dead by then; the walls outlived them all.

== stop the-crossing
A few steps south, the South Branch bridge between Lake and Randolph. Moses and Kirkland say it 'at first consisted of floating logs tied together', the kind of crossing that tips a drunk man into the river on a dark night. Four old settlers signed that it stood on log abutments and two bents, about ten feet wide and six feet above the water, high enough for teams to pass under it on the ice. It was paid for by subscription, $486.20, 'of which the Indians of the vicinity contributed $200'. Weigh that sum against who would profit most from the town it joined.

== stop south-side
Off the bridge and north to Lake and Market: the Sauganash, white, two-storey, with bright-blue wooden shutters, which the Democrat of 10 June 1835 found newly fitted up. Moses and Kirkland call it 'the most attractive hostlery the city possessed'. Its landlord, Mark Beaubien, was 'fiddler, landlord, merchant, ferryman and a patron of horse racing', a man who made money from every appetite the town had. In winter the town raced its horses on the frozen river you just crossed.

== ending crossed
You have crossed between the divisions as the town did, by the bridge and not the water, from the oldest tavern corner to the most famous one, past the drink, the deals and the debts that built them. Come back when the river freezes, for the races, and bring money you can afford to lose.
