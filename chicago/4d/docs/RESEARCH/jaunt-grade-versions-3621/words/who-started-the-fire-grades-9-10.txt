== premise
Sunday night, 8 October 1871: fire in the O'Leary barn, and the cow takes the blame. Weigh the O'Learys, their neighbours, the watchman, the official inquiry, a boastful reporter and the later investigators, and reach your own verdict. Eight stops.

== opening
JAUNT: WHO STARTED THE FIRE? Sunday night, 8 October 1871, DeKoven Street on the West Side. Everyone knows the story: Mrs. O'Leary's cow kicked over a lamp and burned Chicago down. Yet the official inquiry found no proof of it, a reporter later claimed that he and his friends had invented it, and in 1997 the City Council formally cleared the cow.

So who did it? Eight stops: the barn, the yard, the party next door, the watchman in the Court House tower, the inquiry, the legend, the other suspects and the verdict of 1997. Every witness is heard in their own words, and every account is weighed. Start across the street from the barn, at the moment the fire is discovered.

== stop the-barn
DeKoven Street, about half past nine on Sunday night, and flames are pouring from the hayloft of the barn behind No. 137, the cottage of Patrick and Catherine O'Leary. Daniel Sullivan, a drayman from across the street, later nicknamed 'Peg Leg', has just crossed over, cried 'Fire, fire,' and gone into the barn, where he found 'the hay in the loft on fire'. He tried desperately to cut the animals loose, and rescued only 'a half-burned calf'. While the city was still burning, someone claimed Mrs. O'Leary had been milking when her cow 'kicked over a lighted lamp'. That story travelled around the world, and it has outlasted every attempt to disprove it. Was it true? Begin with the woman who owned the cow.

== stop catherine
In the yard behind the cottage, Catherine O'Leary, who milks five cows and sells the milk to her neighbours, is watching everything she owns go up in flames. Later she told the inquiry: 'I was in bed myself and my husband and five children when this fire commenced.' Two men came to the door, and her husband Patrick ran back to the bedroom and said 'Kate the barn is afire.' A neighbour, Dennis Rogan, supported her account: at half past eight he had found the couple already in bed, and she explained it was because of a 'sore foot'. In a sworn statement a week later, the O'Learys said she milked at 4:30 and fed the horse at about seven, and 'She had no lighted lamp in the house or barn that evening.' But in the front of this cottage, there was a party.

== stop the-party
At the front of No. 137, on DeKoven Street, the O'Learys rent two rooms to the McLaughlin family, and tonight they have company: young people celebrating 'the arrival of a cousin from Ireland'. Mr. McLaughlin plays the fiddle, and through the wall Catherine O'Leary heard it: 'There was a little music there.' Afterwards a neighbour across the street, Mrs. White, told her that someone from the party had slipped out to the barn to milk her cows. Why? 'Some said it was for oysters.' Catharine McLaughlin swore that no one from her family went to the barn, and that everyone was inside when the fire broke out. They drank beer, Andreas wrote, 'but had no whisky or milk punch'. More than a mile away, in the Court House tower, the watchman has noticed a glow.

== stop the-alarm
In the Court House cupola, at 9:28 by his own account, the watchman Mathias Schaffer sees the light of a fire to the southwest. Through his spy-glass he judges it to be near Canalport Avenue and Halsted Street, and signals the operator below, William J. Brown, to strike Box 342. That box is 'a point in the direction of the fire, but a mile beyond it.' Soon Schaffer realises the fire is closer, yet no corrected alarm is sent, and engines that would have answered it remain in their houses. The historian A. T. Andreas called the error 'a fatal mistake'. Something else is puzzling: nobody near the barn pulled an alarm box at all. The inquiry noticed too: 'If any person set the fire, either by accident or design, he was careful not to give the alarm.'

== stop the-inquiry
Back on DeKoven Street, weeks later: on 23 November 1871 the Board of Police and Fire Commissioners opened an inquiry into how the fire began, and about fifty witnesses testified. Catherine O'Leary was the star witness. She had lost her cows, her horse and her wagon, and 'Never had five cents insurance'. The Board found that the fire started in the O'Leary barn, and could go no further: 'There is no proof that any person had been in the barn after nightfall that evening.' A spark from a chimney, or a person? 'we are unable to determine.' The report was criticised, not for clearing her, but for concealing the Fire Department's failures. Andreas dismissed it as 'little more than an attempt to quiet' the complaints. Mrs. O'Leary was cleared on paper. The story was another matter.

== stop the-cow
The alley behind the barn. While the city still burned, 'some now-forgotten tongue' claimed Mrs. O'Leary had been milking when the cow kicked over a lamp. Cartoonists portrayed her as a comic, hapless figure, and later painters named the cow Daisy. The Chicago History Museum explains why the story stuck: she could be made to stand for 'careless building, sloppy conduct, and a shiftless immigrant underclass', and blaming her drew on 'anti-Catholic, anti-immigrant, and possibly anti-female' prejudice. Then Michael Ahern, who had covered the fire for the Chicago Republican, boasted in the Tribune that he and two friends had invented the whole thing. The museum dates his boast to the fire's fortieth anniversary; other accounts say 1893. A colleague later claimed he had ghost-written Ahern's article. If not the cow, then who?

== stop other-suspects
Across the street, a few lots east, where Daniel Sullivan said he was sitting. In 1997 a lawyer and amateur historian, Richard Bales, tested his story against the land records. From here, he argued, another house would have blocked the view, and a man with a wooden leg could not have covered the 193 feet to the barn as quickly as Sullivan claimed. Bales concluded that Sullivan started the fire himself, perhaps while feeding his own cow. A Tribune writer, Anthony DeBartolo, pointed instead to Louis M. Cohn, whose bequest to Northwestern University in 1944 arrived with a story: he, Mrs. O'Leary's son and other boys were 'shooting dice in the hayloft' when one knocked over a lantern. Some even blame a meteor. The historian Carl Smith calls them all 'interesting, but ultimately unprovable theory'.

== stop the-council
The corner of Jefferson and DeKoven, today. By the 1880s the O'Leary cottage was gone, and since 1961 the Chicago Fire Academy, where the city trains its firefighters, has occupied the site, with a sculpture of a flame beside its entrance. In 1971, the fire's centennial, the city's business association cleared Mrs. O'Leary and blamed Sullivan instead. Here, in October 1997, a City Council committee held a hearing, half serious and half playful. Aldermen William Beavers and Edward Burke decided that she and her cow 'had been unfairly maligned', and the full council passed a resolution clearing them of 'all blame'. Her great-great-granddaughter, Nancy Knight Connolly, said: 'We always knew that she was innocent'. The city had delivered its verdict. The evidence, however, had not changed.

== ending your-verdict
So who started the fire? Here is the case as it stands, account by account.

Catherine O'Leary swore that she was in bed, a neighbour found her there at half past eight complaining of a sore foot, the inquiry accepted her testimony, and in 1997 the City Council formally cleared both her and her cow.

Someone from the McLaughlin party may have slipped out for milk, as Mrs. White had heard, although Catharine McLaughlin swore that nobody did.

Daniel Sullivan was the first person into the barn; Richard Bales argues that his testimony does not fit the property records, while Sullivan himself swore that he saw the fire from across the street and ran in to rescue the animals.

Louis Cohn's story of a dice game emerged only after his death, and his own will never mentions it.

A spark from a chimney, on 'that windy night', remains entirely possible.

The Board could not decide, and neither could Andreas. 'It is highly unlikely that we will ever have definitive knowledge of the cause of the fire.' Who do you think it was, and what evidence would it take to be certain?
