== premise
A summer evening on Millionaire's Row: Pullman, Glessner, Kimball, the two Marshall Fields and Armour, and how each one got rich in one lifetime.

== opening
JAUNT: FAMOUS NAMES. It is a summer evening on Prairie Avenue in 1904. The lamplighters have lit the street lamps. The carriages are coming home from downtown. Behind these walls live some of the richest families in America. People call this street Millionaire's Row. At one time, sixteen of the city's sixty top business leaders lived here.

There are six houses on foot, from 1729 to 2115 Prairie, going south. First is the man who let people sleep on trains. Next is a man who made farm machines and lived in a stone fort. Then come the piano king, the big store owner and his son. Last is the meat man who used every part of the pig. Each of them got rich in Chicago in one lifetime.

Start at the corner of Eighteenth Street, at the biggest house on the block.

== stop pullman
This is 1729 Prairie, the Pullman house. Inside there is a theater with about two hundred seats, a pipe organ and a bowling alley. George Pullman got rich young by lifting Chicago out of its mud. He jacked up whole buildings about six feet without breaking them. Then he built railroad sleeping cars. When President Lincoln died, his body rode home in one, and huge crowds lined the tracks. Pullman is dead now, and many of his workers were angry with him. His wife still lives here. The house will be torn down in 1922. Pullman hated one thing on this street. It is right behind you.

== stop glessner
This is 1800 Prairie, John Glessner's house. Pullman said he did not know what he had done to have "that thing staring me in the face" every time he went out. Mr. Glessner got rich making farm machines. Two years ago his company joined a giant new one called International Harvester. His architect died just weeks before the building began. The family moved in at the end of 1887 and still lives here. Many years later, people will save this house from being torn down. Across the street, listen for pianos.

== stop kimball
This is 1801 Prairie, the Kimball house. It looks like a French castle, with a very steep slate roof and a round tower. William Kimball came to Chicago in 1857 with only four pianos to sell. Later his factory made about a hundred pianos and organs every week. This is his last summer. He will die here on 16 December 1904. Now walk one block south to the richest man in the city.

== stop field
This is 1905 Prairie, Marshall Field's house. A famous New York architect designed it. Field came from the East. He became the richest and strongest businessman in Chicago. His store's rule was "Give the lady what she wants", and the ladies shopped there and made him rich. He and George Pullman were among the first rich men to buy land here, and the others followed. His house will be torn down in 1955. Next year his world will fall apart, and it starts two doors south.

== stop field-jr
This is 1919 Prairie, the house of Marshall Field Jr. He bought it in 1890 and kept making it bigger. Two years ago builders added a huge new part. Upstairs live his children, like ten-year-old Marshall Field III. In November 1905 the father will be shot in this house. Two months later the grandfather will die too, after catching a bad cold on a golf course. The boy will grow up in England. For now, the lights are on. One more house is at the end of the walk.

== stop armour
This is 2115 Prairie, the Armour house. The Armour family lived here for almost fifty years. Philip Armour went west during the gold rush. He did not dig for gold. He hired miners to build water ditches. Back in Chicago he built Armour and Company, the biggest food company in the world. He liked to say they used every part of the pig but the squeal. In 1893 he gave a million dollars to start a college. He died in 1901. His wife still lives here. The house will be torn down in 1937.

== leg 0
Across Eighteenth Street. The Battle of Fort Dearborn of 1812 was fought in the sand hills under this end of the street.

== leg 1
Across the street to the tower. Kimball's castle faces Glessner's fortress.

== leg 2
South past more big houses. By 1886 the finest houses in the city stood on Prairie Avenue, each with its own carriage house.

== leg 3
Two doors south: father and son lived side by side.

== leg 4
On past Twentieth Street, down the long sidewalk to the last great house.

== ending finish
The walk ends here, at 2115 Prairie. Mrs. Armour stayed. She was one of the last rich people on the street, and she died here in 1927. Most of the others had already gone. A rich lady named Bertha Palmer built a castle by the lake, to the north. Then the other rich families moved north too. One by one, these houses became rooming houses, storehouses and parking lots.

A few houses survived. Glessner's fortress still stands, and so does Kimball's castle. Come back after dark some night, when the street tells its ghost stories.
