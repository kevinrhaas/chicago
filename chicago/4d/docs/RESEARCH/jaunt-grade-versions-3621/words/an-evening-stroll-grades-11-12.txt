== opening
JAUNT: AN EVENING STROLL. Dusk on 1 July 1835: the land speculators have retired to supper, the river has subsided into quiet, and for once there is nothing you are obliged to accomplish. Chicago has existed as an incorporated town for less than two years, and at its incorporation in August 1833, only "thirteen votes were cast by the twenty-eight legal electors". Now strangers disembark by the boatload, a transformation of almost bewildering velocity.

Undertake a leisurely circuit of the familiar streets, from the Sauganash, past the village's first frame store and the corner where its inaugural newspaper was printed, to the hotel where the town convenes its meetings. Then determine where the evening concludes.

== stop the-sauganash
Commence at the Sauganash, on the corner of Lake and Market above the South Branch, white and two stories tall with bright-blue shutters and newly refurbished this June. Mark Beaubien constructed it, and his career in this town has been extraordinarily varied. Moses and Kirkland characterize him as "the fun of every funeral and the life of every wake", cataloguing him "As fiddler, landlord, merchant, ferryman and a patron of horse racing". In May 1833 Father St. Cyr "celebrated his first mass in a log cabin belonging to Mark Beaubien".

== narrative the-sauganash
Set out from a public house's threshold.

== stop the-shopfront
Cross to the river and proceed east along South Water Street to LaSalle and P. F. W. Peck's store, the first two-story frame structure in the village, constructed of lumber "sawed from black walnut trees". Peck retails dry goods, hardware, groceries, crockery, boots, shoes and hats, and he is a conspicuously prudent man. Years hence he will be credited "with having $40,000 in cash locked in an iron safe in his dwelling", and regarded as "nearly as wealthy as John Jacob Astor", an extraordinary reputation for a frontier storekeeper.

== narrative the-shopfront
A shopfront on the commercial thoroughfare.

== stop the-printing-office
One block east, at South Water and Clark, John Calhoun commenced printing the Chicago Democrat in 1833; by 20 May its office had relocated above Jones and King's hardware along the street. It appears on Wednesdays, and today's issue is No. 11 of its second volume. When the mail arrived, the town possessed an alternative means of disseminating news: "some one with a good voice would mount a dry goods box" and read it aloud, and whenever political passions intensified, there were two boxes and two rival readers.

== narrative the-printing-office
Where the settlement's newspaper originated.

== stop the-exchange
South to Lake Street and west to Wells stands the Exchange Coffee House, another of Mark Beaubien's constructions, which Mr. and Mrs. Murphy have operated as a hotel since August 1834. The town's Democrats assembled here in April and again in June to nominate their candidates, and a dancing master registered his pupils' addresses here last winter, evidence of the genteel aspirations of a raw settlement. Determine where the evening concludes.
