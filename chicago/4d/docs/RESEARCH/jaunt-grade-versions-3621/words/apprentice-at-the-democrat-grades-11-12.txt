== premise
Play the Chicago Democrat's newest apprentice on publication day, 1 July 1835: determine where the paper circulates first and where curiosity detains you, and return to the printing office with an account of the morning shaped entirely by your own decisions.

== opening
JAUNT: APPRENTICE AT THE DEMOCRAT. Wednesday, 1 July 1835, and you are thirteen years old. Six weeks ago the Chicago Democrat printed an advertisement: 'Wanted immediately, an apprentice at this office to the printing business'. You responded, and the printing office engaged you. Your ordinary duties consist of sweeping, inking the type and folding the printed sheets; on Wednesdays, when the weekly edition comes off the press, you distribute it through a settlement that seems perceptibly larger every week.

Today's number is folded and tucked beneath your arm. Where it circulates first, and where you permit yourself to linger, depends entirely on you. The locations and the newspapers are authentic; the apprentice is your invention, and you are the apprentice.

== stop old-corner
At this intersection of South Water and Clark, John Calhoun printed the inaugural number of the Democrat on 26 November 1833. The office has subsequently relocated: by May its colophon situated it upstairs over Jones & King's hardware establishment, a short distance along South Water Street, which is where you are now employed. The newspaper appears every Wednesday, and today's issue is No. 11 of its second volume. It also confronts competition. Since 8 June the community has sustained a second weekly, the Chicago American, published on Saturday mornings.

== narrative old-corner
Westward to the river, or eastward toward the competition?

== label old-corner west-first
Westward to the Sauganash

== consequence old-corner west-first
The river end of the settlement receives the news before anyone else.

== label old-corner east-first
Investigate the competition

== consequence old-corner east-first
You will examine the competing paper before anybody has seen yours.

== stop sauganash
Westward along the river to Lake and Market, where the branches converge, stands the Sauganash. Juliette Kinzie remembered 'a pretentious white two-story building, with bright-blue wooden shutters'. Your own newspaper inspected it this June and reported it repaired, fitted up and furnished with neatness and taste. Moses and Kirkland describe it as 'the most attractive hostlery the city possessed', and remember Mark Beaubien as 'the fiddler par excellence of the town'. Before Chicago possessed a newspaper, information arrived irregularly with the mail, and somebody with a good voice declaimed it standing on 'a dry goods box'. Now it arrives beneath your arm.

== narrative sauganash
Where the settlement's travellers congregate to read the news.

== stop rival
Eastward along South Water Street, near the drawbridge, is the office of the Chicago American. T. O. Davis established it on 8 June, so it is scarcely three weeks old. Its most recent number, of 27 June, carries a druggist's advertisement for the Fire King's Specific, a cholera elixir promoted as a certain preventive and cure. The Democrat's own number of 24 June had assembled the cholera intelligence from Alton and Springfield, including a Springfield paper and the Alton Spectator, pronounced Chicago uncommonly healthy, and promised to publish the facts should the disease appear. Two newspapers in one small community, each scrutinizing the other every week.

== narrative rival
Where the competing newspaper is composed and printed.

== stop the-wharf
Onward to the river and Newberry & Dole's warehouse. The partnership advertised in the Democrat's inaugural issue as forwarding and commission merchants, and its card appears in today's number as well, on page four: storage, forwarding and commission merchants, agents for the Merchants' Line, wholesale and retail dealers in dry goods, groceries and provisions. When the schooner Illinois, the first vessel through the newly excavated harbour cut, ascended the river on 12 July 1834, she was cheered at their wharf. Which bank the warehouse occupied remains disputed. A few copies remain, and the morning is advancing.

== narrative the-wharf
Linger beside the river, or complete the distribution?

== label the-wharf watch-sails
Await a sail

== consequence the-wharf watch-sails
You will observe whatever ascends the river and carry it back in your memory.

== label the-wharf finish-round
Hurry to the Tremont

== consequence the-wharf finish-round
The hotel's readers receive their newspaper before noon.

== stop the-draw
Eastward to the foot of Dearborn Street and the settlement's first drawbridge, which the ship-carpenter Nelson R. Norton began constructing in March 1834. Moses and Kirkland describe it as '300 feet in length, by sixteen in width', with 'a double draw'; Andreas records an opening of approximately sixty feet for passing craft, with gallows frames at either end of the draw. Vessels repeatedly collided with it, and it was paid for repairs in September 1834 and mended again this year. Every sail destined for the upriver wharves must negotiate this gap, and a newspaper requires intelligence about each one. Remain on the south bank.

== narrative the-draw
Where the river is crossed by pedestrians and navigated by vessels.

== stop the-tremont
Upward to Lake and Dearborn and the Tremont House, the frame hotel Alanson Sweet constructed in 1833, managed since 1834 by Ira Couch. Its brick successor of 1850 would be considered so 'absurdly large' that people nicknamed it Couch's Folly. Last week's Democrat announced a new auction and commission house on South Water Street, with its first public sale scheduled for 'the 1st day of July'. That is today, and the hotel's guests will undoubtedly appreciate directions.

== narrative the-tremont
Where the final copies are delivered.

== leg 0
The river end of the settlement comes first.

== leg 1
Keep the bundle securely tucked: not a single copy belongs to the competition.

== leg 3
Back past your own office, running.

== ending two-papers
Back up the stairs over Jones & King's, with ink on your fingers and two newspapers' worth of intelligence in your head: what the American is advertising, and what ascended the river through the draw. Nobody instructed you to examine either. A perceptive apprentice notices regardless, and eventually you may become the person who writes such intelligence up.

== ending the-river-news
Back to the office with empty arms and the river occupying your thoughts: the Sauganash supplied first, the sails at the draw observed last of all. The Democrat appears again next Wednesday, and whatever arrived on the river this week will require reporting.

== ending sale-day
Every copy delivered, and both newspapers consulted before noon: the American's elixir at one extremity of the morning, the auction house's inaugural public sale at the other. Upstairs over Jones & King's there is type to clean and an entire week's columns to fill before next Wednesday.

== ending the-round-done
The distribution is complete: the Sauganash first, the Tremont last, and every copy delivered appropriately before noon. Upstairs over Jones & King's, next week's type awaits composition. Six weeks ago you answered an advertisement in the newspaper; this morning the entire settlement read the newspaper you carried.
