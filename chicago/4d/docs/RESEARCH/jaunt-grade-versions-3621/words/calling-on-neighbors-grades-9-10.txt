== premise
Make a few imagined calls: present a letter of introduction from Brown's boarding house, read the Peck household on its card, locate where the town assembles at the Exchange, and leave a calling card.

== opening
JAUNT: CALLING ON NEIGHBORS. You have taken a room in town this summer and carry two letters of introduction, which in 1835 is how a stranger becomes a neighbor. Chicago is emphatically a town of strangers, and the Democrat found them "crowding every room of our public houses": everybody is new, everybody is introducing themselves, and everybody leaves a card.

Present a letter, read a merchant's household, discover where the town meets, and leave your card. Come winter, this town dances, and at one ball Charles Hoffman saw "a veteran officer in full uniform balancing to a tradesman's daughter", but in summer it conducts business. Mind your manners accordingly.

== stop the-room
Begin behind Peck's store at Mrs Rufus Brown's log boarding house, which Andreas names among the first-class boarding houses of the town. You lodge here, carrying two letters of introduction from home. In a town where nearly everyone arrived within the last three years, a letter from someone back east is how residents learn who you are and whether you can be trusted. Choose which introduction to present first.

== narrative the-room
Present one letter first; the other can wait for a later occasion.

== label the-room merchant-letter
A letter from a merchant back east

== consequence the-room merchant-letter
It introduces you to the town's commercial community.

== label the-room minister-letter
A letter from your minister at home

== consequence the-room minister-letter
It introduces you to the town's religious congregations.

== stop the-merchant
A few steps around to Peck's corner of South Water and LaSalle. P. F. W. Peck arrived in July 1831 with a stock of goods, and when the Reverend Jeremiah Porter came in 1833 Peck provided him "a lodging place and study in the second story of his store building", where "one of the earliest Sunday-schools organized in the town" assembled. This is also the year Peck marries Mary K. Wythe of Philadelphia. Read the household from its card rather than knocking.

== narrative the-merchant
Read the household at its card, not at its door.

== stop the-exchange
West to Lake and Wells and the Exchange Coffee House, kept by Mr and Mrs Murphy since August 1834. The town's Democrats met here in April and again in June 1835 to nominate their candidates, and in 1834 the dancing master J. A. Marshall requested that prospective pupils leave their addresses here. A public house functions simultaneously as post box, notice board and meeting hall. Leave your card, or carry it onward.

== narrative the-exchange
Where the town meets, a card is found.

== label the-exchange card-at-exchange
Leave your card at the Exchange

== consequence the-exchange card-at-exchange
Left where the 1834 notice asked addresses to be left, although no caller is recorded.

== label the-exchange card-kept
Keep it for the Sauganash

== consequence the-exchange card-kept
The card continues with you, unopened, to the final call of the afternoon.

== stop the-sauganash
Along Lake Street to Market stands the Sauganash, newly fitted up in June and, according to Moses and Kirkland, "the most attractive hostlery the city possessed". A week after the refit the Democrat found strangers crowding every room in town, and every one of them, like you, is merely a name on a card. A card still in your pocket is left here.

== narrative the-sauganash
A town of strangers, all introducing themselves to one another.

== leg 0
Letter in hand, continue around to the front of the block.

== leg 1
With one household read, continue to where the town gathers.

== ending merchant-at-the-exchange
You have presented a merchant's letter and left your card at the Exchange, where the Democrats meet and the dancing master collected his names. By next week the appropriate people will know exactly who you are.

== ending minister-at-the-exchange
You have presented a minister's letter and left your card at the Exchange, where the town comes to meet. With luck, a congregation will call on you before Sunday.

== ending card-at-the-sauganash
Your card is left at the Sauganash, among a summer's crowd of strangers. Within a year most of them will be neighbors, and a few of them will be wealthy.

== keepsake text
A fictional calling card from July 1835. No real introduction, caller or reply is written on it, and no named person is met.
