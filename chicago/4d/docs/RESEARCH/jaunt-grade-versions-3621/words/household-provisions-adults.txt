== opening
JAUNT: STOCK THE HOUSEHOLD. You have just found a share of a room at a boarding house behind Peck's store, and two dollars to begin a cupboard of your own, which in this town is not much more than a promise. When the Democrat started in 1833, flour was $6 a barrel, butter 17 cents a pound, coffee 17, loaf sugar 20, tea anything up to $1.25, and 'whiskey 40 cents a gallon'. A pound of good tea cost more than three gallons of whiskey. You can guess which one the town drank more of.

Four stops, all on one block of South Water Street: decide what the list is for, then fill it at the grocer's, the druggist's and Peck's corner, and try to make two dollars last.

== stop the-room
Start behind Peck's store at Mrs Rufus Brown's log boarding house, where you have a share of a room with someone you met last week. Andreas names her as keeper of one of the first-class boarding houses, first-class by this town's standard. A share of a room is not a kitchen, and a shelf is not a lock, so whatever you keep must be small, and you must trust your room-mate with it. Decide what your short list is for.

== stop provisions
West along the block to Jones's grocery and provision store. His November 1833 advertisement keeps 'tea, coffee and sugar' and most kinds of provisions on hand. No source says where the store stood, and even his initial, B or H, is disputed: a whole man's business, and history has lost his name. The Democrat's 1833 prices put coffee at 17 cents a pound and tea at 87 cents to $1.25, and 'whiskey 40 cents a gallon'. Choose what your money goes on, and remember it does not come back.

== stop the-druggist
Back east to Philo Carpenter's, the druggist at the Sign of the Golden Mortar. He landed in July 1832 while cholera was at Fort Dearborn and 'all ordinary intercourse had been suspended', and opened his drugs in 'a small log house standing near the river' in a town that badly needed medicine. Then he built here and added salt, sugar and hardware: a druggist in a frontier town sells whatever sells. His notice of 27 June 1835 lists cream of tartar, lemon syrup, black lead and tooth powder.

== stop the-corner
End at Peck's corner of LaSalle and South Water, a few steps from where you began. His store was the village's first two-storey frame, of lumber 'brought from Detroit by a boat'. His card lists dry goods, hardware, groceries and crockery, and a December 1834 notice adds flannels and calicoes. Finish the basket, or keep what is left of the two dollars against the week when work runs out.

== ending table-laid
Something to drink and a cup of your own to drink it from. Your place at the boarding-house table is begun, and the cupboard with it. The rest of the purse will have to last until wages come, if they come on time.

== ending room-kept
Flannel for the nights and tooth powder by the basin. Your share of the room is starting to feel like your own, though the other half of the bed still belongs to someone else.

== ending purse-kept
You bought little and kept most of the purse. A household can begin with patience, and the block's stores will be there tomorrow. In a town this dear, the coins in your pocket are the only security you have.

== ending basket-begun
A basket that is not quite the list you set out with, which is how most first days go, and a purse lighter than you meant. The cupboard is begun, if not yet finished.
