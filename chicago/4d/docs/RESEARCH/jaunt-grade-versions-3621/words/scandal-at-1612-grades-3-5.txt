== premise
A carriage maker's fancy house has become the most talked-about house on the block. There is a divorce, a judge's order, a violin in a broken car, and a small boy caught in the middle.

== opening
JAUNT: THE SCANDAL AT 1612 PRAIRIE. It is a Monday afternoon in November 1904, at the quiet north end of Prairie Avenue.

There are four stops along one short block. For twenty years the house at 1612 was one of the finest on the street. This year it is the scene of a big fight. A husband and wife are getting a divorce. A judge has let the wife keep the house. In November the husband stood up in court and said things about her that the whole city has now read in the newspaper. The neighbors keep their curtains closed and their ears open.

Start next door, where you can picture the house the way it used to be.

== stop showplace
This is 1608 Prairie, and next to it is the big square house at 1612. Twenty years ago it belonged to a Studebaker. The Studebaker brothers made carriages. Theirs was the brightest house on the street. In 1884 about two hundred guests came here after a family wedding. Every room was lit up and filled with flowers and green vines. A newspaper said that morning and night, a fancy carriage stood in front of the door. A carriage maker's house should have the best carriages, and it did. Look at it now. No carriage waits at the curb today. A maid next door is watching from her steps. Walk to the gate of 1612.

== caption showplace
A maid at 1608 sweeps her steps and keeps one eye on the house next door.

== stop decree
This is 1612 Prairie. The newspaper has the story, and so does everybody else. Last May the husband, Charles Brown, asked a court for a divorce. His wife, Catherine, is 27. She is the daughter of a rich general from Buffalo. Mr. Brown said she drank too much and did other wrong things. Then a judge, Judge Tuley, let Mrs. Brown keep living in this house. He also ordered Mr. Brown to pay her $200 a month until the case was over. So the wife is inside, and the husband is outside. Behind those windows live a woman the papers have made famous and a small boy. The house will be torn down around 1960. Step next door to hear what the husband said in November.

== caption decree
A lady comes out on the steps of 1612. She looks up and down the street and goes back in quickly.

== stop charges
This is 1616 Prairie, the house next door to the south. The neighbors here read the newspaper with their breakfast. In November Mr. Brown went back to court. He said his wife often drank too much and did very strange things. He said she once played a violin in a broken car while a man pushed it down the road. He said she danced behind the house in her nightgown. He also said she had run away with another man, a car salesman, first to a summer town and then to New York. It is the juiciest gossip on Prairie Avenue in years, and two maids on these steps are enjoying it. But a divorce is not only about two people. Look for the boy.

== caption charges
Two maids on the steps of 1616 share the newspaper and look over at 1612.

== stop boy
Cross to 1613 and look back at the other side of the block. The boy is Charles Edward Brown, the couple's only son. He is the real prize in this fight. A nurse walks him along the sidewalk past his own front door. His mother has the house. His father wants the house back, and he wants the boy too. The court has not decided yet. In the newspapers the boy is just one line at the end of each story. On this walk he is the only person in the whole fight who has done nothing wrong at all. The story will end in March 1905.

== ending march
The walk ends here. Early in 1905, after a hard fight, the husband won. He got the divorce, the house and the boy. On 8 March 1905 he sold the house to the bishop of a Chicago church. That same week the newspaper found Catherine Brown in jail. The police had arrested her at another house, where they said she was using a different name. She had to pay a fine, but she said, "I am penniless," and, "I cannot pay the fine". The general's daughter from Buffalo had kept 1612 Prairie for less than a year.
