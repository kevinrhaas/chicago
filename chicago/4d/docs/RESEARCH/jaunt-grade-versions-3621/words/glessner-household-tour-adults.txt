== opening
JAUNT: THE GLESSNER HOUSEHOLD. A spring morning, April 1904, at 1800 Prairie Avenue, one of the richest households on the richest street in Chicago.

Seven stops round the outside of the house, and at each one somebody who lives or works here is waiting at their own door: John and Frances Glessner, their cook, the parlor maid and the house maid, the stable helper, and the old coachman, who left the Glessners in 1902 and has come back this morning to show you his stable. Two of them own the house. The rest keep it, for wages, and sleep in the wings built for them, men in one, women in the other.

The people and the facts are from the house's own records: John Glessner's 1923 account of the house, the museum's research into Frances Glessner's journals, and the census of 1900. The words they say are written for this walk, and this time nobody is minding their manners.

Let's start the morning with Mr. Glessner, out on his front walk.

== stop john
Good morning. John Glessner. I came to Chicago from Springfield, Ohio, in 1870, a few weeks married, to be nearer the farmers who buy our machines. We sold 800 of them in 1871 and 25,000 in 1884, and two years ago our firm went into the new International Harvester Company, and I am one of its four vice-presidents. That is what paid for this corner, $50,000 for the land alone, and for Mr. Richardson's last work. A dear old lady once said to me 'you get it from your wife and your house', meaning my standing in this town. I took it as a compliment, for I selected the one and I built the other. Money builds a house. A household takes servants. Mrs. Glessner is in the courtyard: go in under the house by the carriage passage.

== stop frances
I'm Frances Glessner. Out here the house shows its sunny face; the street side was built to keep Chicago out. Every Monday my Reading Class meets in the library, and this year I have set up a silversmith's bench in the basement. I have kept a journal since 1879, and in it I write down every servant we hire, their pay and why they left or why I let them go. Running a house like this is management, whatever the men think. One August evening in 1891 the staff showed me how it feels from the other side: 'After dinner Fredrick appeared with a document signed by all five saying they would all leave as soon as they could be paid.' All five, at once. So I sent for Mattie. Meet her by the kitchen side of the court.

== stop mattie
Mattie Williamson, the cook. This house keeps one cook: me. I was born in Quebec, and my brother Alex runs the Glessners' farm in New Hampshire. In the winter of 1891 Mrs. Glessner went through seven cooks in seven weeks, all beaten by her new gas stove, or by her. In February 1892 she wrote, 'Mattie Williamson is going to try my cooking. I shall have her taught.' I was hired as a maid. Now I send up eight-course dinners and feed the live-in staff of eight besides; we eat our own dinner at three, so the kitchen is clear for theirs. A board of bells on my wall tells me which room wants something, and somebody always does. Christine is back from market, round at the corner.

== stop christine
Hej. Christine Nelson, the parlor maid, from Sweden in 1892. The cleaning is mine, and in this city it never ends: the smoke is so thick the drawings in the hall had to go under glass, and every yard of it settles on something I must wipe. People walking by laugh at those narrow windows on Eighteenth Street. They only light the corridors, and the corridors are ours: every room has two doors or more, and the passages are planned so that servants rarely are in evidence. That is the whole idea of us. A good maid in this house is one the family never sees. You can clean a whole floor and never cross them, and nobody thanks a ghost. Mary keeps the rooms with me. She'll be at our door under the great arch.

== stop mary
I'm Mary Reynolds, the house maid, an Illinois girl. Under the great arch is our own front door, and our own address, 35 Eighteenth Street, so our letters never mix with the family's. We women sleep up above the kitchen wing, four bedrooms off one hall, one bathroom between us. The men live over the coach house, and there is no way through from their wing to ours. I'll let you guess why. This door is where the music gets smuggled in. On the twenty-fifth wedding anniversary Mr. Thomas brought the entire orchestra this way and up the back stairs. Our stairs, for their surprise. Charles, the coachman until 1902, is back at the stable doors.

== stop charles
Good morning. Charles Nelson. I'll be honest: I'm not the coachman here in 1904. I drove for Mr. Glessner from 1878, back at the old house on Washington Street, until 1902, twenty-four years at the reins, and I've come back this morning to show you my old stable. I came from Sweden in 1871, and by 1900 Martha and I had five children in the rear building here, a family of seven over the horses. Six stalls, all white glazed brick so it can be washed down like a dairy. The furnace room is underneath, so the coal, the ash and the dirt never come near the family. That's the arrangement. Who holds the reins now, the records don't say. August is round the corner at the courtyard gates.

== stop august
Hallo. August Billings, the stable helper, from Sweden in 1892, same year as Christine, and I sleep in the rear building with the other men. The coachman has me at the harness and washing down the carriage, and the doves: the dovecotes hold near sixty, and their eggs go to the family's table, not ours. These gates are the back way into the court. In December 1887, before my time, the family brought every stick they owned in through here and into the house 'before our neighbors suspected even that we were about to move'. Rich people like their privacy. That night they lit the new hearth with fire carried from the old one. That's the whole household, the ones upstairs and the ones who keep it running.

== ending finish
Two years after this morning, in 1906, the Glessners gave up horses for motor cars and the wall between the stable and the carriage house came down. Mattie cooked here until 1912, when she left to marry in California; Frances Glessner wrote of her that she was 'one of the finest people that ever lived.' John and Frances lived in this house to the end of their lives, and in 1924 he signed a deed of gift for it. The rooms these people kept, upstairs and down, are a museum now.
