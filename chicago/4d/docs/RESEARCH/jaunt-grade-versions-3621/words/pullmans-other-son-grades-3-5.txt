== premise
A German soldier said George Pullman was his father. For four years he followed the family, to the office, to the funeral, to the front steps, and once to the wrong end of a judge's gun.

== opening
JAUNT: PULLMAN'S OTHER SON. It is a Monday afternoon in November 1904, outside the Pullman house at Eighteenth Street.

There are four stops. You go around the Pullman house, then down the street to the house of Pullman's daughter and her husband. George Pullman had twin sons, and the whole city knew their names. But another young man said he was Pullman's son too. His mother told him so as she was dying, and he crossed the ocean to say it to Pullman's face. The family called him crazy. The doctors kept saying he was not.

Start at the front of the house, where his story begins.

== stop mother
This is 1729 Prairie, the Pullman house. Picture a German soldier named Gustave Behring, 28 years old, sitting by his mother's bed. As she was dying, she told him his real father was George Pullman. She said she had once worked in Chicago looking after a family's children. When she was going to have a baby, she said, Pullman sent her back to Germany with $500. Behring came to Chicago. Pullman threw him out of his office. Behring kept coming back, and he was arrested again and again. Four days before Pullman died, someone wrote down what they said. Behring said, "You are my father!" Pullman said, "You are a crazy man!" The house will be torn down in 1922. Walk around to the front steps.

== caption mother
A stranger in a dark coat stops at the Pullman gate. He looks up at the house, then walks on.

== stop steps
These are the front steps. Pullman died in October 1897. Less than twelve hours after the funeral, Behring came to this house. He wanted to speak to Mrs. Pullman. He never got to the door. Pullman's twin sons and the family's lawyers met him on these steps. He was arrested again. Then a doctor who studied the mind looked at him and said he was not crazy. So he was not a madman. He was just a man who would not go away. Emma Scott, the housekeeper, still answers this door. Walk around the corner to the glass room.

== caption steps
Emma Scott, the housekeeper, opens the front door. She looks at the empty steps and closes it again.

== stop ship
This is the conservatory, the family's glass room full of plants. Behind this glass the Pullmans waited for their problem to go away. In September 1898 it did. Detectives walked Behring onto a big ship sailing to Germany. But he came back. By then the twins had troubles of their own, and one of them, George Jr., died in 1901. Pullman's daughter, Florence, was married to a lawyer named Frank Lowden. So when Behring came back, he went to Mr. Lowden. Walk south down Prairie to the Lowden house.

== caption ship
The groom, Samuel Whatter Jr., comes out of the stable yard by the glass room and looks down the street.

== stop gun
This is 1912 Prairie, where Frank Lowden lives with Florence and their children. Mr. Lowden is a careful man. But on 25 June 1901, Behring walked into Lowden's law office. He asked for $5,000 in cash, and $10,000 a month for six months. Lowden's partner, Judge Davis, did not wait. He pointed a gun at Behring and held him there until the police came. Watch Mr. Lowden come home this afternoon. He looks back down the street before he goes in. This house will be torn down in 1939.

== ending name
The walk ends here. Gustave Behring was arrested, locked up, checked by doctors, found not crazy, put on a ship, and arrested again. In the newspapers, his story ends with a judge's gun. "You are my father!" "You are a crazy man!" Those two sentences are all that is left of the only talk the two men ever had. You can decide for yourself which one was telling the truth.
