== opening
JAUNT: A BED FOR THE NIGHT. The sun is going down on the busiest summer Chicago has ever seen, and you have nowhere to sleep. The land office opened in May, and buyers and speculators 'filled the streets and overflowed improvised camps on the adjacent prairies'. The American counted some hundreds of strangers on top of the town's own two or three thousand people, and store houses were thrown open for the emigrants who had no roof at all.

Four houses that take in strangers lie between the wagon yards of the west side and Lake Street. See what each one is like, decide whether cost or convenience comes first, and choose where to knock. Know what you are buying: in this town, 'full meant three in a bed sometimes, with the floor covered besides'. A bed here is a share of a mattress with men you have never met, their boots, their breath and their whiskey.

== stop west-side
The Western Hotel, Randolph and Canal. W. H. Stow built it with a forty-foot front, a sixty-foot wing and a wagon yard, the stopping place for farmers and teamsters coming in from the west; he dates it 1834, a chronology 1835. The yard smells of horses and the bar of the men who drive them. Back in 1831 the county fixed what an innkeeper could charge: 12½ cents for lodging for men, 25 for supper, 50 for keeping a horse all night. Note that the horse's night costs four times the man's. In a summer like this one, good luck finding a bed at those prices.

== stop the-sauganash
Over the South Branch to Lake and Market and the Sauganash, repaired and newly fitted up in June. A week later the Democrat described 'the immense congregation of strangers crowding every room of our public houses', some 'sleeping on the floor'. The landlord, Mark Beaubien, was frank about it. He liked to say he 'kept hotel like —', and the place he named is the one where sinners go. He told how he would put a man to sleep on the floor, give him a blanket and, once the traveller was 'slumbering soundly', take it back for the next guest. Every blanket in the house earned its keep several times a night.

== stop the-boarding-house
East to LaSalle, behind Peck's store: Mrs Rufus Brown's log house, one of the first-class boarding houses, Andreas says. First-class is relative. A boarding house feeds you by the week at a common table, where the quickest hand gets the meat, and it is packed: 'full meant three in a bed sometimes, with the floor covered besides.' On 13 June the American found strangers filling the public houses and the streets, and store houses thrown open for the emigrants who had no roof at all. A woman ran this house, fed this crowd and kept its accounts, and Andreas gives her only her husband's name.

== stop lake-street
On along Lake to Dearborn: the Mansion House, Dexter Graves's log tavern of 1831, which got a frame front out to the street line two years later, a respectable face on an old log house. Haddock, his son-in-law, kept it until 1835, then Abram Markle took it over; nobody wrote down the month, so either man may be behind the bar tonight, and either will take your money. Almost opposite stands the first Tremont House. Time to choose.

== ending thrift
Cost first, and you knock first at Mrs Brown's. If there is a corner of a bed, it is yours, and you will have company in it: in June 1835 every house in town was full. You lie awake between two strangers, listening to a third snore on the floor, and count yourself lucky to be indoors.

== ending close-at-hand
Convenience first, and you ask first on Lake Street. If the house is full there is always the floor, and in June 1835 plenty of strangers slept on it, boots on, purses buttoned inside their shirts.

== ending second-thoughts
You set out wanting one thing and chose by another, which a full town makes easy. Sleep with one hand on your blanket and the other on your money: Mark Beaubien is not the only landlord who counts what he lends, and not every guest is honest.
