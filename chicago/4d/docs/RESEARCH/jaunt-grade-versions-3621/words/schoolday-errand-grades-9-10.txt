== opening
JAUNT: A SCHOOLDAY ERRAND. Your household has a single question: where could a child receive an education? In this town, reaching school is half the lesson, and the schoolmaster Granville Sproat remembered carrying the smaller scholars on his back, "not daring to trust them on the slippery plank", and once falling into the mire with a child in his arms.

Ride past the places where Chicago's first classes met, on both sides of the river, and finish where the Democrat is printed, with a notice dated today concerning the construction of a genuine school house.

== stop michigan-street
On Michigan Street, between Cass and Rush, Andreas has John Watkins, hired in 1833, later teaching in a house that Moses and Kirkland identify as Richard J. Hamilton's home. Watkins had begun in 1832 "in a log house about twelve feet square", with only twelve pupils in his first quarter, so Chicago's schools began on a remarkably modest scale.

A schoolroom once, and a home again.

== stop north-bank
Down to the bank east of Clark, where Andreas reports that Hamilton and Owen built a school-house in 1833, the first constructed for the purpose, although Watkins himself did not remember it; by 1835 it was the North Side's public school. South of the river, Sproat's school met upstairs in the Baptist church, and he described it as "a very pleasant one, the pupils obedient and attentive".

== stop the-log-house
Across the river to State Street, beside the old military reservation, Andreas places Eliza Chappel's infant school of 1833 and 1834 in a log house, although other accounts disagree. In 1834 the school moved into the Presbyterian church.

== narrative the-log-house
Where the town's youngest scholars first assembled for lessons.

== stop the-notice
Continue to South Water and Clark, where the Democrat was printed from 1833. Today's number repeats a notice: School District No. 4 meets in the Presbyterian Church on 7 July at six, to consider building a school house. Money is scarce because the school section was sold in 1833 for $38,865, and "The sale was a great mistake": held twenty years, it would have brought a hundred times more.

== narrative the-notice
Preserve a useful notice.

== ending two-answers
The north-bank school is written down, and the District No. 4 meeting is copied out for 7 July: two answers to a single household question, namely a school already teaching and another still to be debated.

== ending the-meeting
No school is written down yet, but the notice is copied out: 7 July, six in the evening, the Presbyterian Church. Whether a school house is built depends on the meeting, and you intend to attend.

== ending a-long-way
The north-bank school is written down, but the river lies between it and the stores, which makes a long journey each morning for a child of five. Somebody may have to carry the little one, exactly as Sproat did.

== ending a-scholars-walk
The north-bank school is written down: a walk and a river crossing each morning, no great hardship for a child of ten. Whether there is room for one more scholar remains a question for the schoolmaster.

== ending still-asking
Nothing is written down and nothing copied, so the question returns home exactly as it departed. The schools you passed are real, the meeting is next week, and the inquiry continues.

== keepsake text
A fictional note of one household's question about school in July 1835, and what you wrote down or copied out. No real pupil, teacher's answer or fee is written on it.
