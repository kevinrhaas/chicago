== premise
A nocturnal walk along Millionaire's Row: a battlefield underfoot, a tycoon entombed in concrete, a widow who rattles the windows, a drowned brother and the unexplained gunshot at 1919.

== opening
JAUNT: GHOSTS OF PRAIRIE AVENUE. An autumn night in 1904. The gas lamps are illuminated and the carriages stabled. This is Millionaire's Row, where the mansions of George Pullman, Marshall Field, John J. Glessner and Philip Armour stand shoulder to shoulder, and where the wealthiest families in Chicago secure their doors each night against everyone except their ghosts.

Seven stops, on foot, from the corner of Eighteenth Street south to 1919 Prairie. You will encounter a battlefield beneath the pavement, an industrialist entombed in concrete, an architect who never witnessed his masterpiece, a widow who rattles the windows, a man who walked into the lake, and a gunshot nobody has satisfactorily explained. The narratives are legends, perpetuated by commercial ghost tours and by the Glessner House Museum itself; the deaths underlying them are unquestionably genuine.

Mind the shadows. Begin where the avenue begins: on the battleground of 1812.

== stop battle-ground
Prairie at Eighteenth, after dark. Before the mansions, sand hills occupied this ground, and on 15 August 1812 the Battle of Fort Dearborn was fought across them. The dead "lay in the sand, half buried where they were killed, until 1816", and for a century an enormous cottonwood marked the location. A Tribune correspondent asserted in 1900 that the Pullman mansion before you "stands about where the massacre took place". George Pullman was conscious of the association: in 1893 he erected a bronze depicting the Potawatomi chief Black Partridge rescuing Margaret Helm near this intersection. According to legend, the fallen still patrol here on clear, frigid nights: "The Americans go searching for their families and friends while the Natives continue to patrol their land". The statue's patron walks nowhere, as his family deliberately guaranteed.

== stop pullman
1729 Prairie, the Pullman mansion. George Pullman accumulated his initial fortune elevating Chicago from its own mud, jacking up entire structures "on average six feet without causing them any damage". Subsequently came the sleeping car, and Lincoln's body traveled home to Springfield aboard one while "hundreds of thousands of people lined the route in homage". In 1894 he reduced his employees' wages, but "he didn't reduce the rent in Pullman", his company town, provoking a strike. Upon his death in 1897, his family feared desecration, so, according to the account, he was interred in a "lead-lined mahogany coffin, which was then sealed inside a block of concrete", beneath steel rails "bolted together at right angles". Mrs. Pullman remains in residence; the mansion will be demolished in 1922. Her husband detested the house opposite.

== stop glessner
1800 Prairie, Glessner House, the granite fortress Pullman intended when he complained, "I do not know what I have ever done to have that thing staring me in the face every time I go out of my door". Its architect, Henry Hobson Richardson, died on 26 April 1886, five weeks before construction commenced on 1 June, and never observed it rise. According to legend, he inspected it posthumously. The family perceived a presence; inexplicable cold spots invade heated rooms; "If you're lucky, you might catch the man's spirit standing on that same staircase". One subsequent visitor characterized it as a "pleasant presence". Considerably less pleasant was the eventual avocation of the Glessners' daughter Frances, who constructed miniature, meticulously accurate dioramas of actual deaths, the Nutshell Studies of Unexplained Death, which still train detectives. Now turn around: the residence opposite is rattling.

== stop kimball
1801 Prairie, the Kimball mansion, a composition of precipitous slate and turret. William W. Kimball commenced business in 1857 "beginning with only four pianos" and developed a factory "churning out around 100 pianos and organs every week". He and his wife Evaline furnished the entrance hall with Old Master paintings. This winter the music ceases: he "died at his home in Chicago on December 16, 1904". Evaline remained until 1921, and during her final years, reportedly, she hosted receptions for "empty rooms she thought were full of visitors". The paintings now hang in the Art Institute, yet the immense north windows shudder and rattle in perfectly windless conditions. The museum itself speculates, "Could it be that the spirit of Evaline Kimball has returned to the house" Perhaps she seeks her collection.

== stop keith
1900 Prairie, Elbridge G. Keith's residence, its mansard roof silhouetted against the sky. Several doors south, at 1906, resides the widow of his brother Edson, a merchant. Edson Keith had suffered chronic insomnia for a decade. Early on Monday, 21 September 1896, he departed his house, walked to the lake at the foot of Sixteenth Street, and entered the water; the newspapers reported that he "committed suicide Monday morning by drowning himself in Lake Michigan". He never returned. Or did he? Paranormal investigators maintain that "his ghost has been seen at his brother's house, where he sometimes appears in the windows". Glance toward the darkened panes as you pass. Across the avenue, the wealthiest man in Chicago sleeps.

== stop field
1905 Prairie, Marshall Field's mansion. "Give the lady what she wants", the merchant proclaimed, and his customers made him "the wealthiest and most powerful businessman in Chicago", valued at $125 million by 1905. Tonight everything appears impregnable; it is not. In thirteen months his son, next door, will be shot. Six weeks afterward, Field will play golf on New Year's Day 1906 with Robert Todd Lincoln, Abraham Lincoln's son, contract pneumonia and die on 16 January, so that father and son perish within a single winter. The mansion will endure until 1955, when it too will be demolished. Continue to the son's residence, the one with the footsteps.

== stop field-jr
1919 Prairie, Marshall Field Jr.'s residence, with its massive new Burnham addition. On Wednesday, 22 November 1905, a gunshot will resound upstairs. He will die five days later at Mercy Hospital, maintaining, "I shot myself with that revolver – accidentally", and the coroner will concur. Chicago will not. Reporters will receive tips that he spent the evening at the Everleigh Club, a brothel several blocks west on Dearborn Street: "The most popular story is that he was in fact shot at the Everleigh Club" and transported home to simulate an accident. Nothing was ever substantiated. Nevertheless subsequent owners reported footsteps reverberating through vacant corridors and "strange cries in the dead of night", and one family reportedly engaged an exorcist to expel a spirit that "sours the air around it". This residence retains its ghost.

== leg 0
Across the intersection to the Pullman mansion, the most massive edifice on this block, its windows gaslit.

== leg 1
Over Eighteenth Street to the southwest corner and the rusticated granite wall confronting Pullman's entrance.

== leg 2
Back across Prairie Avenue to the corner turret of the Kimball mansion.

== leg 3
South along the western sidewalk. This was Millionaire's Row: by 1886 "the finest mansions in the city, each equipped with its own carriage house, stood on Prairie Avenue".

== leg 4
Cross to the eastern side; Field's mansion is the broad one with the corner bay.

== leg 5
Two doors south, where father and son resided side by side.

== ending finish
The walk concludes at 1919 Prairie. One final apparition for the journey home. Decades later the Clarke House, "purported to be the city's oldest standing house" and "twice relocated", will return to this neighborhood, and in the adjacent park, they claim, a woman in a settler's dress drifts among the shrubbery after dark, while spectral carriage horses clip past that "always seem to have their bits, but never their harnesses". Legends like these persist, perhaps, because they allow a neighborhood to remember tragedies that polite society preferred not to discuss.

Sleep well, Prairie Avenue.
