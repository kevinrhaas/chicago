== premise
Spend a spring morning in 1904 circling 1800 Prairie Avenue with the people who inhabit and maintain it: the Glessners, their cook and maids, the stable helper, and the retired coachman returned for the occasion, each stationed at a separate entrance.

== opening
JAUNT: THE GLESSNER HOUSEHOLD. A spring morning, April 1904, at 1800 Prairie Avenue.

Seven stops encircling the exterior of the residence, and at each one somebody who lives or works here awaits you at their particular entrance: John and Frances Glessner, their cook, the parlor maid and the house maid, the stable helper, and the veteran coachman, who concluded his service in 1902 and has returned this morning to exhibit his stable. Each describes their responsibilities within the household and directs you onward.

The individuals and the facts derive from the residence's own documentation: John Glessner's 1923 account of the house, the museum's research into Frances Glessner's journals, and the federal census of 1900. Their dialogue is composed for this walk.

Commence the morning with Mr. Glessner, on his front walk.

== stop john
Good morning, and welcome to 1800 Prairie Avenue; I'm John Glessner, and I relocated to Chicago from Springfield, Ohio, in 1870, a few weeks married, to be nearer the farmers who purchase our agricultural machinery. We sold 800 machines in 1871 and 25,000 in 1884, and two years ago our firm was consolidated into the new International Harvester Company. Mr. Richardson designed this residence, his final commission, and we first slept here on 1 December 1887. An elderly acquaintance once observed that my standing in this city derived from my wife and my house, which I received as a compliment, since I selected the former and constructed the latter. The entire household is occupied this morning; Mrs. Glessner is in the courtyard, so enter beneath the residence through the carriage passage immediately to your left.

== stop frances
You found us! I'm Frances Glessner, and here the residence reveals its sunlit countenance: observe the rounded bay of the hall, and the door whose yellow glass makes the interior feel illuminated even on an overcast Chicago day. The creeper covering these walls was transplanted from our previous residence, including one vine fifty-five feet long, which flourished. Every Monday morning throughout winter and spring my Reading Class assembles in the library, the ladies sewing while a professional reader performs. This year I installed a silversmith's workbench in the basement, and I additionally maintain beehives. Since 1879 I have kept a journal documenting every servant we engage, their wages and their reasons for departing. One August evening in 1891 our entire staff of five resigned simultaneously after dinner; consequently I summoned Mattie, whom you'll find on the kitchen side of the court.

== stop mattie
Hello! I'm Mattie Williamson, and this household employs precisely one cook: myself. I was born in Quebec, and my brother Alex manages the Glessners' agricultural property in New Hampshire, where every summer I travel a week ahead of the family to prepare the house. During the winter of 1891 Mrs. Glessner exhausted seven cooks in seven weeks, each defeated by her innovative gas stove, and in February 1892 she recorded, "Mattie Williamson is going to try my cooking. I shall have her taught". My kitchen lies beyond this wall, where a board of bells announces which room requires attention. I produce eight-course dinners while also feeding the staff, who dine at three o'clock so the kitchen remains available for the family. Last January an entire orchestra performed Beethoven on my pots and pans! Christine has returned from market, around the corner.

== stop christine
Hej! I'm Christine Nelson, the parlor maid, and I emigrated from Sweden in 1892. Mattie and the waitress provision the household, and the ladies' maid supervises the ladies' wardrobes; the cleaning is my responsibility, which in this city proves interminable. The industrial smoke is so dense that the drawings in the hall required protective glass. Pedestrians ridicule those narrow windows along Eighteenth Street, yet they merely illuminate the corridors, and those corridors constitute our domain: every principal room has two or more doors, and the passages are arranged so that servants rarely are in evidence. I can clean an entire floor without encountering a single member of the family. Even the iceman remains outside, inserting the ice through a diminutive half-door into the refrigerated closet. Mary maintains the rooms alongside me; she'll be at our entrance beneath the great arch.

== stop mary
Hello, I'm Mary Reynolds, the house maid, a native of Illinois, and welcome to our side of the residence, where beneath the great arch stands our private entrance. We even possess a separate address, 35 Eighteenth Street, so our correspondence never intermingles with the family's. We women sleep above the kitchen wing, in four bedrooms along a single corridor with a small porch overlooking the street, while the men occupy quarters above the coach house, with no connecting passage between the two wings. This entrance also facilitates musical conspiracies: on the twenty-fifth wedding anniversary, Mr. Thomas smuggled his entire orchestra through here and up the service stairs, and nobody upstairs suspected anything until the performance commenced. Last New Year's Day a double quartet of horns ascended by the identical route. Charles, our coachman until 1902, awaits you at the stable doors.

== stop charles
Good morning to you; I'm Charles Nelson, and I'll be forthright: I'm not the coachman here in 1904. I drove for Mr. Glessner from 1878, beginning at the former residence on Washington Street, until 1902, and I've returned this morning to exhibit my former stable. I emigrated from Sweden in 1871, and by 1900 Martha and I were raising five children in the rear building. There are six stalls, with comparable capacity for carriages, all constructed of white glazed brick so the interior can be washed like a dairy. The furnace room lies beneath, ensuring coal and ashes never approach the residence. When construction commenced in June 1886, young George drove his father's horse and buggy to the site and turned the ceremonial first soil with his sister. Who currently holds the reins, the surviving records don't specify. August waits at the courtyard gates.

== stop august
Hallo! I'm August Billings, the stable helper, and I emigrated from Sweden in 1892, contemporaneously with Christine, and I sleep in the rear building alongside the other men. The coachman assigns me the harness and the washing of the carriages, and I additionally tend the doves: the dovecotes accommodate nearly sixty, and their eggs supply the family's table. These gates constitute the rear entrance to the court, and they carry a story predating my employment. In December 1887 the family transported every possession they owned through here and into the residence before the neighbors even suspected an impending move, and that evening they kindled the new hearth with fire carried ceremonially from the old one. That concludes the household, and I thank you sincerely for coming around!

== leg 0
Mr. Glessner paid $50,000 for this corner in March 1885: two complete lots plus the northern seventeen feet of a third.

== leg 1
Along Prairie Avenue, the Pullman mansion stands diagonally across the intersection.

== leg 2
The exterior walls are Wellesley granite facing the streets and hollow brick within, leaving no cavity where a mouse could establish itself.

== ending finish
Two years after this morning, in 1906, the Glessners replaced their horses with automobiles, and the partition between the stable and the carriage house was demolished. Mattie cooked here until 1912, when she departed to marry in California; Frances Glessner wrote that she was "one of the finest people that ever lived". John and Frances inhabited this residence for the remainder of their lives, and in 1924 he executed a deed of gift for it. The rooms these individuals maintained have become a museum, which now interprets the domestic labor that the architecture was originally designed to conceal.
