== opening
RIDE: WOMEN OF 1835. A summer afternoon, west to east, from the forks to the fort. Most of what was written about this town in 1835 was written by men, about men: land sales, votes, harbours, fortunes. The women appear in it as wives, as daughters, as a husband's name with Mrs in front. But read closely and the women are everywhere. They ran the hotels, kept the crowded boarding houses, taught the first classes, bore children in log rooms, wrote the book that remembers half these buildings, and held families together across two worlds while the ground under one of those worlds was being sold.

Seven stops, seven lives, each woman named by a source.

== stop robinson
Wolf Point. Across the water stand the log cabins Wau-Bun puts here in 1831 as the home of Alexander Robinson and his wife's family. Catherine Chevalier Robinson was Potawatomi, the daughter of Marianne and François Chevalier and the granddaughter of the Potawatomi leader Naunongee. Her sister Archange married Antoine Ouilmette; after the 1829 treaty the government deeded 1,280 acres to Archange and her children, and the village of Wilmette carries their name. Robinson descendants kept their Des Plaines land into the mid-twentieth century.

== stop kinzie
John H. Kinzie's store. His wife Juliette Kinzie later wrote Wau-Bun (1856), and its memories of 1831 are the evidence for several buildings in this very scene: much of what this town looked like survives because a woman wrote it down. On 18 June 1835, thirteen days before this date, she gave birth in Chicago to Eleanor. Eleanor's daughter, Juliette Gordon Low, founded the Girl Scouts in 1912. She is two weeks from childbed today.

== stop murphy
The Exchange Coffee House, Mark Beaubien's second hotel. Andreas: 'Mr. and Mrs. Murphy took charge of this new hotel, which they christened the Exchange Coffee House.' Open under the Murphys from August 1834 until 1836, it was a couple's business, and the history names Mrs. Murphy as its keeper beside her husband, but never gives her a first name of her own. Feeding, bedding and cleaning up after a hotel full of men was her work as much as his.

== stop brown
Mrs. Rufus Brown's boarding house, behind Peck's store. 'Mrs. Rufus Brown kept one of the first-class boarding houses', Andreas writes, in a town whose houses 'were always full; and full meant three in a bed sometimes, with the floor covered besides.' Think what that meant for the woman running it: the cooking for a crowd of strangers, the washing, the bedding, the drunks and the debts. She ran the business; the record keeps only her husband's name.

== stop chappel
Eliza Chappel's school. She kept Chicago's first infant school, opened in 1833 in a small log house once used as a store; one account has some pupils paddling canoes across the river to class, small children alone in canoes on the river. Andreas wrote that Miss Eliza Chappel 'is entitled to especial distinction in this work.' A young unmarried woman, far from home, teaching other people's children in a town of transient men: it took nerve.

== stop church
First Presbyterian Church. Eliza Chappel was one of its twenty-six founding members in June 1833, and on 15 June 1835 she married its pastor, Jeremiah Porter. Marriage did not quiet her. In the Civil War, as Eliza Chappel Porter, she directed the Chicago Sanitary Commission and went to Chattanooga with Mary Ann Bickerdyke to care for the Union wounded, among the wounded and the dying.

== stop beaubien
Fort Dearborn, from the south-west; the Beaubien homestead stands just south. Josette LaFramboise Beaubien, wife of Jean Baptiste Beaubien, was the daughter of the trader Francois LaFramboise and Shawenoqua, a Potawatomi woman. She lived at the meeting point of two peoples and two faiths in a town that was deciding which of them it would keep. In July 1834 a Josette Beaubien stood sponsor at a baptism at St Mary's, the town's first Catholic parish.

== ending finish
End of the ride at the fort. These are the women whose places this scene holds, most of them known to history through a husband, a father or a single line in a man's book. Others in the town's papers kept shops and schools of their own; their rooms are waiting to be found, and their names with them.
