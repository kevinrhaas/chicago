== premise
Three blocks west of the mansions, two sisters from Virginia operate the most notorious house in Chicago, furnished with gold cuspidors and a $15,000 piano. Prairie Avenue pretends not to know the route.

== opening
JAUNT: THE SISTERS OF THE LEVEE. Dusk on a Monday in November 1904, at Eighteenth Street and Prairie Avenue.

Four stops, walking west from Millionaire's Row toward the Levee, the city's red-light district, and ending at a church on its border. Prairie Avenue lies three blocks from the most famous address in Chicago, and it is not the Glessner House. Two sisters who called themselves Everleigh opened a club on Dearborn Street in 1900, and some of the wealthiest men in the world have found their way to its door.

Begin at the corner, and watch which direction the cabs turn.

== stop corner
Eighteenth and Prairie, as the gas lamps are lit. Eighteenth Street functions as a border: to the north and east stand the mansions; to the south and west, "several blocks away from Prairie Avenue", begins the Levee, the district stretching from Eighteenth Street to Twenty-second and from Wabash to Clark, home to the city's most infamous brothels. The ladies of Prairie Avenue never acknowledge it. Yet one of the two most celebrated houses in Chicago, Vic Shaw's, operated for a time on Prairie Avenue itself. The two gentlemen hurrying around this corner are not heading home. Follow them west.

== caption corner
Two gentlemen in evening coats round the corner and stride briskly west along Eighteenth Street.

== stop sisters
Indiana Avenue, one block west. Continue three more blocks and you reach Dearborn Street, where at 2131 and 2133 stands the Everleigh Club. Its proprietors are two sisters, Ada and Minna, who claim a Kentucky childhood, which was "another story concocted by the sisters". They were born Simms, in Virginia, in 1864 and 1866. With an inheritance of roughly $35,000 they operated a house in Omaha, then came to Chicago and purchased an existing establishment on Dearborn. They adopted a new surname from their grandmother, who signed her letters "Everly yours". The club opened on 1 February 1900, and on that first night they turned away many callers. The Everleighs understood that in their business, refusal was how you established a price.

== caption sisters
A gentleman descends the steps of an Indiana Avenue house, consults his watch, and turns west toward the Levee.

== stop rooms
Farther south along Indiana, with the Levee two blocks west. Inside the Everleigh Club are fifty rooms across two mansions: a Japanese Throne Room, a Mirror Room, a Silver Parlor, "a $15,000 gold-leafed piano for the Music Room" and "$650 gold cuspidors". A bottle of champagne costs $12, "in an era when an entire meal could be had for fifty cents", and admission alone is $50. Ada manages the accounts and hires the women; Minna receives the guests. In March 1902 Prince Henry of Prussia visited, and when a slipper flew off during a dance and spilled champagne, a member of his party drank from it, launching an entire fashion. The club employs fifteen to twenty-five cooks and maids, and its women are called "butterflies".

== caption rooms
Two maids, finished for the day, emerge from the side passage onto Indiana Avenue and hurry north, deep in conversation.

== stop church
First Presbyterian Church, at Indiana and Twenty-first Street, standing at the Levee's eastern edge, close enough to hear it on a summer night. According to the stories, the club's famous clients include the actor John Barrymore and the authors Ring Lardner and Theodore Dreiser, and gossips add young Marshall Field Jr., who lives only a few blocks away. Later, "a rival madam maliciously accused the Everleigh sisters" of something far worse, and that rumor never died. The minister's door is illuminated, and so is the club's. Tonight, as on every night, the Levee is the busier of the two.

== caption church
A family emerges from the church hall into the dusk as a cab passes in the opposite direction.

== leg 0
West along Eighteenth Street, past the last of the mansions, to Indiana Avenue.

== leg 1
South along Indiana Avenue, with the Levee's commotion two blocks to the west.

== leg 2
South to Twenty-first Street and the church at the Levee's border.

== ending close
The walk ends here. The Everleigh Club survived eleven years. On 24 October 1911 Mayor Carter Harrison Jr. ordered it closed, and Minna accepted the decision as she accepted everything: "If the Mayor says we must close, that settles it...I'll close up shop and walk out with a smile on my face". The building was demolished in July 1933. Prairie Avenue never once admitted that it knew the way to Dearborn Street; it only ever acknowledged the way back.
