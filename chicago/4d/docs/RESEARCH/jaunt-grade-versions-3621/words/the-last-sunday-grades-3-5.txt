== premise
Sunday 8 October 1871. Walk through busy, proud Chicago on its last normal day, from Lake Street to State Street, the Water Tower and a barn on DeKoven Street. Nine stops.

== opening
JAUNT: THE LAST SUNDAY. It is Sunday 8 October 1871, early in the afternoon. It has not rained for more than a week. A warm wind is blowing hard from the southwest. Last night a fire on the West Side burned four blocks near the river. The firemen are still working on it. This morning's newspaper, the Tribune, wrote three columns about that fire. Then it wrote about the city's big plans.

There are nine stops. You will see Lake Street, the Court House, a big hotel, the grain market, a theatre, the new stores of State Street, and the Water Tower across the river. Then, as the light goes, you will come to a small house on DeKoven Street. Start on Lake Street.

== stop lake-street
This is Lake Street at Clark, at two in the afternoon. It was once the main street of Chicago. Big stores that sell goods to other stores still fill the blocks down to the river. Stone buildings with arched windows line both sides. Iron awnings cover the sidewalks, and telegraph wires run overhead. Some of these buildings once stood lower. In 1859 a man named George M. Pullman began to lift them up. He raised whole blocks of brick and stone with big screw jacks. That made them level with the new, higher street. The shops inside stayed open the whole time. Today is Sunday, so the stores are shut. This morning's paper said people in Chicago had big plans. One block south stands one of the biggest, a hotel made of white marble.

== stop sherman-house
This is the Sherman House, at Clark and Randolph. Francis C. Sherman built his first hotel on this corner in 1836 and 1837. Later he was the mayor. This new hotel opened in July 1861. It is six floors tall and made of white marble from the quarries at Lemont. Its front door is on Clark Street, under a tall porch. This week the hotel has a famous guest. Theodore Thomas leads an orchestra, and he and his sixty musicians are staying here. Tomorrow night they will play the first of ten concerts. Across Randolph Street, in the square, a huge bell hangs in the Court House tower. It weighs more than five tons.

== stop court-house
This is Court House Square. It fills the whole block between Randolph, Clark, Washington and LaSalle Streets. With its grass, trees and a fountain, it looks like a park. An iron fence goes around it. The building in the middle holds almost every city and county office, from the jail in the basement to the mayor's rooms. John M. Van Osdel designed it in 1853. Look up at the tower on top. A spiral stair climbs to a balcony 120 feet high. That is where the fire watchman walks and looks out over the city. Tonight the watchman will be Mathias Schaffer, with his spyglass. Below him is the office that sends out the fire engines. Across LaSalle Street, grain is bought and sold.

== stop chamber
This is the Chamber of Commerce, on the corner of Washington and LaSalle. Edward Burling designed it, and it opened on 30 August 1865. Charles Randolph, the head of the Board of Trade, gave the big speech that day. Inside is a great hall with paintings on the walls and ceiling. Here the Board of Trade buys and sells the grain of the farms out West. Today is Sunday, so the hall is quiet. LaSalle Street has a row of stone buildings with steep roofs. Insurance companies and banks are moving in. Two blocks east, on Washington Street, workers are busy even today. They are getting a theatre ready for Monday night.

== stop crosbys
This is Crosby's Opera House, on Washington Street between State and Dearborn. Uranus Crosby opened it in 1865. The architect was W. W. Boyington. It is five floors tall, with statues over the front arch. They stand for Painting, Sculpture, Music and Commerce. Commerce means business. Shops fill the ground floor. The theatre in back has three thousand seats. All summer, workers have fixed it up, at a cost of about $80,000. This morning's paper said almost every seat is sold for the first night. The first night is tomorrow, when Theodore Thomas and his orchestra will play. Late this evening, the gas lights inside will be lit for the first time, just to see how they look. One block east is the newest shopping street in town.

== stop state-street
This is State Street at Washington. Four years ago, this part of the street had a small wooden house, an ale store and a furniture shop. Then Potter Palmer bought almost a mile of land along State Street. He wanted it to be the best place in town to shop. On the corner is the big store of Field & Leiter. It is six floors tall and almost 150 feet long. Marshall Field and Levi Leiter moved their store here from Lake Street. Their clerks treat the ladies who shop here like guests. A few blocks south is Palmer's own new hotel, the Palmer House. Across the river, on the North Side, the oldest and richest families live in big houses under tall elm trees.

== stop water-tower
This is the Water Tower, on Pine Street at Chicago Avenue, on the North Side. Chicago held a great party for it in 1867. Across the street is the Pumping Station. It pulls clean water through a tunnel that goes two miles out under the lake. That keeps it far from the dirty Chicago River. W. W. Boyington, who also built Crosby's, made both buildings of stone, like little castles. South of here are the big old houses of rich families. For years, the men in charge of the police and firemen have said the city does not have enough water. The whole city needs these pumps. And the roof over them is made of wood.

== stop saturday-ruins
This is the east end of the Van Buren Street bridge. Look across the river to the West Side. Last night, between ten and eleven, a fire started in a wood mill on Canal Street. The buildings there burn easily. People call the area the 'Red Flash'. By this afternoon, four blocks were gone. Piles of coal and lumber burned all day, and thousands of people came to look. Many firemen worked for eighteen hours in a row, until three o'clock this afternoon. They are very tired, and some of their tools are broken. It has not rained for more than a week. The wind is still strong. Ten blocks south, on DeKoven Street, a woman has milked her cows for the evening.

== stop dekoven
This is 137 DeKoven Street, on the West Side, at dusk. The houses here are small and made of wood. There are wood shavings in every barn and under every house. Patrick and Catherine O'Leary and their five children live in the back of this little house. The McLaughlin family rents the front. Behind the house is a wooden barn, full of fresh hay. Inside are five cows, a calf and a horse. Catherine O'Leary sells milk to her neighbors. She milked the cows at half past four. At about seven she will feed the horse and shut him in the barn. Tonight the McLaughlins are having a party for a cousin from Ireland. Mr. McLaughlin plays the fiddle, so there will be music. The wind has not stopped.

== ending the-barn
That night, at about nine, a wagon driver named Daniel Sullivan was sitting across the street. He saw fire in the O'Leary barn. Within thirty minutes, the firemen could not stop it. In the Court House tower, Mathias Schaffer saw the glow. He thought it was farther away than it was, and he called the wrong alarm box. By Monday morning, the Court House bell had fallen through the burning building. The Sherman House was a wreck. Crosby's Opera House never opened. The pumps at the Water Tower were wrecked too. Theodore Thomas had to run for his life.

People say Mrs. O'Leary's cow kicked over a lamp. That is a legend. No one ever proved how the fire started. The Great Chicago Fire jaunt begins in that barn.
