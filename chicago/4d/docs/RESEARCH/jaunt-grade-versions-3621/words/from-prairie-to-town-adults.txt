== opening
JAUNT: FROM PRAIRIE TO TOWN. Sunset, and you have come up the open shore from the south, saddle-sore and filthy. This is how many people first saw Chicago. When Philo Carpenter landed on this shore in July 1832, Joe Ellis carried him to the fort 'with an ox team', and inland he saw only 'the flat prairie, covered with sedgy grass'.

Three years later the prairie is being carved up and sold. Since the land office opened, buyers 'filled the streets and overflowed improvised camps on the adjacent prairies', and before the year is out, sales of land 'to the amount of 370,043 acres were made in 1835'. Many of the men camped out there are speculators, not farmers. They mean to buy cheap and sell dear, and some of them will. Ride north past the fort, find a store on the river street, and reach a public house before dark.

== stop factors-house
Start at the U.S. Factor's House below the stockade. Hubbard, who knew it from 1818, recalled a two-story, squared-log house, and Harrison's 1830 plan letters it here. It was a government trading house once, then sold to the American Fur Company in 1822: the old trade in furs is finished, and the new trade is in land. Beyond the Reservation the shore lies open: Wright's 1834 map names that ground Fractional Section 15, and nobody had bought it yet. Within a year someone will. It is the same shore where Philo Carpenter came ashore in 1832.

== stop the-stockade
North to Fort Dearborn's stockade at the river mouth. Juliette Kinzie, who lived inside it in 1831, wrote of high pickets and large gates to the north and south. Troops had held the post since June 1832, so today it is a working garrison. By the end of 1836 the soldiers will be gone for good. Look from outside; the gates are shown shut.

== stop the-store
West along the river to South Water and LaSalle and P. F. W. Peck's store, the village's first two-storey frame, its lumber 'brought from Detroit by a boat'. His card in the Democrat offered dry goods, hardware, groceries, crockery, boots and shoes, and hats: everything the road wore out of you, at a merchant's price. In May 1833 a newly arrived minister lodged in its unfinished loft, preaching to a town that badly needed it.

== stop the-public-house
Last, on to Lake and Market and the Sauganash, on the east bank of the South Branch. Wau-Bun recalled it as 'a pretentious white two-story building, with bright-blue wooden shutters'; on 10 June 1835 the Democrat reported it repaired and newly fitted up. Moses and Kirkland call it 'the most attractive hostlery the city possessed'. Whether a bed is free tonight is anybody's guess. In a land-sale summer, the floor is the likelier offer.

== ending in-town
You reach the Sauganash's door having come in from the south: from the last house below the fort, along the river street to a store, and on to a public house. Three years ago this was sedgy grass and an ox team. Tonight it is a town of speculators, drinkers and strangers, and you are one of them now.
