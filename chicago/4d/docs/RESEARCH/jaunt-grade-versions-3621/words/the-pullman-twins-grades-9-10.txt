== premise
George Pullman left his twin sons a mere $3,000 a year and a name they could never live up to. One is already dead. At dusk in 1904 the other is still riding recklessly, and his mother is still paying.

== opening
JAUNT: THE LAST PULLMAN TWIN. Dusk on a Monday in November 1904, as the lamps are lit along Prairie Avenue.

Four stops, from the grandest house on the street to the home of the one Pullman child who turned out exactly as her father hoped. George Pullman built sleeping cars, a company town and an immense fortune, but he could not build sons. His twins, George Jr. and Sanger, were born in 1875 into more wealth than almost anyone in America, and they spent their lives demonstrating to the city what such wealth can do to a boy.

Begin at the Pullman mansion, where the widow lives with her shades half drawn.

== stop widow
1729 Prairie, the Pullman mansion, the most imposing pile on the block. Harriett Sanger married George Pullman in 1867, and they had four children: Florence, Harriett, and in 1875 the twins, George Jr. and Walter Sanger, whom everyone calls Sanger. George Pullman died in 1897, leaving his widow this house, which she still maintains. To the twins he left a verdict, written into his will: "Pullman's will stipulated that the twins were to be given a yearly stipend of only $3,000". Their father understood them. Mrs. Pullman's carriage waits at the door; Henry Windish, the German-born coachman, has it ready, and Miss Robinson, her companion, accompanies her. The mansion will be demolished in 1922. Walk around to the conservatory, where the boys grew up.

== caption widow
Harriett Pullman emerges on her companion's arm to her waiting carriage while the housekeeper watches from the doorway.

== stop boys
The Pullman conservatory, all glass and palms along the side of the house. The twins were raised here and educated at home on Prairie Avenue, and rumor claimed they were "arranging and promoting pit bull fights". As teenagers they "frequently were seen riding around Chicago in cabs filled with champagne bottles". Their father sent them east to a preparatory school for Harvard, where "Sanger and George were ranked 147 and 148 out of a class of 148 students", last and next to last, side by side, as inseparable as twins ought to be. And the more intelligent twin was Sanger, "widely acknowledged as the smarter of the 2", though his intelligence never rescued him. Cross the street to view the house where the other twin's story concluded.

== caption boys
Samuel Whatter Jr., the groom, leans on the stable gate beside the conservatory, chatting with a stable hand.

== stop george
From the west side of the street, look back at the Pullman mansion in the dusk. The other twin is gone. George Jr. secretly married Lynn Fernald, "his brother's betrothed", stealing his own twin's fiancée. A man named Frederick Bowers sued him for $100,000, "eventually settling for $50,000". Then in November 1901 George died of pneumonia at twenty-six, and the World printed the headline "Death of George Pullman a Warning to Rich Fathers". So Sanger is the last twin, twenty-nine now and still hopeless with money: expelled from the New York Athletic Club for unpaid dues, his debts so tangled that a creditor once seized his sister-in-law's luggage for five months. His mother has rescued him before. Walk south to the sister who never needed rescuing.

== caption george
Two gentlemen stroll past the Pullman mansion and pause to look back at it.

== stop sister
1912 Prairie, the Lowden residence. Florence, the eldest Pullman child, married Frank Orren Lowden, an attorney, and here they live with their children. Because Lowden is a lawyer, when trouble strikes the family it tends to arrive at his office. Their mother once "paid the unnamed woman $10,000 to leave Sanger and never return". The Lowden children play at their nurse's feet on a quiet Monday evening, as the twins once did on this very street. The house will be demolished in 1939. Meanwhile the last twin is still out there somewhere, riding recklessly.

== ending ride
The walk ends here. Sanger Pullman lived nine more months. In August 1905 he "died after being thrown from a horse and carriage after yet another wild ride". His mother had now buried her husband and both of her sons; her husband lay at Graceland in a "lead-lined mahogany coffin, which was then sealed inside a block of concrete", so that nobody could ever reach him. Nothing could reach the boys either: not their father, not their money, not their mother's $10,000. The World had said it in 1901, and it remained true: "a Warning to Rich Fathers".
