== opening
JAUNT: THE WALK TO THE LAKE. Daybreak on a Monday in November 1904, at the south end of Prairie Avenue.

Four stops, north along Prairie toward the lake. The Keiths are the steadiest family on the street: three brothers, three houses, one great wholesale firm. But one Monday morning eight years ago, at about this hour, the gentlest of them got up after another sleepless night, put on his hat and coat, walked out of his front door and went to the lake to drown himself.

This is a walk about a suicide, and about a family that watched a good man's mind wear away for years and could not stop it. Start at his door, where his widow still lives.

== stop door
1906 Prairie, at daybreak. The widow here is Mrs. Edson Keith. Eight years ago her husband, Edson, a merchant, "disappeared from his home at 1906 Prairie avenue about 6 o'clock in the morning". He was 63 and had come to Chicago in 1854. For at least ten years he had been sleepless and ill. That last night he paced the hallways again, and "his butler found him in a state of deliriousness at 2 AM". By six he was gone. Paul Rasmussen, the coachman, is crossing the yard this morning, and Mrs. Keith comes to the door to look at the weather. Walk north to the brother's house next door.

== stop brother
1900 Prairie, the house of his brother Elbridge, built in 1870 in the Second Empire style that once ruled this street. Elbridge and his wife saw it coming. A few weeks before that Monday they had spoken with Edson's wife, and her words were these: "I … suspect that he was losing control of his mind through long suffering." They had the warning, and the houses, and the money, and none of it was enough. His personal secretary had never heard him say anything nasty or unkind about anyone. That was Edson Keith: the gentle brother. Elbridge is up early this morning too, and he still passes his brother's door. Walk north up Prairie, the way Edson went.

== stop firm
1808 Prairie, the Osborn R. Keith house. He and Albert Faxon founded the family firm on Lake Street in 1858, and by the 1880s Edson Keith & Company was selling $4.5 million a year, with two hundred people in Chicago and five hundred more in a Milwaukee factory. Three Keith houses on one street, and a fortune, and none of it bought him a night's sleep. In 1904 the house is Stanley Field's, but the street still calls it a Keith house. Edson walked past it in the dark that Monday, going north, toward the lake. The house will be razed in 1968, all but the north wall that screens the Glessner courtyard. Keep going north to Sixteenth Street.

== stop lake
Sixteenth Street, at the north end of the avenue. East of here, past the railroad, is the lake. This is as far as the street goes. Edson Keith went on, into the water. On the beach that Monday morning someone found a hat and a coat, and fishermen on the beach gave an account of what they saw. The papers do not agree where: one says the foot of Sixteenth Street, another the foot of Thirteenth. All day Monday they searched. The household learned it only on Tuesday, from the newspaper, which put it plainly: he "committed suicide Monday morning by drowning himself in Lake Michigan". Stand here and look east at first light. The lake is grey and flat and very large.

== ending dragged
End of the walk. The news came to the family "with paralyzing suddenness", and the dispatch reported that "the lake is now being dragged for the recovery of the remains." Edson Keith had made his will the year before, leaving $300,000 each to his wife Susan and their two sons.

His wife stayed on at 1906 Prairie, and his brother next door. The street remembers him as the gentle brother, the one who never said an unkind word about anyone, who walked out of his own front door at six one Monday morning and did not come back.
