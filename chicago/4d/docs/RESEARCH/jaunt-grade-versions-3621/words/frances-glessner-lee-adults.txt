== opening
JAUNT: THE DAUGHTER WHO MADE THINGS. A grey Monday morning, 21 November 1904, at the north end of the Glessner family's world.

Four stops, from the twin houses the Glessners built for their two children, down to the great stone house at Eighteenth Street where the daughter grew up. Frances Glessner Lee is 26. She was taught at home, married at 19, and is now a lawyer's wife with two small children, living in a house her parents built for her, a block from the house her parents live in. So far her life has followed the path laid out for a rich man's daughter. She is the last person on this street anyone would expect to change the way violent and unexplained deaths are investigated. Watch her hands, and watch her marriage.

Start at 1700 Prairie, at the door of the young Lees.

== stop lees
1700 Prairie, the north half of a pair of twin townhouses. John and Frances Glessner built them for their two children, and the young families moved in in 1902: a gift, and a way of keeping the children close. This one belongs to their daughter Frances and her husband Blewett Lee, a lawyer. She was born in 1878, the only daughter, tutored at home, and on 9 February 1898, at 19, she married Lee in her parents' house; the cook baked the wedding cake. The couple "at first appeared happy". Now there is a boy, John, who is six, and a baby girl born last year. Watch the front door. Her husband is leaving for his office, and she stays on the step.

== stop george
1706 Prairie, the other twin, shares a wall with the Lees. Here lives her brother George Glessner, his wife Alice and their four children, the newest, Emily, born this year. George's passion is photography; he photographed these houses going up in 1901, and in 1902 he was made a manager at International Harvester. His is the life the street understands: a job, a camera, a nursery full of children. His sister's life is supposed to look the same from the sidewalk, and it does. But her son will say, years later, that what broke her marriage was her "creative urge coupled with high manual dexterity—the desire to make things—which [Lee] did not share." A woman with clever hands and nothing to do with them, through a wall from a man with everything.

== stop mother
1800 Prairie, the house she grew up in, built like a fortress at Eighteenth Street. Her mother, Frances Glessner, is 56, and this year she has done something few ladies of Prairie Avenue would do. She set up a workbench in the basement and began to make silver with her own hands, taking lessons from Annibale Fogliata, a master jewelry craftsman of Hull House, and Madeline Yale Wynne, a Chicago silversmith. Every piece carries her mark, "a G encircling a honeybee", for the bees she keeps. She has kept a journal since 1879; she misses nothing. The daughter comes to the door. Inside is a mother who has decided, at 56, that a lady may also work.

== stop court
The courtyard, the sunny private heart of the fortress. The mother shows the daughter what her hands have made: a woman of 56, given permission by nobody, doing exact, patient work at a bench. Frances Glessner Lee is clever, and she is bored, and her marriage is "not a happy one". She was married at 19 to a man who does not share the one thing she most wants to do. Everything that happens to her next starts with what she is looking at now: small things, made precisely, by hand, in a house where every detail is a clue. She will have one more child. Then, in a world where a respectable woman does not leave her husband, she will leave hers.

== ending nutshell
End of the walk. Martha was born in 1906. In 1914 the Lees divorced. Years later Frances Glessner Lee made the Nutshell Studies of Unexplained Death: tiny, exact rooms where someone has died, built at one inch to a foot with dental tools, every clue in place. She wrote to an adviser, "I found myself constantly tempted to add more clues and details…stop me when I go too far." In 1931 she gave Harvard $250,000 for legal medicine, and in 1943 New Hampshire made her the first woman state police captain in the country. Detectives still train on her dollhouses of death. The girl who was tutored at home and married at 19 taught the police how to look at a corpse.
