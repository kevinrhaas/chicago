== premise
A summer dusk on Millionaire's Row: Pullman, Glessner, Kimball, both Marshall Fields and Armour, and how each amassed an industrial fortune within a single lifetime.

== opening
JAUNT: FAMOUS NAMES. A summer dusk on Prairie Avenue, 1904. The lamplighters have completed their rounds, the carriages are returning from the Loop, and behind these walls reside some of the wealthiest families in America. Contemporaries designate the avenue Millionaire's Row: at one point "sixteen of the 60 members of the Commercial Club of Chicago lived on Prairie Avenue".

Six residences on foot, from 1729 to 2115 Prairie, proceeding south: the industrialist who accustomed America to sleeping aboard trains, an agricultural-machinery manufacturer in a granite fortress, the piano magnate, the merchant prince and his heir, and the meatpacker who utilized everything but the squeal. Every one of these fortunes originated in Chicago within a single generation, a velocity of accumulation that defined the Gilded Age.

Begin at the corner of Eighteenth Street, beside the most massive residence on the block.

== stop pullman
1729 Prairie, the Pullman mansion, constructed between 1870 and 1876, incorporating a theater of approximately two hundred seats, a pipe organ, a bowling alley, and subsequently a palm room and an immense conservatory. George M. Pullman prospered early by elevating Chicago from its mud, jacking up entire structures "on average six feet without causing them any damage". He then manufactured sleeping cars, and when Lincoln's body traveled to Springfield aboard one, "hundreds of thousands of people lined the route in homage". Pullman died in 1897, reviled by the strikers of his company town; his widow maintains this residence, which will be demolished in 1922. He likewise despised one feature of this avenue, which stands directly behind you.

== stop glessner
1800 Prairie, John J. Glessner's residence. Pullman protested, "I do not know what I have ever done to have that thing staring me in the face every time I go out of my door". Glessner derived his wealth from agricultural machinery for prairie farming, and two years ago his firm was consolidated into the colossal International Harvester, with Glessner as vice president. His architect, Henry Hobson Richardson, died weeks before construction commenced in 1886; the family took possession on 1 December 1887 and remain in residence. Six decades hence the house will narrowly escape demolition, rescued in 1966 "for just $35,000". Across the avenue, listen for pianos.

== stop kimball
1801 Prairie, the Kimball mansion of 1890, a French chateau designed by Solon S. Beman, with a precipitously pitched slate roof and a cylindrical tower addressing Eighteenth Street. William W. Kimball arrived in Chicago in 1857, "beginning with only four pianos", and by 1881 his factory was "churning out around 100 pianos and organs every week". This constitutes his final summer: he "died at his home in Chicago on December 16, 1904". Proceed one block south to the wealthiest individual in the city.

== stop field
1905 Prairie, Marshall Field's mansion, designed by the New York architect Richard Morris Hunt. Field emigrated from Massachusetts and became "the wealthiest and most powerful businessman in Chicago", valued at $125 million by 1905. His department store's governing principle was "Give the lady what she wants", and his female clientele generated his fortune. He and George Pullman both acquired property here early, establishing the precedent Chicago's elite subsequently followed. His residence will endure until 1955, when it will be demolished. Next year his world will disintegrate, and the disintegration commences two doors south.

== stop field-jr
1919 Prairie, Marshall Field Jr.'s residence. He acquired it in 1890 and expanded it repeatedly; in 1902 D. H. Burnham and Company constructed an enormous addition. Upstairs reside his children, including Marshall Field III, aged ten. In November 1905 the father will be shot within this residence, and within two months the patriarch will also be deceased, of pneumonia contracted on a New Year's golf course. The boy will be raised in England. For the moment, the windows glow. One residence remains, at the walk's southern terminus.

== stop armour
2115 Prairie, the Kelley-Armour residence, constructed in 1871 and occupied by the Armour family for nearly fifty years. During the gold rush, Philip D. Armour eschewed prospecting and "started a business employing out-of-work miners to construct sluices". Returning to Chicago, he established Armour and Company, "the world's largest food processing and chemical manufacturing enterprise", boasting that he utilized "everything but the squeal". In 1893 he contributed a million dollars to found the Armour Institute of Technology. He died in 1901; his widow remains and will outlast virtually everyone on the avenue. The residence itself will be demolished in 1937.

== leg 0
Across Eighteenth Street. The Battle of Fort Dearborn of 1812 was fought in the sand hills beneath this end of the avenue.

== leg 1
Across the avenue to the turret, where Kimball's chateau confronts Glessner's fortress.

== leg 2
South past the Coleman and Sears residences. By 1886 "the finest mansions in the city, each equipped with its own carriage house, stood on Prairie Avenue".

== leg 3
Two doors south, where father and son resided side by side.

== leg 4
Onward past Twentieth Street, along the extended eastern sidewalk to the final great residence.

== ending finish
The walk concludes at 2115 Prairie. Mrs. Armour remained: the district marker records her among the avenue's final residents, dying here in 1927. Most of her neighbors had previously departed. After Bertha Palmer constructed her castle on Lake Shore Drive, "the elite residents began to move north", and progressively these mansions were converted into boarding houses, warehouses and parking lots, a decline as rapid as the fortunes that had built them.

A few survived. Glessner's fortress endures, as does Kimball's chateau. Return after dark some evening, when the avenue recounts its ghost stories.
