== premise
Mansard roofs pierced by dormers: six Second Empire houses from 1620 to 2115 Prairie, representing the avenue's most popular architectural style.

== opening
JAUNT: SECOND EMPIRE STYLE. A spring sunset, 1904. The mansard roof originated in Paris: a steep slope concealing an entire additional story, crowned with iron cresting and pierced with dormers. By the 1880s it dominated the avenue, and the Glessner House Museum identifies it as Prairie Avenue's most popular style.

Six houses on foot, from 1620 to 2115 Prairie, north to south: a pioneer's roof that swells into a crown, Pullman's palace, a house that purchased a mansard to keep pace with its neighbors, the avenue's last survivor, the richest man in Chicago, and the meatpacker's widow. Watch the sunset illuminate the slate.

Begin at 1620, with a roof engraved for a magazine in 1874.

== stop law
1620 Prairie, Robert Law's house, engraved in 1874 for a series on the homes of Chicago: two stories above a raised basement beneath a slate mansard with round-headed dormers, its northern section rising into a taller curved crown. Compare the engraving's roof with the one before you, then walk a block south to the most magnificent mansard on the avenue.

== stop pullman
1729 Prairie, George Pullman's mansion of 1870 to 1876, described by the district register as a brownstone block beneath a mansard with dormers and iron cresting, entered through a portico on Prairie Avenue. Inside are a theater of about two hundred seats, a pipe organ and a bowling alley, and later a palm room and a spacious conservatory by Solon S. Beman. Pullman died in 1897, and his widow still maintains it. It will be demolished in 1922. Across Eighteenth Street stands a smaller house that refused to be left behind.

== stop jones
1834 Prairie, Fernando Jones's house, built in 1866 as an Italianate design with a low roof. Two decades later his neighbors all possessed mansards, so in 1886 the house "received a mansard addition to bring it into fashion with many of its neighbors": an entirely new top story, acquired purely to keep pace. That is precisely how a style conquers a street. The hat comes off permanently in 1942, when the house is demolished. Two doors south stands the single Second Empire house that will outlive them all.

== stop e-keith
1900 Prairie, Elbridge G. Keith's house of 1870, designed by John W. Roberts. The district marker describes it as "the last surviving example of the popular Second Empire style, which dominated the street by the 1880s". Keith was a bank president, and his house will remain standing long after the palaces surrounding it have vanished. Locate the slate mansard above the corner bay, then cross the avenue to the greatest fortune of all.

== stop field
1905 Prairie, Marshall Field's mansion, designed by Richard Morris Hunt and described by the district register as a broad brick and stone residence beneath a mansard, with an angular corner bay, dormers and a central portico. Field, "the wealthiest and most powerful businessman in Chicago", lived here. Imagine his carriage turning in at sunset; the mansion will be demolished in 1955. One more mansard awaits at the far end of the row.

== stop armour
2115 Prairie, the Kelley-Armour house of 1871, which the district marker calls a stately Second Empire residence: a patterned slate mansard with cresting above a central arched doorway beneath a pediment. Built for the lumberman David Kelley, it became the home of the meatpacker Philip D. Armour, who boasted of using "everything but the squeal". His widow still lives here and will be among the avenue's final residents. The house will be demolished ten years after her death, in 1937.

== ending finish
The Second Empire walk ends here. Count the mansards you passed: by the 1880s they lined the avenue, and even older houses acquired them.

Then taste shifted, the families migrated north, and most of these roofs were demolished. Only Elbridge Keith's survives. Return and examine it once more before the light fades.
