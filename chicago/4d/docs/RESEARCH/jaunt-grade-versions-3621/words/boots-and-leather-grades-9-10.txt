== premise
Outfit a rider for tomorrow's road: boots at Holbrook's on South Water Street, a view across the forks from Wolf Point toward Miller's tannery, where the town's leather industry began, the saddler at Lake and Canal, and the Green Tree to lay everything out.

== opening
JAUNT: BOOTS, LEATHER AND THE ROAD. On this spring afternoon the mud is finally drying, and you ride out tomorrow. Strangers pour through this town "by land and by water", and most of them, like you, intend on "locating farther west". Before you join them you require an outfit, and an outfit means leather: boots for the rider and tack for the horse.

Here is the complication: in this town those belong to two different trades, practiced in two different shops on opposite sides of the river. Purchase boots on South Water Street, look across the forks toward the tannery where Chicago's leather began, visit the saddler at Lake and Canal, and lay everything out at the Green Tree tonight.

== stop ready-made
Begin your preparations one door east of Dearborn on South Water Street. In June 1835 John Holbrook advertised hats, clothing, boots and shoes, wholesale and retail, as agent for the manufacturers, with fresh additions arriving every fifteen or twenty days. These are manufactured goods, shipped in from the East and sold from stock, because in a town expanding this rapidly, nobody has time to wait for a cobbler.

== stop tannery
Across the South Branch to Wolf Point. On the far side of the North Branch, in 1831, John Miller built a log house on the point and operated it as a tannery, considered Chicago's first recorded factory, and Andreas names Benjamin Hall, tanner, as his partner from 1832. Before the boats delivered boots by the crate, leather here began as hides soaking in a pit. Whether hides were still being worked this summer, no source reveals.

== narrative tannery
Leather must begin as hides somewhere, and here it began on the point.

== stop saddler
South down the west bank to Lake and Canal, where Goss and Cobb opened in 1833, the year Silas B. Cobb arrived, making saddles, harness, bridles, martingales and trunks to order and promising that repairing would be immediately attended to. The partnership dissolved in February 1835, and Cobb's June card announces that he will continue. Neither printed card mentions boots or shoes.

== consequence saddler ask-boots
That is not his trade: his card lists saddles, harness, bridles, martingales and trunks, while boots and shoes were Holbrook's line, back across the river.

== stop road
East along Lake to West Water and the Green Tree, which James Kinzie built in 1833 as the first house here constructed for hotel purposes, "so called from an oak tree standing close by". Old settlers recalled Chester Ingersoll keeping it from 1834 to 1837, and it stood until 1880, when it was moved to Milwaukee Avenue. Lay the entire outfit out on the floor for the morning.

== leg 0
Whatever you purchased rides in front of you across the saddle.

== ending another-day
Another day in town, then. Whatever the outfit lacks is advertised on a card somewhere along these streets, and the road will wait, because a day late is not the same as unprepared.

== ending equipped
Boots from the clothier and a bridle from the saddler: leather for the rider and leather for the horse, from two trades that never performed each other's work. You ride out at first light, equipped to travel.

== ending trunk-bound
A trunk will not ride on a saddle, so the morning begins with locating a wagon traveling your direction: the baggage is ready, but the means of carrying it is not.

== ending half-ready
You ride out with what you possess. Boots for the rider and a bridle for the horse were the outfit's two leathers, one from each trade, and whatever is missing, the road will demand before long.

== keepsake text
A fictional tally of one rider's outfit: what came from the clothier, what from the saddler, and what was left behind. No real sale, price or customer of either shop is written on it.
