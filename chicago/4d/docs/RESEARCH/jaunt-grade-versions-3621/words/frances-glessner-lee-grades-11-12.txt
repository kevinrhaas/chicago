== premise
At twenty-six, Frances Glessner Lee possesses an attorney husband, two children and an elegant townhouse her parents constructed. Her hands crave something different, and one block south her mother has just discovered it.

== opening
JAUNT: THE DAUGHTER WHO MADE THINGS. An overcast Monday morning, 21 November 1904, at the northern boundary of the Glessner family's domain.

Four stops, from the twin townhouses the Glessners constructed for their two children to the monumental stone residence at Eighteenth Street where the daughter was raised. Frances Glessner Lee is twenty-six, privately educated, married at nineteen, and the least probable person on this avenue to revolutionize the investigation of suspicious deaths. Attend carefully to her hands.

Begin at 1700 Prairie, at the entrance of the young Lees.

== stop lees
1700 Prairie, the northern half of a pair of twin townhouses that John and Frances Glessner commissioned for their two children, whose families took possession in 1902. This half belongs to their daughter Frances and her husband Blewett Lee, an attorney. Born in 1878, the youngest child and only daughter, she was "tutored at home" in the stone residence down the avenue and spent fourteen months touring Europe with her aunt before her debut. On 9 February 1898, at nineteen, she married Lee in her parents' drawing rooms, and the family's cook baked the wedding cake. There is now a son, John, aged six, and a daughter born the previous year. Observe the entrance: her husband departs for his office while she remains on the threshold. Her brother occupies the adjoining house.

== caption lees
Blewett Lee descends his steps and departs for his office, while his wife and their son remain at the entrance.

== stop george
1706 Prairie, the other twin, sharing a party wall with the Lees. Here resides her brother George Glessner, his wife Alice and their four children, Elizabeth, Frances, John and the newborn Emily. George pursues photography with lifelong dedication; he documented these very houses under construction in 1901, and in 1902 he was appointed a utility manager at International Harvester. His existence is precisely what this avenue comprehends: a respectable position, an absorbing pastime, a crowded nursery. His sister's existence is expected to appear identical from the sidewalk, and superficially it does. Nevertheless her son would eventually attribute the disintegration of her marriage to her "creative urge coupled with high manual dexterity—the desire to make things—which [Lee] did not share". Walk one block south to discover that impulse's origin.

== caption george
George and Alice Glessner emerge with their children for a morning promenade.

== stop mother
1800 Prairie, her childhood home, constructed with the defensive severity of a fortress at Eighteenth Street. Her mother, Frances Glessner, is fifty-six, and this year she has undertaken something few ladies of Prairie Avenue would contemplate. In 1904 she installed a workbench in the basement and began fabricating silver with her own hands, instructed by Annibale Fogliata, a master jewelry craftsman associated with Hull House, and by Madeline Yale Wynne, a distinguished Chicago silversmith. Every piece bears her hallmark, "a G encircling a honeybee", commemorating her beekeeping. She has maintained a meticulous journal since 1879, and virtually nothing escapes her observation. The daughter arrives at the entrance. Within is a mother who resolved, at fifty-six, to master a demanding manual discipline.

== caption mother
Frances Glessner Lee approaches her mother's entrance, and her mother opens the door herself.

== stop court
The courtyard, the sunlit and secluded center of the fortress. The mother displays what her hands have accomplished, and one can readily imagine what the daughter perceives: a woman of fifty-six who solicited nobody's permission, performing exacting, patient, beautiful work at a bench. Frances Glessner Lee is intelligent, restless and intellectually unfulfilled, and her marriage is "not a happy one". Her son would later identify the "creative urge coupled with high manual dexterity" that her husband never shared. Everything subsequent in her extraordinary life originates in what she observes at this moment: diminutive objects, fabricated with precision, by hand, inside a residence where every architectural detail operates as evidence. She will bear one more child. Then she will depart, and construct something nobody on this avenue could possibly have envisioned.

== ending nutshell
The walk concludes here. Martha was born in 1906, and in 1914 the Lees divorced. Decades afterward Frances Glessner Lee constructed the Nutshell Studies of Unexplained Death: miniature, scrupulously accurate interiors in which a death has occurred, executed at a scale of one inch to a foot with dental instruments and a carpenter's assistance, every clue deliberately positioned. She confessed to an adviser, "I found myself constantly tempted to add more clues and details…stop me when I go too far". In 1931 she endowed Harvard with $250,000 to establish legal medicine, and in 1943 New Hampshire appointed her the nation's first woman state police captain. Investigators still train on her dioramas. The privately tutored daughter of Prairie Avenue taught professional detectives how to observe.
