== opening
JAUNT: WORK ON THE WATERFRONT. Morning, and you need a job, because your money will not last the week. The good news: Chicago in 1835 needs everybody. Buildings go up as fast as lumber comes in, every boat needs unloading, and the papers carry notices headed 'Wanted, immediately'. The bad news: the strangers keep coming 'by land and by water', in wagons, 'on horseback and on foot', and half of them want work too, and are as hungry as you.

Choose what you can do, read the notices, ask at two of Newberry & Dole's houses, and plan your next move over a breakfast you can barely afford. Nobody here owes you a living, and nobody will notice if you fail to make one.

== stop notices
John Calhoun's Democrat, printed here at first and since May over Jones & King's hardware, wants 'an apprentice at this office to the printing business', promising 'A good opportunity is offered by an immediate application'. On 17 June Jones, King & Co. printed: 'Wanted, immediately, one copper smith, and one tin and sheet iron worker.' A town building this fast needs printers for its notices and tinsmiths for its stovepipes: since 1833 every stovepipe through a roof must be 'guarded by tin or sheet iron', or the owner pays $5. A trade is worth money here. A pair of hands without one is worth much less.

== stop packing
Andreas puts George Dole's 1832 warehouse and slaughter yard near Lake and Dearborn. Dole began the town's packing trade here, 'the nucleus of one of those immense industries' that made Chicago famous: killing and cutting hogs, cold, bloody work that the city will one day do on an immense scale. But packing is winter work, hogs killed in December, and by 1834 the partners had moved their killing to the South Branch. In July there is nothing to pack, and no wage to be had.

== stop forwarding
Newberry & Dole have advertised as forwarding and commission merchants since November 1833; in June the American named them agents for the steamboat Michigan to Detroit and Buffalo. When the schooner Illinois came up through the new harbour cut in July 1834, she was cheered at their wharf. Every vessel since has needed hands to unload her, barrels and crates on bent backs, and nobody advertises for those: carrying is the work that goes to whoever is standing on the wharf when the sail comes in, for whatever the clerk decides to pay.

== stop breakfast
Breakfast at the Exchange Coffee House at Lake and Wells, built by Mark Beaubien and kept by the Murphys since August 1834. The county's old tariff put breakfast at 25 cents, two nights' lodging, and a day's carrying may not pay much more. Lay out the morning: two notices for trades, a yard waiting on winter, and a wharf that never stops. Count what is left in your pocket, and how many breakfasts it buys.

== ending apprentice
You will answer the Democrat's notice for an apprentice to the printing business. It was printed on 20 May, six weeks ago, so the place may be filled, and an apprentice works for his keep and little more. Ask anyway, and ask early.

== ending tin-and-copper
You will take your tin and copper to Jones, King & Co., whose notice of 17 June wanted both trades immediately. A fortnight is long enough for someone hungrier to have answered first, so go before breakfast is cold.

== ending no-notice-for-a-back
No notice you read asked for a strong back. The papers advertise trades; carrying is found on the wharf, asked for in person and paid for at the employer's pleasure. Tomorrow, try the river first, and early, before the other strong backs.

== ending chit-for-the-boat
You carry a chit for a day's carrying when a vessel next unloads at the forwarding house, with no wage written on it, which means the wage is whatever they say on the day. Be on the wharf when a sail comes in.

== ending remembered-for-the-winter
You asked to be remembered when there is packing to do. That is a winter promise, with no wage, no name and no date: something to hold on to until December, when the hogs come in and the killing starts, if you can last that long.

== ending a-days-work-in-prospect
No work today, but a plan for tomorrow: two notices that asked for trades, a yard waiting on the winter, and a firm that forwards freight by the boatload. A day's work is in prospect, which in this town is not the same as a day's pay.
