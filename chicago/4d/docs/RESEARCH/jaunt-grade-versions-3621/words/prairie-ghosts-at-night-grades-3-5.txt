== premise
A night walk down Millionaire's Row: a battlefield under your feet, a rich man sealed in concrete, a widow who rattles the windows, a brother lost in the lake, and a gunshot at 1919.

== opening
JAUNT: GHOSTS OF PRAIRIE AVENUE. It is an autumn night in 1904. The gas lamps are lit and the carriages are home. This is Millionaire's Row. Big houses for George Pullman, Marshall Field, John Glessner and Philip Armour stand side by side. The richest families in Chicago lock their doors at night against everyone but their ghosts.

There are seven stops, on foot, from Eighteenth Street south to 1919 Prairie. You will meet a battlefield under the street and a rich man sealed in concrete. You will meet an architect who never saw his best house, and a widow who rattles the windows. There is a man lost in the lake, and a gunshot no one has explained. The ghost stories are legends. The people in them were real.

Watch the shadows. Start where the street starts, on the battleground of 1812.

== stop battle-ground
This is Prairie at Eighteenth, after dark. Before the big houses, there were sand hills here. On 15 August 1812 the Battle of Fort Dearborn was fought across them. The people who died lay in the sand for four years. For a hundred years a big cottonwood tree marked the place. A newspaper writer said the Pullman house in front of you stands about where the battle was. In 1893 George Pullman put up a statue of the battle near this corner. Legend says that on clear, cold nights the people who fell still walk here. The settlers search for their families, and the Native people keep watch over their land. The man who put up the statue is not walking anywhere. His family made sure of that.

== stop pullman
This is 1729 Prairie, the Pullman house. George Pullman got rich by lifting Chicago out of its mud. He jacked up whole buildings about six feet without breaking them. Then he built railroad sleeping cars. When President Lincoln died, his body rode home to Springfield in one, and huge crowds lined the tracks. In 1894 Pullman cut his workers' pay but kept their rent the same, and they went on strike. When he died in 1897, his family feared someone would dig up his grave. So, the story goes, they sealed his coffin inside a big block of concrete, with steel rails bolted on top. Mrs. Pullman still lives here. The house will be torn down in 1922. And Mr. Pullman hated the house across the street.

== stop glessner
This is 1800 Prairie, the Glessner House, a stone fortress. Pullman grumbled that he did not know what he had done to have "that thing staring me in the face" every time he went out. Its architect, Henry Richardson, died five weeks before the building began. He never saw it built. But legend says he came to see it anyway. The family felt someone there. Cold spots creep into warm rooms. Some people say you might see his spirit standing on the staircase. One visitor called it a friendly feeling. The Glessners' daughter, Frances, grew up to build tiny dollhouse rooms. Each one shows a real mystery, to help train detectives. Now turn around. The house across the street is rattling.

== stop kimball
This is 1801 Prairie, the Kimball house, with its steep slate roof and round tower. William Kimball started selling pianos in 1857 with just four of them. Later his factory made about a hundred pianos and organs every week. He and his wife, Evaline, filled their front hall with famous old paintings. This winter the music stops. He will die here on 16 December 1904. Evaline will live on here for many years. People say that in her last years she held parties for empty rooms she thought were full of guests. Now the paintings hang in the Art Institute. The big windows here shake and rattle when there is no wind at all. Is Evaline looking for her pictures? Next is a family with a sadder story.

== stop keith
This is 1900 Prairie, the house of Elbridge Keith, with its black roof against the sky. A few doors south lives the widow of his brother, Edson. Edson Keith had not slept well in ten years. Early one Monday morning, he slipped out of his house. He walked to the lake at Sixteenth Street. He went into the water and drowned. He never came home. Or did he? Ghost hunters say his ghost has been seen at his brother's house, sometimes in the windows. Look up at the dark windows as you pass. Across the street, the richest man in Chicago is asleep.

== stop field
This is 1905 Prairie, Marshall Field's house. "Give the lady what she wants", the store owner said. The ladies shopped at his store and made him the richest businessman in Chicago. Tonight everything looks safe, but it is not. In thirteen months his son, who lives next door, will be shot. Six weeks after that, Mr. Field will play golf on New Year's Day 1906 with Robert Todd Lincoln, Abraham Lincoln's son. Field will catch a bad sickness of the lungs and die on 16 January. Father and son will both be gone in one winter. The house will be torn down in 1955. Walk on to the son's house, the one with the footsteps.

== stop field-jr
This is 1919 Prairie, the house of Marshall Field Jr., with its huge new addition. Next year, in November, a gunshot will go off upstairs. He will die five days later in the hospital. He will say the gun went off by accident, and the officials will agree. But many people in Chicago will not believe it. Some will say he was really shot at a club with a bad name a few blocks west, and carried home. Nobody ever proved it. Later owners said they heard footsteps in empty halls and strange cries in the night. One family even called in a priest to send the spirit away. Some houses keep their secrets. This one keeps its ghost.

== ending finish
The walk ends here, at 1919 Prairie. Here is one more ghost for the road. Years later the Clarke House, said to be the oldest house in the city, will be moved to this neighborhood. People say that in the park beside it a woman in a settler's dress drifts among the bushes after dark. And carriage horses trot past that always have their bits in their mouths, but never their harnesses.

Sleep well, Prairie Avenue.
