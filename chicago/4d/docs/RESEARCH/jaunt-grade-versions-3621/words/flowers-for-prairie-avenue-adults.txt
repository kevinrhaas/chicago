== opening
JAUNT: FLOWERS FOR PRAIRIE AVENUE. A spring morning in 1904. You are thirteen and you carry for a florist, and this morning's basket holds four orders for Prairie Avenue, the street where Chicago's money chose to live. Each bunch has a house number on its tag.

The order of the round is yours. The houses and their people are as the directories, the census and the histories give them; the runner is invented.

== stop glessner
1800 Prairie, the Glessner house, on the corner of Eighteenth Street: the first bunch. Frances Macbeth Glessner has kept a journal since 1879 and will keep it for forty years. She keeps bees, holds the Monday Morning Reading Class in the library, and this year has put a silversmith's bench in the basement to work silver with her own hands. She and John Glessner have lived here since 1 December 1887 and will stay until they die, she in 1932, he in 1936. Three bunches left. North or south?

== stop lee
1700 Prairie, a block north, one of the twin houses the Glessners built for their two married children. Their daughter Frances married Blewett Lee in February 1898 and moved in within a few years; the 1904 Blue Book prints the Lee household here. Nobody on the avenue would guess what she will make of herself: in legal medicine, the Nutshell Studies of Unexplained Death, miniature crime scenes built to train the people who investigate deaths, still in use. Hand in the flowers and go south past Twentieth Street.

== stop jones
1834 Prairie, the Fernando Jones house. Jane Grahame Jones is a longtime and outspoken activist for woman suffrage; the Fernando Jones scrapbook holds clippings on the Illinois Woman Suffrage Association from 1875. Nearly thirty years of it, and she still cannot vote. Illinois will let women vote for president in 1913, the first state east of the Mississippi to do it. The house will be razed in 1942. Hand in the flowers and go on south.

== stop buckingham
2036 Prairie, where the 1904 Blue Book lists Clarence Buckingham, philanthropist and businessman. His sister Kate Sturges Buckingham, philanthropist and art patron, will outlive him by more than twenty years. In 1927 she pays for a fountain in his name, dedicated on 26 August: twice the size of the Latona Basin at Versailles that inspired it, its major display throwing water 150 feet up. One bunch left.

== stop armour
2115 Prairie, the Kelley-Armour house, the Armour family's home for nearly fifty years. The Blue Book lists Mrs. Philip D. Armour; the census of 1900 has her as Belle O. Armour, born in Ohio, wife of the household. After the 1890s the leading families died off or left for suburbs like Lake Forest. She did not. The district marker counts her among the last residents of the street, dying here in 1927. The house is torn down in 1937. Basket empty; back to the shop.

== stop frank
1608 Prairie, the Henry L. Frank household, at the north end of the round. Henrietta Greenebaum Frank has worked steadily for proper nursing education at the Illinois Training School for Nurses. In 1876 she and her sister, Hannah Greenebaum Solomon, became the first Jewish members of the Chicago Woman's Club; Hannah went on to found the National Council of Jewish Women and be its first president. Basket empty; back to the shop.

== ending north-then-south
North, then all the way south: four houses, four doors, not a bunch dropped. The florist says you have the avenue by heart. Next week you plan the round.

== ending north-and-back
North, south, then all the way back north: the long way, and the last bunch wilting by the time it reached the door. Every order delivered. Plan the round next time.

== ending south-and-on
South and on south in a straight line: the quickest round there is. Every order delivered, and you are back at the shop before the florist has missed you.

== ending south-and-back
South first and then the long walk back to 1608, with your arms aching. Every order delivered. Tomorrow you sort the basket by house number before you set out.
