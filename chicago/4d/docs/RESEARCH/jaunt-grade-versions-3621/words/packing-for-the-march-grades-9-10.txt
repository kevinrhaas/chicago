== premise
Play a young person of the settlement around Fort Dearborn during the last two days before the garrison's evacuation, 13 and 14 August 1812: choose your errands, and pack for the march.

== opening
JAUNT: PACKING FOR THE MARCH. August 1812, and you are twelve years old. Your household occupies a log house outside the pickets of Fort Dearborn, on the south bank of the Chicago River. The United States has declared war on Britain, and the general commanding at Detroit has ordered the garrison to abandon the post. In two days the soldiers march out, and your household marches with them.

The places and the documents are genuine; the young person is you. You will not converse with the captain or the chief, and nobody will explain everything to you. Observe, carry, and decide which errands come first.

== stop the-order
Thursday, 13 August 1812, morning, on the north bank. Across the river stands the fort, and on this side 'the dwelling-house and trading-establishment of Mr. Kinzie', who with his partner holds the exclusive right to sell to the garrison. On the 9th, according to Heald's journal, the general's order arrived: evacuate the post. 'The order for evacuating the post was read next morning upon parade', and the officers' unanimous advice was to remain and fortify. Captain Heald intends to obey. At nine on the 15th the garrison marches for Detroit, accompanied by the women and children and the twelve men of the town's militia. Your household must decide what is carried, what is distributed, and what is abandoned. Your mother has two errands for you across the river.

== narrative the-order
Which errand takes priority: the government factory, or the garrison's gate?

== label the-order to-factory
To the factory, where the goods are being distributed

== consequence the-order to-factory
You will witness exactly what the departing garrison surrenders.

== label the-order to-gate
To the garrison's gate, to gather information

== consequence the-order to-gate
You will learn who is arriving before anyone announces it.

== stop factory
Afternoon at the factory, the government's trading house, one of two log houses a short distance south-west of the fort. Everything on its shelves is being distributed: 'blankets, broadcloths, calicoes, paints'. Heald's official report says he delivered 'all the goods in the factory store, and a considerable quantity of provisions which we could not take away with us', though Wau-Bun dates the distribution a day earlier than his report does. General Hull's order permitted it, naming 'the Friendly Indians' who might escort the garrison to Fort Wayne. It also named 'the Poor & needy of your Post.' Your household qualifies as needy. Beside the factory stands the agent's house, its piazzas 'planked up, for greater security', with port-holes cut. You carry home a bolt of calico, and nothing more.

== narrative factory
Whatever the departing garrison cannot transport, it distributes deliberately.

== stop the-gate
Afternoon at the main gateway, in the middle of the south side, flanked by the soldiers' barracks. The fort is a quadrangle of barracks with blockhouses at its north-western and south-eastern corners, 'inclosed within a double row of palisades'. Riders arrive: Captain William Wells of Fort Wayne, who according to Heald's report came 'with about 30 Miamies, for the purpose of escorting us in'. Wau-Bun places his arrival a day later and insists he came 'all too late', since the ammunition had already been destroyed. Wells is the uncle of Rebekah Heald, the commander's wife. Nobody at the gate pauses to explain any of this to you. You listen, and carry the news home with the water bucket.

== narrative the-gate
Information arrives at the gate considerably before it reaches anyone's household.

== stop covered-way
Evening, on the river side of the fort's north-east corner, where a covered way descends to the water so the garrison can reach it safely under attack. Tonight soldiers are working inside it. 'The same evening the ammunition and liquor were carried, part into the sally-port, and thrown into a well'. The muskets 'were broken up and thrown into the well' as well. The remaining liquor leaves through the northern gate, 'the heads of the barrels knocked in, and the contents poured into the river.' Heald feared 'they would make bad use of it'. The operation is meant to be secret, but the knocking carries across the water, and the following morning the water tastes of 'strong grog'. Your household draws its water from this river.

== narrative covered-way
Should you draw uncontaminated water upstream tonight, or postpone it until morning?

== label covered-way draw-water
Fill the keg upstream tonight

== consequence covered-way draw-water
Your household will have uncontaminated water for the march.

== label covered-way keep-watch
Remain near the fort tomorrow

== consequence covered-way keep-watch
You will observe who arrives and departs on the final day.

== stop river-mouth
Friday, 14 August, morning, where the river enters the lake, half a mile below the fort. In 1808 Captain Whistler drew 'John Kinzie Esq. House on the opisite side River' and declared the garrison 'perfectly secure from any ambuscade or Barrier', apart from the houses across the river standing somewhat in the way. Wau-Bun says Mrs. Kinzie's boat will descend to this river mouth tomorrow while the column marches. Your keg is filled and stoppered, and today you sort everything else: a blanket apiece, the cooking pot, the calico or the news, and the possessions that must remain behind. Tomorrow the column takes the beach south, 'with the lake on our left'.

== narrative river-mouth
The final complete day, devoted entirely to deliberate packing.

== stop the-medal
Friday, 14 August, evening, on the parade inside the fort, near the commanding officer's quarters on its east side. Black Partridge, 'a Pottawatomie chief from the Illinois River', has arrived. According to Wau-Bun he 'entered the quarters of the commanding officer' and surrendered his peace medal: 'I will not wear a token of peace while I am compelled to act as an enemy.' Lieutenant Helm and Sergeant Griffith both recorded his warning, though only Helm mentions the medal. You witness nothing more than a door closing across the parade, and you hear about the medal afterwards, as everyone does. Tomorrow at nine the gate opens.

== narrative the-medal
An unmistakable warning, delivered on the garrison's final evening.

== leg 0
Across the river by canoe first, then southward along the entire length of the pickets.

== leg 1
Around the stockade to the gateway where the information arrives.

== ending ready-to-walk
Night on the 14th. The bundle is tied, with the calico folded beneath the blankets and the keg stoppered beside the cooking pot, because you completed both errands and kept your household's water uncontaminated. Tomorrow at nine the gate opens and the column marches south along the beach. What happened there that morning is told in the jaunt The Battle of Fort Dearborn.

== ending the-warning
Night on the 14th. You brought home the news from the gate, and tonight you bring home the account of the medal, which everyone in the settlement is repeating. Nobody can unpack now: tomorrow at nine the gate opens and the column marches south along the beach. What happened there that morning is told in the jaunt The Battle of Fort Dearborn.

== ending the-gift
Night on the 14th. The calico from the factory lies folded on top of the bundle, and you observed the final evening at the fort. You understand now how much was surrendered, and why. Tomorrow at nine the gate opens. What happened on the beach that morning is told in the jaunt The Battle of Fort Dearborn.

== ending packed
Night on the 14th. The keg is filled, the bundle is tied, and you know exactly who rode in at the gate, and which way the column will walk in the morning. At nine the gate opens and the column marches south. What happened on the beach that morning is told in the jaunt The Battle of Fort Dearborn.
