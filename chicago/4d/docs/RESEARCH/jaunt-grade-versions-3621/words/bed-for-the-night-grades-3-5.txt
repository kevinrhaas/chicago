== premise
Look at four places in town that rent beds to strangers. Choose where to ask first if you do not have much money.

== opening
JAUNT: A BED FOR THE NIGHT. The sun is going down on the busiest summer Chicago has ever seen. You have nowhere to sleep. The land office opened in May. People who want to buy land have filled the streets. Some camp out on the open grass around town. The paper counted hundreds of strangers, on top of the two or three thousand people who live here.

Four houses that take in strangers stand between the wagon yards on the west side and Lake Street. See what each one is like. Decide if cost or convenience comes first. Then choose where to knock. Be warned. In this town, full sometimes means three people in a bed, and more sleeping on the floor.

== stop west-side
Start at the Western Hotel, at Randolph and Canal Streets. W. H. Stow built it with a big front, a long side wing and a wagon yard. Farmers and wagon drivers coming in from the west stop here. Back in 1831 the county set the prices an inn could charge. A bed for a man cost about twelve cents. Supper cost twenty-five cents. Keeping a horse all night cost fifty cents. In a busy summer like this one, good luck finding a bed at those prices.

== consequence west-side cost

== consequence west-side convenience

== stop the-sauganash
Cross the river to Lake and Market Streets and the Sauganash. It was fixed up new in June. A week later the paper said strangers were crowding every room in town. Some were sleeping on the floor. The owner, Mark Beaubien, had his own trick for a full house. He would give a man a blanket and let him sleep on the floor. Then, once the man was fast asleep, Mark would take the blanket back for the next guest.

== narrative the-sauganash
A fixed-up inn, and an owner who counts his blankets.

== stop the-boarding-house
Walk east to LaSalle Street, behind Peck's store. Here is Mrs. Rufus Brown's log house. A history book calls it one of the best boarding houses. A boarding house rents you a bed by the week and feeds you at one big table. It is packed. Full sometimes means three in a bed, and more on the floor. In June the paper said strangers filled the inns and the streets. Some store houses were opened up for families who had no roof at all.

== narrative the-boarding-house
A bed and a table by the week. This is the plainer choice.

== stop lake-street
Walk on along Lake Street to Dearborn Street. Here is the Mansion House, a log inn from 1831. Two years later it got a new wooden front facing the street. One man ran it until 1835. Then another man took over. Nobody wrote down which month, so either man might be in charge tonight. Across the street stands the first Tremont House. Time to choose.

== narrative lake-street

== ending thrift
Cost comes first, so you knock first at Mrs. Brown's. If there is a corner of a bed, it is yours. You may have company in it. In June 1835 every house in town was full.

== ending close-at-hand
Convenience comes first, so you ask first on Lake Street. If the house is full, there is always the floor. In June 1835 lots of strangers slept on it.

== ending second-thoughts
You set out wanting one thing and chose another. A full town makes that easy. Sleep with one hand on your blanket. Mark Beaubien is not the only owner who counts them.
