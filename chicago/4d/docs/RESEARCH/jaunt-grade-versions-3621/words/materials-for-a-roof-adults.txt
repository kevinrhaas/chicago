== opening
JAUNT: MATERIALS FOR A ROOF. You are putting up a small frame house, and you are doing it the new Chicago way: fast, cheap and on borrowed time. The old frame of heavy sills, posts and beams 'was found too slow', so the town builds with 'the balloon frame, originated by George W. Snow': timbers 'a foot square' gave place to ones 'two by four inches', nailed fast. Wise heads made 'the prediction that they would be blown away by the first high wind'. Instead they 'were found sufficiently strong to weather the gale and the storm', and a town of thin walls went up in a summer.

The roof is next. See what comes in by lake, choose the ironwork at Peck's corner, look in at the first brickyard, and end at the Lake House, the grandest building in town, still going up for guests who could never live in a house like yours.

== stop the-wharf
Start at Newberry & Dole's warehouse on the river. In the Democrat of 1 July 1835 they advertise storage, forwarding and commission, agents for the Merchants' Line. A balloon frame needs nails by the keg, and nails, glass and iron do not grow on the prairie: what the town cannot make comes in by lake and lands at houses like this one, and every keg is dearer for the miles it travelled and the hands it passed through. Your house begins as someone else's freight bill.

== stop the-ironwork
Along South Water Street to Peck's corner at LaSalle. In September 1834 he advertised nails, nail rods, bar iron and English blister and cast steel, his stock 'just received from New York', for cash or country produce. A roof wants nails, and a balloon frame wants a great many more: the whole method trades a carpenter's skill for a merchant's iron. Ready nails cost money now; nail rods cost a smith's time later. Choose how to buy them.

== stop the-brickyard
Over the river to the North Side, where Andreas puts Tyler K. Blodgett's brickyard of spring 1833 near the bank, with Henry S. Lampman, 'undoubtedly the first brick-maker'. The first brick building outside the fort went up that same year, for John Noble, on the lot next to where the Lake House is rising. In a town of 165 new frames, one brick house was news: brick is what a man builds when he means to stay, and wood is what he builds when he means to sell. One shed stands for the yard.

== stop the-lake-house
East along the bank to Rush Street and the Lake House. Andreas describes the finished hotel: brick, three storeys and a basement, opened in autumn 1836, and it 'aimed to cater for only the highest class of guests'. Before it was even finished, the first Unitarian service in Chicago was held inside, with the English writer Harriet Martineau in the congregation: a fashionable creed in an unfinished shell, for the town's better sort. Look from the street at the walls going up, laid by men who will never sleep inside them.

== ending nails-and-brick
Nails to drive and brick for a chimney: the list is short and complete, and the purse much shorter. The Lake House will want a great deal more of both, and can pay for it.

== ending rods-and-brick
Nail rods for a smith and brick for a chimney: the list is done, and some of the work is still to be hammered out by hand, at night, after the day's paid work.

== ending a-plain-roof
Ironwork for the shingles and no brick this season: a plain roof first, and the chimney another year. It will stand the first high wind. A fire in a wooden town is another matter.
