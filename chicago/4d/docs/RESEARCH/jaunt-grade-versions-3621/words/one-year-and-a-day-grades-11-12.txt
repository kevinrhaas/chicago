== premise
Marshall Field Jr. possesses a wife, three children, a lavish new wing and the prospect of inheriting the largest fortune in Chicago. On this November afternoon, he has precisely one year and one day remaining.

== opening
JAUNT: ONE YEAR AND A DAY. A Monday afternoon, 21 November 1904, at the Field residences on Prairie Avenue.

Four stops between two houses: the patriarch's and the heir's. Marshall Field constructed one of the preeminent department stores of America and accumulated the largest fortune in Chicago. His only surviving son lives two doors away: thirty-six, affluent, and still anticipating, as he has throughout his existence, the moment he will become his father's equal. Count the days alongside him. Exactly one year and one day from now, a gunshot will reverberate through the residence at 1919.

Begin at the heir's entrance.

== stop son
1919 Prairie, the residence of Mr. and Mrs. Marshall Field Jr. He acquired the property in 1890, and in 1902 D. H. Burnham and Company constructed "a huge addition" on his behalf. He married Albertine Huck, and they have three children: Henry, Marshall III and the youngest, Gwendolyn. Observe them on the front walk this afternoon, the nurse carrying the infant, the boys bundled in overcoats. Every conceivable advantage is assembled on this walk, excepting his own identity, which remains the property of his father. He has been designated Junior his entire life, and the diminutive has never relaxed its grip. Walk north two doors to the residence where the name genuinely resides.

== caption son
Marshall Field Jr. and his wife step onto the walk where the nurse waits with their children.

== stop father
1905 Prairie, the patriarch's mansion, where the heir was raised, "the first in Chicago to be completely wired for electricity". An elder son, Louis, died in infancy; this one survived and was cultivated to inherit the enterprise that instructed America to "Give the lady what she wants". His father is "the wealthiest and most powerful businessman in Chicago", and he relinquishes authority to nobody. Indeed, he intends to transmit it beyond his son altogether: his will is structured to hold the fortune in trust for forty years for the grandsons now playing on the walk at 1919. The heir ascends to his father's entrance for his customary Monday visit, and Benson the butler admits him. The mansion will be demolished in 1955.

== caption father
Marshall Field Jr. approaches his father's entrance, and Thomas Benson opens the door for him.

== stop talk
Farther along the avenue, turn and contemplate both residences. This is what society observes: a dutiful son discharging his obligations. And this is what society murmurs behind its curtains. The Levee district lies three blocks west, where the Everleigh Club on Dearborn Street, the city's most notorious brothel, numbers the wealthiest young men of Chicago among its clientele, and the gossips include Marshall Field Jr. Nobody has substantiated it, and nobody ever will. Nevertheless, a year and a day hence, rumor will crystallize into legend: "The most popular story is that he was in fact shot at the Everleigh Club", then transported home and staged as an accident. Others will maintain it was suicide. Return to 1919.

== caption talk
A gentleman and a lady promenade past the Field residences, and the gentleman inclines his head meaningfully toward 1919.

== stop shot
1919 once more, as the afternoon wanes. The children have retired indoors. On Wednesday, 22 November 1905, precisely one year and a day from this moment, Marshall Field Jr. will be shot within this house, surviving five days. At Mercy Hospital he will insist, "I shot myself with that revolver – accidentally", and the coroner's jury will determine that he "had shot himself unintentionally while examining a revolver". Two reporters will receive anonymous tips that he spent that evening at the Everleigh Club; the proprietors will deny it, and no corroboration will ever emerge. Contemplate the illuminated windows. The son of the wealthiest man in Chicago is home for dinner, with a single year and a day remaining.

== ending six-weeks
The walk concludes here. Marshall Field Jr. died at Mercy Hospital on Monday, 27 November 1905. His father, seventy-one and recently remarried, played golf on New Year's Day, contracted pneumonia, and "six weeks later, Marshall Field, Sr. was dead". The fortune, valued at approximately $125 million, passed in trust to the boys from the front walk at 1919, precisely as the will had prescribed: sixty percent to Marshall III and forty percent to Henry. The heir never transcended the designation of Junior; his sons inherited everything, which suggests how thoroughly a dynasty's ambitions could eclipse the individual life lived beneath them.
