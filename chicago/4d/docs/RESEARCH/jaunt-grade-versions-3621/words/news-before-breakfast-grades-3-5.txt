== premise
Read both of the town's newspapers before breakfast. Learn to tell a news story from an ad. Keep one useful clipping.

== opening
JAUNT: NEWS BEFORE BREAKFAST. It is sunrise on 1 July 1835. Chicago has two newspapers this summer. They each come out once a week, and one of them is only three weeks old. A dangerous sickness called cholera has been in the state. Everyone wants to know how close it is. But the papers also want to sell you things. Learning to tell news from ads is the most useful thing a newcomer can do.

Choose your question. Ride from the Sauganash to both newspaper offices. Decide which parts tell the news and which parts sell things. Then carry one clipping to breakfast at the Tremont House.

== stop question
Start at the Sauganash. Juliette Kinzie remembered it as a white two-story house with bright blue shutters. In June the paper said it was fixed up and neatly furnished. Both papers are printed a short ride east along the river. Before the town had a paper, news came with the mail. The mail was often late. Somebody would stand on a box and read it out loud. Now you can read it yourself.

== narrative question
Choose the question you want the papers to answer.

== label question cholera
Is the cholera near?

== consequence question cholera
You will read to find news about the sickness.

== label question today
What is new in town today?

== consequence question today
You will read to find anything happening today.

== stop democrat
John Calhoun printed the Chicago Democrat at this corner starting in 1833. Now it is printed over a hardware store on South Water Street. The paper from 24 June gathers news about the sickness from other towns. It says it could not check one rumor. And it says Chicago is very healthy.

== narrative democrat
Is this news, or an ad?

== label democrat report
Call it reporting

== consequence democrat report
You put it with the news.

== label democrat advert
Call it an advertisement

== consequence democrat advert
You put it with the ads.

== stop american
Near the drawbridge is the other paper, the Chicago American. It is only three weeks old. Its paper from 27 June has a notice for a medicine called Cholera Elixir. A druggist named Frederick Thomas sells it. The notice says it will surely stop the sickness and cure it. The notice is dated 24 June. That is the same day the Democrat printed its news.

== narrative american
Is this news, or an ad?

== label american report
Call it reporting

== consequence american report
You put it with the news.

== label american advert
Call it an advertisement

== consequence american advert
You put it with the ads.

== stop tremont
Have breakfast at the Tremont House, a wooden hotel from 1833 at Lake and Dearborn Streets. Ira Couch has run it since 1834. Later, in 1850, a new brick Tremont House was built. People thought it was so huge and silly that they called it Couch's Folly. The Democrat says a new auction house is opening on South Water Street. Its first sale is on the 1st of July. That is today!

== leg 0
Ride east with your question in mind.

== ending cholera-answered
You asked if the sickness was near. You bring back a news report, not a promise. It has news from other towns, a rumor that could not be checked, and the editor saying Chicago is healthy.

== ending sale-today
You asked what was new today. You bring back a sale. A new auction room has its first sale on South Water Street, this very morning. The notice does not say what will be sold.

== ending one-fooled-you
One of the two notices fooled you. That is worth knowing too. The Democrat said which papers its news came from. The medicine ad was signed and sold by the druggist. Read them again over breakfast.

== ending cure-kept
You kept the ad. It promises a sure cure, but a promise is all it is. The seller wrote it, not a news editor. That is a useful thing to know.

== ending a-useful-clipping
You compared two papers before breakfast, and you have one clipping in your pocket. You learned this morning's lesson. A news report says where its news came from. An ad says who is selling.

== keepsake text
A made-up clipping from a morning's reading of Chicago's two weekly newspapers, kept for what it taught you.
