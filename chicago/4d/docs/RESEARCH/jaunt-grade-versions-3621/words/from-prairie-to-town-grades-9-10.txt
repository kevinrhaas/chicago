== premise
Come in off the open shore south of the fort: begin at the factor's house below Fort Dearborn, pass its stockade, locate a store on South Water Street and finish at a public house's door.

== opening
JAUNT: FROM PRAIRIE TO TOWN. Sunset, and you have traveled up the open shore from the south, which is how many people first encountered Chicago. When Philo Carpenter landed on this shore in July 1832, Joe Ellis carried him to the fort "with an ox team", and inland he saw only "the flat prairie, covered with sedgy grass".

Three years later the prairie is filling rapidly: since the land office opened, buyers have "filled the streets and overflowed improvised camps on the adjacent prairies". Ride north past the fort, find a store on the river street, and reach a public house before dark.

== stop factors-house
Begin at the U.S. Factor's House below the stockade, the government trading house. Hubbard, who knew it from 1818, recalled a two-story house of squared logs, and Harrison's 1830 plan labels it here. Beyond the Reservation the shore lies open: Wright's 1834 map names that ground Fractional Section 15, and nobody had yet purchased it. This is the same shore where Philo Carpenter came ashore in 1832.

== narrative factors-house
Establish your bearings: the town lies ahead, and the prairie behind.

== stop the-stockade
North to Fort Dearborn's stockade at the river mouth. Juliette Kinzie, who lived inside it in 1831, described high pickets and large gates to the north and south. Troops have occupied the post since June 1832, so today it functions as a working garrison. Observe it from outside, since the gates are shown shut.

== narrative the-stockade
The earliest works of the town.

== stop the-store
West along the river to South Water and LaSalle and P. F. W. Peck's store, the village's first two-story frame, its lumber "brought from Detroit by a boat". His advertisement in the Democrat offered dry goods, hardware, groceries, crockery, boots and shoes, and hats. In May 1833 a newly arrived minister lodged in its unfinished loft.

== narrative the-store
Somewhere to replace whatever the journey consumed.

== stop the-public-house
Finally, continue to Lake and Market and the Sauganash, on the east bank of the South Branch. Wau-Bun recalled it as white and two stories tall, with bright-blue shutters, and on 10 June 1835 the Democrat reported it repaired and newly fitted up. Moses and Kirkland call it "the most attractive hostlery the city possessed". Whether a bed is available tonight is anybody's guess.

== narrative the-public-house
A public house at the end of the ride, and a fiddle somewhere inside.

From the last house to the earliest works of the town.

Into the bustling trading street.

== leg 2
The final few blocks to a welcoming door.

== ending in-town
You reach the Sauganash's door having entered from the south: from the last house below the fort, along the river street to a store, and onward to a public house. Three years ago this was sedgy grass and an ox team, but tonight it is unmistakably a town.
