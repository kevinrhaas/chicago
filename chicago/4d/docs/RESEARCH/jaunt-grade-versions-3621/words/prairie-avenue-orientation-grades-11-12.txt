== premise
Circumnavigate Richardson's final residence in summer 1904, from the avenue it confronted to the sunlit courtyard it concealed, in ten stops on foot.

== opening
JAUNT: MEET THE GLESSNER HOUSE. A summer morning, 1904, at 1800 Prairie Avenue. Morning illumination from the lake falls across the granite façade. Behind it reside John J. Glessner, an agricultural-machinery executive from Ohio; his wife Frances, a dedicated diarist since 1879; and the residence that scandalized the avenue.

Ten stops on foot, completely encircling a single building: the corner, the Eighteenth Street elevation, the alley, the principal entrance, the carriage passage and the interior courtyard. The narrative derives predominantly from the owner's own account, composed for his children in 1923. Exteriors exclusively.

Commence at the corner, where the neighbors first confronted the residence and disapproved of what they observed.

== stop corner
An antiquated Chicago saying characterized Prairie Avenue as "the sunny street that holds the sifted few". Pullman's mansion occupies the diagonal corner, and Kimball's French chateau faces it across the avenue. Then there is this composition: rusticated granite, diminutive recessed windows, precipitous red roofs. It was Henry Hobson Richardson's final residential design, and he never witnessed its completion.

== stop north-wall
This is the elevation contemporaries criticized when the residence was new. Pedestrians remarked upon the narrow windows, "just enough to light the narrow corridors". Richardson maintained that the windows of an urban residence were "not to look out of", since occupants invariably smother them in shades and draperies. The architecture deliberately turns its back upon the street.

== stop arch
The great arch constitutes the service entrance, where a narrow corridor leads toward the kitchen. It also accommodates musical conspiracies. On New Year's Day 1903 a double quartet of horns infiltrated through the Eighteenth Street door and ascended the service stairs, and at four o'clock commenced performing in the upper hall.

== stop stable
The western wing contains the stable and carriage house, with an expansive carriage doorway onto Eighteenth Street and interior walls of white glazed brick. The furnace room occupies the level beneath it, ensuring that the dust, dirt and disturbance associated with coal and ashes never penetrate the residence itself.

== stop gate
Around the corner, these alley gates open into the courtyard, and they facilitated an ingenious maneuver. On 1 December 1887 the family transported everything through here, "before our neighbors suspected even that we were about to move". That evening they kindled the new hearth with live fire carried ceremonially from the previous one.

== stop door
The principal entrance: massive oak reinforced with iron straps, with a square window behind an iron grille. Richardson prohibited painting the ironwork; it was instead Bower-Barffed against corrosion, a process Glessner never encountered elsewhere. The wall appears monolithic, but it is brick veneered with granite slabs, with deep stones at every opening to perpetuate the illusion.

== stop porte
Not every visitor utilizes the principal entrance. Here a carriage could depart Prairie Avenue and proceed directly beneath the residence, through two paneled oak doors suspended on massive iron straps, and emerge into a private courtyard. Follow its route through.

== stop court-north
Inside, the residence reverses its architectural character. From the street it appeared impenetrable; here it opens generously. Richardson positioned expansive windows along this sunny courtyard elevation, admitting, Glessner wrote, "a flood of sunlight when the sun shines in Chicago". The principal family rooms orient inward toward this tranquil enclosure rather than outward toward the avenue.

== stop hall-bow
The rounded bay above the terrace steps encloses the entrance hall. Its courtyard door is glazed with yellow glass beneath chipped white glass, so the interior seems sunlit even on overcast days. These vines were transplanted from the family's previous residence; one specimen, fifty-five feet long, was replanted intact, and it flourished.

== stop family
Behind these walls, 1904 is an eventful year. Frances Glessner, a diarist since 1879, has installed a silversmith's workbench in the basement, and throughout winter and spring her Monday Morning Reading Class convenes in the library. Years earlier her son George and his companions operated a telegraph company from his schoolroom, connected to seven neighboring houses.

== leg 0
In March 1885 Glessner paid $50,000 for this corner: two complete lots plus the northern seventeen feet of a third.

== leg 1
Norcross Brothers of Worcester, Massachusetts, constructed the residence for the contracted price, without submitting a single invoice for extras.

== leg 2
Three weeks after the horns, the complete orchestra attended dinner, and the musicians performed a Beethoven movement on pots, pans and dishes.

== leg 3
Glessner remembered his architect as weighing 370 pounds: "big-minded, big-bodied, big-hearted".

== leg 4
The residence has accommodated receptions exceeding four hundred guests and dinners exceeding a hundred, all prepared in its own kitchen.

== leg 5
By special arrangement the adjacent residence employed identical pinkish brick, so every courtyard wall corresponds.

== ending finish
Richardson never witnessed any of this. Approximately three weeks before his death in April 1886, too debilitated to leave his bedroom, he indicated on the drawings where the light fixtures would be installed and informed Glessner, "There, Mr. Glessner if I were to live five years longer, that is the last thing I would do on your house, my part is finished". Excavation commenced that June, and George and Frances turned the ceremonial first soil. Glessner remained convinced it was the residence Richardson would most have preferred to inhabit himself.
