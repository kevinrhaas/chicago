== opening
JAUNT: PACKING FOR THE MARCH. August 1812, and you are twelve. Your household lives in a log house outside the pickets of Fort Dearborn, on the south bank of the Chicago River, among the few families who live off the garrison and its trade. The United States is at war with Britain. The general at Detroit has ordered the post given up, and blamed its want of provisions on the man who commands it. In two days the garrison marches out, and your household marches with it, because there is nowhere else to go.

The places and the records are real; the young person is you. You will not meet the captain or the chief, and nobody explains anything to a child. Watch, carry, and choose your errands, knowing less than the grown people around you, who know less than they would like.

== stop the-order
Thursday, 13 August 1812, morning, on the north bank, outside 'the dwelling-house and trading-establishment of Mr. Kinzie', whose firm holds the sole right to sell to the fort. On the 9th, Heald wrote, the order came to 'evacuate the Post of Chicago and proceed with my Command to Detroit.' It was read on parade, and the officers' 'unanimous advice was, to remain where they were, and fortify themselves as strongly as possible.' Captain Heald will obey; a later historian defends him with 'Obedience to orders is the primary duty of a soldier.' At nine on the 15th the garrison marches, and with it the women and children and the twelve men of the town's militia. Everything your household owns must be sorted: carried, given away, or left behind. Your mother has two errands for you across the river.

== stop factory
Afternoon at the factory, the government's trading house, a log house just south-west of the fort. The shelves are emptying: 'blankets, broadcloths, calicoes, paints'. Heald's report says he gave out 'all the goods in the factory store, and a considerable quantity of provisions which we could not take away with us.' Hull's order, written 'with regret', blamed the evacuation on 'the want of Provisions only a neglect of the Commandant'. The same order let the goods go to 'the Friendly Indians' who might escort the garrison to Fort Wayne. It also named 'the Poor & needy of your Post', and your household is poor enough to be counted. Next door the agent's house has its piazzas 'planked up, for greater security', with port-holes cut. You carry home a bolt of calico, and nothing else.

== stop the-gate
Afternoon at the main gate, in the middle of the south side. The fort is a square of two-storey barracks with blockhouses at opposite corners, 'inclosed within a double row of palisades'. Riders come in: Captain William Wells of Fort Wayne, by Heald's report 'with about 30 Miamies, for the purpose of escorting us in, by the request of General Hull.' Wau-Bun has him a day later, come to save 'his relative, Captain Heald', and 'all too late': the ammunition was already gone. Rebekah Heald, the captain's wife, is his niece. Nobody at the gate explains any of this to you, but you can read the faces of the men who hear it. You listen, and carry the news home with the water bucket.

== stop covered-way
Evening, on the river side of the fort's north-east corner, where a covered way runs down to the water. Tonight the soldiers work in it. 'The same evening the ammunition and liquor were carried, part into the sally-port, and thrown into a well'. The muskets 'were broken up and thrown into the well'. The rest of the liquor goes out of the northern gate, 'the heads of the barrels knocked in, and the contents poured into the river.' Heald gave his reason: 'fearing they would make bad use of it if put in their possession.' The fort is sinking its powder and pouring out its drink in the dark. It is meant to be secret, but the knocking carries, and next morning the water tastes of 'strong grog'. Your household's water comes from this river.

== stop river-mouth
Friday, 14 August, morning, where the river meets the lake, half a mile below the fort. In 1808 Captain Whistler drew 'John Kinzie Esq. House on the opisite side River' and called the garrison 'perfectly secure from any ambuscade or Barrier', apart from the houses across the river being 'somewhat in the way'. Wau-Bun says Mrs. Kinzie's boat will come down to this river mouth tomorrow while the column walks. Your household goes on foot. Your keg is full and stoppered, and today you sort the rest: a blanket each, the pot, the calico or the news, and what stays behind for strangers. Tomorrow the column takes the beach south, 'with the lake on our left, and a high sand bank on our right'.

== stop the-medal
Friday, 14 August, evening, on the parade inside the fort, beside the commanding officer's quarters on its east side. Black Partridge, 'a Pottawatomie chief from the Illinois River', has come in. Wau-Bun says he 'entered the quarters of the commanding officer' and gave back his peace medal: 'I will not wear a token of peace while I am compelled to act as an enemy.' Lieutenant Helm and Sergeant Griffith both recorded his warning; 'Helm alone mentions the surrender of the medal.' You see no more than a door closing across the parade. Afterwards you hear the grown people repeat his words, and hear them fail to agree on what the captain should do now. Tomorrow at nine the gate opens anyway.

== ending ready-to-walk
Night on the 14th. The bundle is tied, the calico folded under the blankets, the keg stoppered beside the pot. You did both errands and kept the household's water clean of the fort's grog. Wau-Bun says that in the morning the band played the Dead March as the troops left the fort, and that Wells had blackened his face 'in token of his impending fate.' What happened on the beach is told in the jaunt The Battle of Fort Dearborn.

== ending the-warning
Night on the 14th. You carried home the news from the gate, and tonight the story of the medal, which nobody in the settlement can stop repeating. Nobody can unpack now. Wau-Bun says that in the morning the band played the Dead March as the troops left the fort. Tomorrow at nine the column walks south along the beach. What happened there is told in the jaunt The Battle of Fort Dearborn.

== ending the-gift
Night on the 14th. The calico from the factory is folded on top of the bundle, and you watched the last evening at the fort. You know now how much was given away, on whose orders, and that the man who gave the order blamed the man who obeyed it. Tomorrow at nine the gate opens. What happened on the beach is told in the jaunt The Battle of Fort Dearborn.

== ending packed
Night on the 14th. The keg is full, the bundle is tied, and you know who rode in at the gate and which way the column walks in the morning. Nobody in the house sleeps much. Tomorrow at nine the gate opens and the column walks south. What happened on the beach is told in the jaunt The Battle of Fort Dearborn.
