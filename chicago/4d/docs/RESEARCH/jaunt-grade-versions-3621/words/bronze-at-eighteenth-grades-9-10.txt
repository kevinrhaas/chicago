== premise
George Pullman installed a bronze of the 1812 battle beside his own door; a year later the army returned to Chicago, this time against his own striking workers. A walk about history, memory, and who gets to cast it in metal.

== opening
JAUNT: THE BRONZE AT EIGHTEENTH STREET. A Monday afternoon in November 1904, at the intersection of Eighteenth Street and Prairie Avenue.

Four stops around a single street corner. Beneath the pavement lies a battlefield; beside it stands a bronze that offers one interpretation of that battle; behind the bronze rises the mansion of the industrialist who financed it; and across the intersection looms the granite house that stared back at him. Every stop poses a question about what actually happened, and about who decided how it would be commemorated.

Begin where the street begins: on the ground where the battle was fought.

== stop ground
Prairie Avenue at Eighteenth Street. Before the mansions, this was a landscape of sand hills along the lake. On 15 August 1812 the Battle of Fort Dearborn was fought across them, and the dead "lay in the sand, half buried where they were killed, until 1816". For more than a century an enormous cottonwood tree marked the site, although accounts disagree about its exact location. In 1900 a Tribune writer asserted that the Pullman house in front of this corner "stands about where the massacre took place". Now observe the street in 1904: brick, granite, gas lamps, and a schoolteacher escorting her class to see the sights. The battle is ninety-two years past, and it has been transformed into a lesson. The question is who composed that lesson. Walk east along Eighteenth Street to the bronze.

== caption ground
A teacher leads a line of schoolchildren along Eighteenth Street toward the monument.

== stop bronze
The monument. George Pullman commissioned it and installed it in 1893 near his own front door: Carl Rohl-Smith's bronze of the Potawatomi chief Black Partridge rescuing Margaret Helm, an officer's wife. It is theatrical and noble, and it may never have occurred. "Some historians regard the incident as the product of romantic imagination instead of eyewitness testimony", arguing that "Mrs. Helm rode her horse to the shore of Lake Michigan where she found refuge". The sculptor recruited his models at Fort Sheridan, among survivors of Wounded Knee, whom he described as "Indians of the most untamed sort", and one historian suggests the figures "may have owed more to Buffalo Bill Cody's Wild West Show than to actual research". The teacher is reading the plaque aloud. Now consider the mansion this bronze stands guard over.

== caption bronze
The teacher reads aloud to her class at the base of the monument while the children gaze up at the bronze.

== stop strike
1729 Prairie, the Pullman residence. The patron of the bronze manufactured railroad sleeping cars and constructed a company town south of the city, where his employees paid him rent. In 1894 he reduced their wages, but "he didn't reduce the rent in Pullman", and they went on strike. Here is the irony the bronze cannot convey: "a year after the statue's dedication", the Seventh Cavalry, the regiment of Little Big Horn and Wounded Knee, "came to Chicago to quell the Pullman riots of 1894". A regiment of the army whose wars the monument romanticizes arrived to suppress a strike against the man who had placed that battle at his doorstep. Pullman died of a heart attack in 1897, and the house will be demolished in 1922. Cross Prairie to the house that stared at his.

== caption strike
Two laborers carrying tools pause on the opposite sidewalk to study the Pullman house, then continue on their way.

== stop grave
The Glessner corner, diagonally opposite Pullman's door. Pullman resented this house: "I do not know what I have ever done to have that thing staring me in the face every time I go out of my door". He spent his final years between the bronze and the granite. After his death, amid a string of notorious grave robberies and fears that former employees still sought revenge, his family buried him in a "lead-lined mahogany coffin, which was then sealed inside a block of concrete", beneath steel rails "bolted together at right angles". The historian Adam Selzer is candid about the legends: "Whether it's true or not, we don't really know". That uncertainty is the lesson of this corner: bronze, granite and concrete, and beneath all of it, the sand.

== ending sand
The walk ends here. In 1904 the bronze still stands at Eighteenth Street, narrating its story from a single perspective, even though several historians doubt the rescue it depicts ever took place. Pullman lies at Graceland beneath his concrete, the strike he crushed appears in every history of American labor, and the ground beneath this corner still contains the sand of 1812. A monument reveals who paid for it and what they wished to be remembered. Everything else, the historian must excavate.
