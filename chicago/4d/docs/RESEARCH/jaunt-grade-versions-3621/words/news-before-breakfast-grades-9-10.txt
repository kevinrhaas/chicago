== premise
Read both of the town's newspapers before breakfast, distinguish a report from an advertisement and keep one useful clipping.

== opening
JAUNT: NEWS BEFORE BREAKFAST. Daybreak, 1 July 1835. Chicago prints two weekly newspapers this summer, one of them merely three weeks old. Cholera has been in the state, and everyone wants to know how close it is; the papers, however, also want to sell you things. Learning to distinguish the two is the most valuable skill a newcomer can acquire.

Choose your question, ride from the Sauganash to both printing offices, decide which columns report and which ones sell, and carry one clipping to breakfast at the Tremont House.

== stop question
Begin at the Sauganash, the white two-story house with bright-blue shutters that Juliette Kinzie remembered; the Democrat of 10 June reported it repaired, fitted up and furnished with neatness. Both papers are printed a short ride east along the river. Before the town had a paper, news arrived with the mail, "often irregular and long delayed", and somebody read it aloud standing on "a dry goods box". Now you can read it yourself.

== consequence question cholera

== consequence question today

== stop democrat
John Calhoun printed the Chicago Democrat at this corner from 1833, although by 20 May its office was above Jones and King's hardware on South Water Street. The 24 June number, the most recent before today, gathers cholera reports from Alton and Springfield, admits that it cannot trace an Ottawa rumor, and declares Chicago uncommonly healthy.

== narrative democrat
Is this reporting, or an advertisement?

== label democrat report

== consequence democrat report

== stop american
Near the drawbridge, T. O. Davis's Chicago American is three weeks old. Its 27 June number carries Frederick Thomas's Cholera Elixir, the Fire King's Specific, offered as a certain preventive and cure of the malignant disease, in a notice dated 24 June, the very day the Democrat printed its paragraph.

== stop tremont
Breakfast at the Tremont House, Alanson Sweet's frame building of 1833 at Lake and Dearborn, kept since 1834 by Ira Couch. Moses and Kirkland call it "the nucleus of the present Tremont House": its brick successor of 1850 was considered so "absurdly large" that people nicknamed it Couch's Folly. The Democrat's third page announces a new auction house on South Water Street, with its first public sale scheduled for the 1st of July, which is today.

== leg 0
Ride east with your question in mind.

== ending cholera-answered
You asked whether the cholera was near and carry back a report rather than a promise: news weighed from other towns, a rumor that could not be traced, and the editor's assurance that Chicago is healthy.

== ending sale-today
You asked what was new today and carry back a sale: an auction room's first, on South Water Street, this very morning. What will be sold, the notice leaves to whoever brings the goods.

== ending one-fooled-you
One of the two notices fooled you, which is worth knowing too. The Democrat identified the papers its cholera news came from, while the elixir notice was signed, dated and sold by the druggist, so read them again over breakfast.

== ending cure-kept
You kept the advertisement. It promises a certain cure, but a promise is all it offers: signed by the seller, reported by no editor. That is a useful thing to recognize, and you carry it back.

== ending a-useful-clipping
Two newspapers compared before breakfast and one clipping in your pocket, and the morning's lesson travels with it: a report identifies where its news came from, while an advertisement identifies who is selling.

== keepsake text
A fictional cutting from a morning's reading of Chicago's two weekly newspapers, kept for what it taught you.
