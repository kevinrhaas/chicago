== opening
JAUNT: FREIGHT FOR THE STORE. Daybreak at the wharf, gulls on the pilings and the river smelling of tar and fish. Everything this town eats, wears and builds with comes in by lake, and somebody has to carry it from the boat to the store for a few coins and no thanks. Today that is you: a bill of lading lists three packages landed at Newberry & Dole's, two for P. F. W. Peck's store and one for Thomas Church's on Lake Street.

Count them, load the wagon, deliver them and carry the tally to George Dole's old warehouse. A missing package is somebody's loss, and the carter is the easiest man to blame. A carter who loses one does not get hired twice.

== stop wharf
Newberry & Dole have advertised as forwarding and commission merchants since the Democrat's first number in November 1833, taking in goods off the lake for other men's stores, and selling dry goods, hardware and crockery of their own, for cash. In June the American named them freight agents for the steamboat Michigan, bound for Detroit and Buffalo. Every barrel that crosses this wharf pays them something going in and something going out. Which bank their house stood on is still argued, but this is where the town's goods begin. Three packages are waiting.

== stop store
East to Peck's corner of LaSalle and South Water. His own cards of 1833 and 1834 list dry goods, hardware, groceries, crockery, flannels and calicoes, with fifty barrels of superfine flour on hand. He built the village's first two-storey frame with lumber 'brought from Detroit by a boat', and he has been unloading boats ever since. His two packages belong among those lines.

== stop church
Up to Lake Street, where the town's business is moving: in 1834, Moses and Kirkland say, trade 'which had heretofore been confined to South Water street' began 'to extend to Lake street'. Thomas Church came that year, and a modern note credits him with the street's first store building, a two-storey frame. It names no stock and no year, and it is the weakest kind of source this project uses. The men who arrived early and bet on the right street became the town's leading citizens; the rest are names nobody wrote down.

== stop tally
Andreas puts George W. Dole's 1832 warehouse and slaughter yard near Lake and Dearborn. Dole is the Dole of Newberry & Dole, and in 1832 he began the town's slaughtering and packing trade, 'the nucleus of one of those immense industries' Chicago would be famous for: the trade that will one day make this city's name. By 1834 the firm killed on the South Branch, so this old yard, where the animals' blood once ran, is quiet. Square the bill here.

== ending second-trip
The tally squares for two packages and says so. Church's package waits at Newberry & Dole's for a second, lighter trip, and a second day's pay. A careful carter and an honest tally: you will be hired again.

== ending taken-on-trust
All three packages are delivered, but the tally only repeats the bill: nobody counted at the wharf. If a store finds one short, there is no saying where it went, and the merchant will not blame himself. Count first next time, if there is a next time.

== ending checked-and-counted
Counted off the wharf, checked against Peck's own card and left one by one: two packages at Peck's, one at Church's. The tally matches the bill to the last package, and no merchant can say you cheated him.

== ending cargo-accounted-for
Three packages counted off the wharf, two left at Peck's and one at Church's, and a tally that matches the bill. Cargo accounted for, and your name stays clean in a town that remembers who it paid.
