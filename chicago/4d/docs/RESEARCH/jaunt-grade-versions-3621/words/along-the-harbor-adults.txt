== opening
JAUNT: ALONG THE WORKING HARBOR. A summer morning, and the lake is the town's highway, its fortune and its graveyard. Three years ago Chicago had no harbour at all, only a river choked by a sand bar. Then the army cut through the bar, the piers went out into the lake, and in 1834 'the schooner Illinois, the first large vessel to enter the river, sailed into the harbor amid the rejoicings of the people'. That year 180 vessels unloaded here, and every one of them carried someone's money.

Follow the freight back the way it came: from a forwarding house on the river, under the Dearborn Street draw and past the lighthouse, out to the piers, both still pushing into the lake on federal money.

== stop the-forwarding-house
Start at Newberry & Dole's warehouse on the river. In the Democrat of 1 July 1835 they advertise storage, forwarding and commission, agents for the Merchants' Line: goods off the lake land at houses like theirs and go on to every store in town, and the forwarder takes his cut at every step. This is where the real money of a boom town sits, not in the lots men gamble on but in the margin on every barrel. When the schooner Illinois came up the new channel in July 1834, she was cheered at Newberry & Dole's wharf. Which bank the warehouse stood on is still argued; who got rich here is not.

== stop the-draw
East to Dearborn Street and the town's first drawbridge, built in 1834 by the ship-carpenter Nelson R. Norton. Moses and Kirkland make it '300 feet in length, by sixteen in width', with 'a double draw'; Andreas gives a sixty-foot opening for craft and gallows frames at either end. Vessels kept striking it, and it was patched in 1834 and again this year, with the town paying each time. This week the American's Marine Journal enters the schooners Hiram, in from Michigan with lumber, Philips, Whig and Jesse Smith; schooners like them work out to the lake through this draw. Stay on the south bank.

== stop the-light
On along the south bank to the light at the river's mouth, the first on Lake Michigan. The first tower fell in 1831 before it was finished. Mr. Jackson put up this one, forty feet high, in 1832, with a fourteen-inch reflector in the lantern on top. Moses and Kirkland add a surprise: 'Samuel S. Lasby was the first, and Mark Beaubien the last keeper of the old light house.' The town's fiddler and tavern-keeper kept the lighthouse too, and a crew beating in on a black night trusted their lives to whoever had trimmed the lamp.

== stop the-piers
Out to the south pier's root. Across the cut stands the north pier, the weather pier that keeps the sand out. The Democrat printed the federal report: 180 vessels arrived and discharged here in 1834, and Congress has voted $32,800 more toward the harbour; the town's whole bet rests on Washington's money. By the season's end the north pier will reach 1,260 feet into the lake. A vessel too deep for the bar still anchors in the offing: in May the brig Illinois lay off here while French Canadians in Mackinaw boats carried her people and cargo ashore, hard, wet work. Choose the mark for your route note.

== ending a-note-to-redraw
Your note reads: in by the end of the north pier, past the light, up under the Dearborn draw. By autumn that pier will be 1,260 feet into the lake, so your mark will want redrawing. A harbour that keeps growing is a good problem to have, as long as Congress keeps paying for it.

== ending a-note-that-keeps
Your note reads: in between the piers, past the light at the mouth, up under the Dearborn draw to the forwarding houses. Of everything on it, the light is the one mark that stays put this summer, and the one a crew in a gale will look for first.
