== premise
Sunday 8 October 1871, from mid-afternoon to dusk: the most confident commercial city in the West on its last ordinary day, from the wholesale blocks of Lake Street through State Street and the Water Tower to a barn on DeKoven Street. Nine stops.

== opening
JAUNT: THE LAST SUNDAY. Sunday 8 October 1871, early afternoon. Not a drop of rain has fallen since the end of September, after a summer of very little, and a warm wind is blowing hard out of the southwest. Last night a fire in a West Side planing mill consumed four blocks near the river, and the exhausted firemen are still pouring water on the smouldering lumber. This morning's Tribune devoted three columns to that fire, then turned, with unmistakable satisfaction, to the city's speculative ambitions.

Nine stops: the wholesale blocks of Lake Street, the Court House and the marble hotel facing it, the grain exchange, a theatre preparing for Monday night, the new commercial avenue of State Street, the Water Tower across the river, and finally, as the light fails, a modest cottage on DeKoven Street. Start on Lake Street.

== stop lake-street
Lake Street at Clark, two in the afternoon. This was once the commercial spine of Chicago: until the mid-1860s it was 'the retail center of Chicago', and the great wholesale houses still crowd the blocks between here and the river. Stone fronts with arched windows line both sides, with iron awnings sheltering the walks and telegraph wires strung overhead. Many of these buildings once stood several feet lower; beginning in 1859, George M. Pullman, afterwards celebrated for his sleeping cars, raised entire blocks of brick and stone on jack-screws to the new street grade, 'without even disturbing the operations of business inside'. Today is Sunday, and the commercial establishments are shuttered. In this morning's Tribune an observer of the property market remarked: 'Every body seems to be swelled up with big schemes.' One block south stands the grandest of those schemes, a hotel faced in white marble.

== stop sherman-house
The Sherman House, at Clark and Randolph. Francis C. Sherman, afterwards twice mayor, built his first hotel on this corner in 1836-37; the present building, opened in July 1861, rises six storeys in 'the finest Athens marble, from the quarries at Lemont', its principal entrance on Clark Street framed by a two-storey portico. Its Randolph Street frontage overlooks the Court House square. This week it accommodates a celebrated guest: the conductor Theodore Thomas and his touring orchestra, who tomorrow night inaugurate a series of ten concerts with 'his concert organization of sixty performers'. Across Randolph Street, in the square, a bronze bell weighing more than five tons hangs in the Court House tower.

== stop court-house
Court House Square, occupying the entire block bounded by Randolph, Clark, Washington and LaSalle. Its lawns, trees and fountain lend it 'the appearance of a park', and an iron fence encloses it. The building at its centre accommodates virtually every municipal and county office, 'from the jail in its basement to the mayor's quarters'. John M. Van Osdel designed it in 1853; the city has since added wings, a new clock and a bronze bell of 10,849 pounds. Look up at the cupola: a spiral staircase ascends to an observation balcony 120 feet above the street, which doubles as the fire watchman's walk. Tonight the watchman on duty will be Mathias Schaffer, surveying the city through his spy-glass, and beneath him the fire-alarm telegraph office dispatches the engines. Across LaSalle Street, the grain of the West is bought and sold.

== stop chamber
The Chamber of Commerce, on the southeast corner of Washington and LaSalle. Designed by Edward Burling, it was dedicated on 30 August 1865, when Charles Randolph, president of the Board of Trade, delivered the principal address. Inside is a great hall 'decorated with frescoes and paintings', and the transaction that matters most here is the Board's commodities exchange, through which the harvest of the prairie is bought and sold. On a Sunday the trading floor stands deserted and silent. LaSalle Street around it, a continuous row of stone fronts beneath mansard roofs, has become 'the home of Insurance Companies and commission merchants', and the banks are migrating steadily toward it. Two blocks east, on Washington Street, workmen are labouring even on the Sabbath, completing a theatre for Monday night.

== stop crosbys
Crosby's Opera House, on the north side of Washington Street between State and Dearborn. Uranus Crosby opened it in 1865 to designs by W. W. Boyington: an Italianate palace of five storeys beneath a mansard roof, with allegorical statues of Painting, Sculpture, Music and Commerce above the entrance arch. Shops occupy the ground floor, and the auditorium behind them accommodates three thousand. Throughout the summer it has been refurbished, at a reported cost of about $80,000. This morning's Tribune announced that 'the whole house, gallery and all, is nearly sold for the first night.' That first night is tomorrow, when Theodore Thomas inaugurates the season. Late this evening the gas will be illuminated inside for the first time, 'that its effects might be seen by gaslight'. One block east lies the newest commercial avenue in Chicago.

== stop state-street
State Street at Washington. Four years ago this stretch held a frame dwelling, an ale store and a furniture dealer. Then Potter Palmer, who had made his first fortune in trade, acquired 'three-quarters of a mile of property along State Street south of Lake', deliberately intending to make it the city's principal retail thoroughfare. On the northeast corner stands the six-storey emporium of Field & Leiter, its frontage almost 150 feet. Marshall Field and Levi Leiter abandoned Lake Street to establish themselves here, and their carefully trained clerks treat the fashionable clientele 'like guests rather than mere customers'. A few blocks south is Palmer's own luxurious new hotel, the Palmer House. Across the river, on the North Side, the city's oldest and wealthiest families occupy substantial residences beneath great elms.

== stop water-tower
The Water Tower, on Pine Street at Chicago Avenue, on the North Side, dedicated amid great celebration in 1867. Across the street the Pumping Station draws clean water through a tunnel extending two miles beneath the lake, well beyond the reach of the 'unspeakably polluted Chicago River'. W. W. Boyington, the architect of Crosby's, designed both buildings in 'castellated Gothic' limestone. South of here, toward the river, stand the residences of the established families, 'substantial and elegant, and shaded with grand elms'. Yet for years the Board of Police and Fire Commissioners has warned that 'the water supply was insufficient', and that the city ought not to rely upon a single set of pumping engines. The entire city relies upon precisely that, and the roof above the machinery is constructed of wood.

== stop saturday-ruins
The east end of the Van Buren Street bridge, looking across to the West Side. Last night, between ten and eleven, a fire broke out in the boiler room of Lull & Holmes's planing mill on Canal Street, in a district insurance brokers call the 'Red Flash' because of the combustibility of everything in it. By this afternoon four blocks and nearly a million dollars of property had been destroyed. 'The piles of coal and lumber burned all day on Sunday', and thousands of spectators came to look. Many of the firemen laboured eighteen hours without relief, until three o'clock this afternoon; they are 'nearly exhausted', and some of their equipment is damaged. No rain has fallen in more than a week, and the wind remains strong from the southwest. Ten blocks south, on DeKoven Street, a woman has finished the evening milking.

== stop dekoven
137 DeKoven Street, on the West Side, at dusk. This is a working-class neighbourhood of small wooden houses, with 'wood shavings piled in every barn and under every house'. Patrick and Catherine O'Leary and their five children occupy the rear of this double cottage and rent the front to the McLaughlin family. Behind it stands a wooden barn, sixteen by twenty feet, its loft recently filled with hay against the winter. Inside, accommodated together, are five cows, a calf and a horse. Catherine O'Leary supplies milk to the neighbouring families; she milked at half past four, and at about seven she will feed the horse beside the fence and shut him in. Tonight the McLaughlins are holding a party for a cousin newly arrived from Ireland. Mr. McLaughlin is a fiddler, and there will be music. The wind, unrelenting throughout the afternoon, has not dropped.

== ending the-barn
That night, at about nine, Daniel Sullivan, a drayman sitting on the sidewalk across DeKoven Street, saw fire in the O'Leary barn. Within thirty minutes it 'was beyond the control of the Fire Department.' In the Court House cupola Mathias Schaffer observed the glow, misjudged its distance, and signalled the wrong alarm box; when he recognised his error, no corrected alarm was transmitted. By Monday morning the Court House bell had crashed through the burning building, the Sherman House was 'one of the most complete wrecks of the night', and Crosby's Opera House never reopened. At about three in the morning the pumping works became 'an utter wreck'. Theodore Thomas 'was compelled to run for his life'.

According to legend, Mrs. O'Leary's cow kicked over a lamp. Even A. T. Andreas, the city's most thorough nineteenth-century historian, conceded: 'There is no proof that Mrs. O'Leary is responsible for the fire.' The Great Chicago Fire jaunt begins in that barn.
