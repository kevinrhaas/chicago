== premise
Sunday night, 8 October 1871: fire in the O'Leary barn, and an Irish milkwoman's cow takes the blame. Weigh the sworn testimony, the official inquiry, a boastful reporter and the later investigators, and reach your own verdict. Eight stops.

== opening
JAUNT: WHO STARTED THE FIRE? Sunday night, 8 October 1871, DeKoven Street on the West Side. Everyone knows the story: Mrs. O'Leary's cow kicked over a lamp and burned Chicago down. Yet the official inquiry discovered no proof of it, a newspaper reporter subsequently claimed that he and his companions had fabricated it, and in 1997 the City Council formally exonerated the cow.

So who was responsible? Eight stops: the barn, the yard, the neighbours' celebration, the watchman in the Court House tower, the official investigation, the legend, the alternative suspects and the municipal verdict of 1997. Every witness is heard in their own words, and every account is weighed against the documentary record. Begin across the street from the barn, at the moment the fire is discovered.

== stop the-barn
DeKoven Street, approximately half past nine on Sunday night, and flames are pouring from the hayloft of the barn behind No. 137, the cottage of Patrick and Catherine O'Leary. Daniel Sullivan, a drayman from across the street, subsequently nicknamed 'Peg Leg', has just crossed over, cried 'Fire, fire,' and entered the barn, where he discovered 'the hay in the loft on fire'. He attempted, desperately and unsuccessfully, to cut the animals loose, and rescued only 'a half-burned calf'. While the city was still burning, someone circulated the assertion that Mrs. O'Leary had been milking when her cow 'kicked over a lighted lamp', and that explanation travelled around the world, proving considerably more durable than any evidence ever offered for it. Was it true? Begin with the woman who owned the cow.

== stop catherine
In the yard behind the cottage, Catherine O'Leary, who milks five cows and sells the milk to neighbouring families, is watching her entire livelihood disappear in flames. Testifying afterwards, she insisted: 'I was in bed myself and my husband and five children when this fire commenced.' Two men came to the door, and her husband Patrick ran back to the bedroom and said 'Kate the barn is afire.' A neighbour, Dennis Rogan, corroborated her account, recalling that at half past eight he had found the couple already in bed, and that she attributed their early retirement to a 'sore foot'. In a sworn statement one week later, the O'Learys declared that she milked at 4:30 and fed the horse at about seven, and that 'She had no lighted lamp in the house or barn that evening.' In the front of the cottage, however, there was a celebration.

== stop the-party
At the front of No. 137, on DeKoven Street, the O'Learys rent two rooms to the McLaughlin family, who tonight are entertaining young people celebrating 'the arrival of a cousin from Ireland'. Mr. McLaughlin plays the fiddle, and Catherine O'Leary, lying in bed on the other side of the partition, heard the festivities: 'There was a little music there.' Afterwards a neighbour across the street, Mrs. White, informed her that a guest had allegedly slipped out to the barn to milk her cows, and when the commissioners asked why, she replied: 'Some said it was for oysters.' Catharine McLaughlin swore that no member of her family approached the barn, and that everyone was indoors when the fire broke out; according to Andreas, the company drank beer, 'but had no whisky or milk punch'. More than a mile away, in the Court House tower, the watchman has noticed a glow.

== stop the-alarm
In the Court House cupola, at 9:28 by his own account, the watchman Mathias Schaffer observes the light of a fire to the southwest. Through his spy-glass he misjudges its location as near Canalport Avenue and Halsted Street, and signals the operator below, William J. Brown, to strike Box 342, which was 'a point in the direction of the fire, but a mile beyond it.' Schaffer soon recognises that the fire is considerably closer, yet no corrected alarm is transmitted, and engines that would have answered it remain in their houses. The historian A. T. Andreas characterised the error as 'a fatal mistake'. Something else is conspicuous: nobody in the neighbourhood of the barn activated an alarm box at all, as the inquiry itself observed: 'If any person set the fire, either by accident or design, he was careful not to give the alarm.'

== stop the-inquiry
Back on DeKoven Street, weeks later: on 23 November 1871 the Board of Police and Fire Commissioners convened an inquiry into the origin of the fire, and approximately fifty witnesses testified over several days. Catherine O'Leary, the principal witness, was preoccupied with the magnitude of her losses, her cows, her horse and her wagon, for which she 'Never had five cents insurance'. The Board concluded that the fire originated in the O'Leary barn, and could proceed no further: 'There is no proof that any person had been in the barn after nightfall that evening.' Accidental spark from a chimney, or deliberate human agency? 'we are unable to determine.' Contemporaries criticised the report, not for exonerating her, but for concealing the Fire Department's incompetence; Andreas dismissed it as 'little more than an attempt to quiet' the complaints. Officially, Mrs. O'Leary was cleared. Popularly, the story persisted.

== stop the-cow
The alley behind the barn. While the city was still burning, 'some now-forgotten tongue' asserted that Mrs. O'Leary had been milking when the cow kicked over a lamp, and contemporary cartoonists caricatured her as a comic, hapless figure; later painters christened the cow Daisy. The Chicago History Museum explains the story's longevity: she could be made to represent 'careless building, sloppy conduct, and a shiftless immigrant underclass', and blaming her exploited 'anti-Catholic, anti-immigrant, and possibly anti-female' prejudice. Subsequently Michael Ahern, who had reported on the fire for the Chicago Republican, boasted in the Tribune that he and two companions had invented the entire story; the museum dates his confession to the fortieth anniversary, while other accounts specify 1893. A colleague afterwards claimed to have ghost-written Ahern's article. If not the cow, then who?

== stop other-suspects
Across the street, a few lots east, where Daniel Sullivan testified that he was sitting. In 1997 a lawyer and amateur historian, Richard Bales, systematically compared Sullivan's testimony with the property records, and argued that from here another house would have obstructed the view, and that a man with a wooden leg could not have covered the 193 feet to the barn as rapidly as Sullivan claimed. Bales concluded that Sullivan started the fire himself, perhaps while feeding his own cow, and lied to conceal it. A Tribune writer, Anthony DeBartolo, nominated instead Louis M. Cohn, whose posthumous bequest to Northwestern University in 1944 arrived accompanied by an extraordinary story: he, Mrs. O'Leary's son and several other boys were 'shooting dice in the hayloft' when one overturned a lantern. Some even blame a meteor, a theory scientists reject. The historian Carl Smith considers them all 'interesting, but ultimately unprovable theory'.

== stop the-council
The corner of Jefferson and DeKoven, today. By the 1880s the O'Leary cottage had disappeared, and since 1961 the Chicago Fire Academy, where the city trains its firefighters, has occupied the site, with a sculpture of a flame beside its entrance. In 1971, the centennial of the fire, the Chicago Association of Commerce and Industry exonerated Mrs. O'Leary and redirected suspicion toward Sullivan. Here, in October 1997, a City Council committee conducted a hearing, half serious and half ceremonial; Aldermen William Beavers and Edward Burke determined that she and her cow 'had been unfairly maligned', and the full council subsequently passed a resolution absolving them of 'all blame'. Her great-great-granddaughter, Nancy Knight Connolly, responded: 'We always knew that she was innocent'. The municipality had delivered its verdict. The evidence, however, remained precisely what it had been in 1871.

== ending your-verdict
So who started the fire? Here is the case as it stands, account by account.

Catherine O'Leary swore that she was in bed, a neighbour testified that he found her there at half past eight complaining of a sore foot, the official inquiry accepted her testimony, and in 1997 the City Council formally exonerated both her and her cow.

A guest from the McLaughlin celebration may conceivably have slipped out for milk, as Mrs. White had heard, although Catharine McLaughlin swore categorically that nobody did.

Daniel Sullivan was the first person into the barn; Richard Bales argues that his testimony is irreconcilable with the property records, while Sullivan himself swore that he observed the fire from across the street and ran in to rescue the animals.

Louis Cohn's story of a dice game emerged only after his death, and his own will never mentions it.

An accidental spark from a chimney, on 'that windy night', remains entirely possible.

The Board could not decide, and neither could Andreas. 'It is highly unlikely that we will ever have definitive knowledge of the cause of the fire.' Whom do you consider responsible, and what additional evidence would be necessary to establish it with certainty?
