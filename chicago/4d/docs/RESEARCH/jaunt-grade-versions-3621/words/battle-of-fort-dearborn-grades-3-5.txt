== premise
From 6 April to 16 August 1812, in seventeen stops: the fort, the killings at a farm up the river, the order to leave, the march down the beach, the surrender, and the houses where the Kinzies and Mrs. Helm hid.

== opening
JAUNT: THE BATTLE OF FORT DEARBORN. It is spring 1812, where the Chicago River meets Lake Michigan. The closest American fort is Fort Wayne, far to the south-east. Detroit, where the general is in charge, is even farther. A war with Britain is coming. Every bit of news that reaches the fort makes it feel smaller and farther from help.

There are seventeen stops, in order by date. Each one has its own time of day and season.

The story begins with the fort, on the afternoon of 6 April 1812.

== stop the-fort
It is 6 April 1812, in the afternoon, in front of the fort. This story begins here. In the summer of 1803, Captain John Whistler's soldiers came from Detroit. They built the fort out of logs. It has soldiers' houses around a small open square, two gun towers, and two rows of log walls. It has stood for nine years. Since 1810, Captain Nathan Heald has been in charge, with fifty-four soldiers. Across the river lives John Kinzie, the trader who sells to the fort. His stepdaughter, Margaret, is married to the fort's lieutenant, Linai Helm. A few other families live along the river too. Trouble is about to start, three miles up the river, just before sunset.

== stop hardscrabble
It is 6 April 1812, just before sunset. Three miles up the river is a farm called Lee's Place. Later it was called Hardscrabble. Charles Lee owns it, but he lives near the fort. The farm grows food for the soldiers. In the log house are Liberty White, who runs the farm, a man who used to be a soldier, a farm worker from Canada, and Lee's young son. Eleven men with guns walk in and sit down. The former soldier feels that something is wrong. He points to the cows by the haystacks across the river. He makes signs that he and the boy must go and feed them. The two walk down to the canoes and paddle across.

== stop the-haystacks
It is 6 April, at sunset, across the river from Lee's Place. Behind the haystacks, out of sight, the man and the boy do not stop to feed the cows. They run into the woods and head for the fort, three miles away. Behind them they hear two shots. In the log house, White and the farm worker are killed. The two keep running down the river as it gets dark. Down the river, a family with a brand new baby does not know what is coming.

== stop burns
It is 6 April, at dusk. The man and the boy run toward the fort. They stop across from the Burns family's house and shout a warning. At the fort, a young officer named George Ronan takes a few soldiers up the river in a flat boat. They carry Mrs. Burns out on her bed, with her baby, who is only a day old. Some soldiers are still up the river fishing, so the captain fires a cannon to call them home. They row back to the fort in the dark and reach the gate late at night. Captain Heald asks fifteen men from town to help guard the fort. For four months, everyone waits and worries. When bad news finally comes, it comes as a letter.

== stop the-order
It is 10 August 1812, in the morning, on the open square inside the fort. The country has been at war with Britain since June. The British have taken an American fort up the lake. General William Hull, in Detroit, sends a runner named Winnemac with a letter for Captain Heald. It says Heald must leave the fort, because there is not enough food. The guns are to be destroyed. The goods in the government store are to be given away. The officers ask Heald to stay and make the fort stronger. But Heald chooses to obey. Outside the fort walls, hundreds of people have already heard the news. They gather to see what Heald will promise them.

== stop council
It is 12 August, in the afternoon, on open ground outside the fort walls. Heald wrote that Potawatomi people came in from all around to get the goods in the government store. He meets their leaders here and promises them the goods. His own officers will not come. Instead, they climb into the gun towers that look over this ground. They point the cannons at the whole meeting. It is a meeting held under the guns. Heald has promised the goods. But he has not said what he will do with the guns and the whiskey.

== stop factory
It is 13 August, in the early afternoon. This log building is the factory, the government's trading house. Now everything on its shelves goes out the door: blankets, cloth and paints. Heald also gives away food that the soldiers cannot carry. General Hull's order said the goods could go to people who would help the soldiers get safely to Fort Wayne. But Heald keeps back the guns, the gunpowder and the whiskey. Those are what everyone watching cares about most. That night, inside the walls, Heald does something he hopes nobody will notice.

== stop covered-way
It is 13 August, in the evening, seen from above. A covered path runs from the fort's corner down to the river. That night, soldiers carry the extra gunpowder and whiskey down the path. They drop the gunpowder into a well. They break the extra guns and drop them in too. They break open the whiskey barrels and pour the whiskey into the river. Heald was afraid people would use those things against the soldiers. But it is not a secret. People can hear the noise. The next morning, one person said, the river water tasted like whiskey. And a rider from Fort Wayne is almost at the gate.

== stop the-gate
It is 14 August, at sunset, at the main gate. Captain William Wells rides in from Fort Wayne with about thirty Miami men. Wells is Rebekah Heald's uncle. He has come to help her and her husband get out safely. But one book says he came too late. The extra gunpowder is already gone. Rebekah and her husband take him to Captain Heald's house inside the fort. At nine tomorrow morning, the gate will open, and everyone inside will walk out.

== stop the-medal
It is 14 August, in the evening, at Captain Heald's house inside the fort. Black Partridge, a Potawatomi leader from the Illinois River, has come to the fort. He walks into the captain's house. He gives back his peace medal. He says he will not wear a sign of peace when he is forced to act as an enemy. Two people who were there remember his warning. Only one of them wrote about the medal. It is the clearest warning anyone gives. At nine tomorrow morning, the soldiers march.

== stop river-mouth
It is 15 August 1812, at nine in the morning. Heald wrote that they started their march at nine o'clock. They go out of the gate, down to the mouth of the river, and south along the beach. Wells and some of the Miami men go first. Then come the soldiers, the wagons with the women and children, and the twelve men from town. A book says the band played slow, sad music. The Kinzie family has been warned, so they wait in their boat at the river mouth. A mile and a half ahead, men are waiting behind a hill of sand.

== stop the-bank
It is about ten in the morning, two miles down the beach. The lake is on the left and a high bank of sand is on the right. Heald wrote that after a mile and a half, they found warriors waiting to attack from behind the sand bank. The soldiers run up the bank. Back at the wagons, there is fighting too. The men from town all lose their lives. A woman named Cicely and her baby son also die. They were among the few people at Chicago known to be held in slavery. Soon Heald has only a few dozen men left standing.

== stop cottonwoods
It is about half past ten. Heald gathers his last men on a small hill in the open grassland. A Potawatomi leader named Black Bird comes out. He shakes Heald's hand and asks him to give up. He promises to spare the lives of all the prisoners. Heald agrees. Heald later counted the dead: twenty-six soldiers, all the men from town, two women and twelve children. Ensign Ronan, the young doctor Isaac Van Voorhis, and Captain Wells are among them. The people who lived are led back north. At the Kinzie house, the danger is not over.

== stop kinzie-house
It is 15 August, at sunset, at John Kinzie's house. Kinzie is the trader who sold things to the fort. His family is kept safe and brought home. An old book says they were "closely guarded by their Indian friends". Mrs. Heald is badly hurt. Kinzie's helper trades a mule to free her. Kinzie carefully takes a bullet out of her arm with his small pocket knife. The gate of the yard is open. Across the river, the fort stands empty. In the morning, it will burn.

== stop the-burning
It is 16 August, the next morning, in front of the main gate. The soldiers marched out of it yesterday and never came back. All night, people took things from the empty fort and shared them. Shawls, ribbons and feathers flutter everywhere. One young man walks around proudly wearing a lady's dress and bonnet. Now young men come from the camps carrying burning sticks from their fires. They set the fort on fire. The soldiers' houses, the gun towers and the log walls catch fire one after another. Only the little brick gunpowder house does not burn. Pick up a burning stick and light the logs yourself.

== stop war-party
It is 16 August, in the morning, back at the Kinzie house. Across the river, the fort is burning. Black Partridge, Waubansee and Keepotah guard the porch. Then a group of warriors from the Wabash River comes in. They have painted their faces black. They sit down on the living room floor in silence. Then Billy Caldwell, called the Sauganash, walks in calmly and greets them as friends. Surprised, they say they only came for some white cloth to wrap their dead. A few doors west, a hurt woman is hiding, and men are searching the houses.

== stop ouilmette
It is 16 August, in the morning, while the fort burns. Margaret Helm, Kinzie's stepdaughter, was hurt near the wagons. Black Partridge has brought her here to the Ouilmette family's house. She is dressed in plain clothes like a French woman from the area. When searchers come, the family hides her under the edge of a big feather bed, with her face to the wall. Mrs. Bisson sits on the bed, sewing, as if nothing is wrong. Margaret is very scared, but she stays still. The searchers look around and leave the house. One historian is not sure every part of this story is true, but he believes the rescue happened.

== ending burning
It is 16 August 1812. Heald wrote that the next morning, the warriors set the fort on fire and left, taking the prisoners with them. Only the brick gunpowder house did not burn. On the third day, the Kinzies were put into a boat to St. Joseph. Heald and his wife were taken there too. Other prisoners were kept in nearby villages until the next spring. Then most of them were taken to Detroit and set free. The army came back in 1816 and built a second Fort Dearborn on the same ground. One historian warned that the Kinzie family's book, which shaped this story for a hundred years, was very unfair to Captain Heald.
