== premise
Three days after Arthur Caton's unexplained death in a New York hotel, Prairie Avenue whispers about his widow Delia and the wealthiest merchant in Chicago, whose mansion stands immediately across the alley.

== opening
JAUNT: THE MERCHANT AND MRS. CATON. Monday morning, 21 November 1904. A telegram has arrived from New York, and the blinds are lowered in a residence on Calumet Avenue.

Four stops, on foot, from the widow's door, through the rear yards the domestic servants share, to the front steps of Marshall Field, the merchant prince of Chicago. Everyone on Prairie Avenue is acquainted with this story, and propriety forbids anyone from acknowledging it. Three decades of dinners, transatlantic voyages and persistent rumors are approaching their resolution, and this morning the only people discussing it openly are those who deliver the coal.

Begin on Calumet Avenue, at the residence with the lowered blinds.

== stop widow
Calumet Avenue, behind Prairie, alongside the Caton residence, its blinds drawn in mourning. On Friday, 18 November, Arthur J. Caton died at New York's Waldorf-Astoria hotel, and the cause remains ambiguous: "Some said he succumbed to liver failure. Others ascribed the demise to peritonitis". A newspaper in Ottawa, Illinois, will insinuate suicide. He was the son of Judge John Dean Caton, among the state's earliest chief justices, and himself an attorney, sportsman and clubman who bred horses and dogs on the family's agricultural estate. His wife Delia, a hardware heiress he married in 1876, presided over the most celebrated entertainments in Chicago. They had no children. This morning there is a widow, and the intelligence is traveling from the residence to the stable. Proceed around the rear, into the yards between Calumet and Prairie.

== caption widow
In the Caton side yard a maid conveys the news to the coachman, who removes his hat, while the groom suspends his work at the coach house door.

== stop yards
The rear yards, where Calumet meets Prairie at the back fence. Turn around and the Field residence stands immediately before you, its coach house fronting the alley. The servants of both households have shared this ground for years and have absorbed every rumor circulating above stairs. Arthur Meeker, raised on this avenue, preserved the most extravagant: "it was even absurdly asserted that an underground tunnel connected the Caton house on Calumet with the Field house on Prairie". Meeker dismissed it as absurd, justifiably. Nevertheless a society diarist documented that "Delia sometimes traveled to Europe with Marshall Field without Arthur accompanying them". Now Arthur is dead, Field has been widowed for eight years, and a laundress from the Field household leans against the fence. Continue to Prairie Avenue and locate the merchant himself.

== caption yards
Karolina Hall crosses from the Field yard to the fence, where a Caton maid waits with the news.

== stop field
1905 Prairie, the Marshall Field mansion, constructed in 1876 for approximately $2 million and "the first in Chicago to be completely wired for electricity". Field is the most powerful businessman in the city, his fortune valued at $125 million the following year. Decades earlier he proposed to his first wife by leaping aboard her departing train; one account maintains that "Mrs. Field led Marshall Field a hell-on-earth life", and Nannie Field died in France in 1896. Meeker articulated the remainder candidly: "he fell in love with his friend's wife years before he was able to marry her", since "in the Nineties, divorce was unthinkable: they had to wait for him (Arthur Caton) to die". Thomas Benson, the Irish-born butler, attends the door; John Dunford, the English-born coachman, approaches from the stable. The mansion will be demolished in 1955.

== caption field
Marshall Field emerges from his entrance and pauses on the steps, gazing east toward Calumet Avenue, before his coachman reaches him.

== stop talk
From farther along the avenue, the Field residence appears indistinguishable from its neighbors; it is not. Field disliked public speaking, and one evening, at one of the Catons' elaborate dinners, he was invited to offer remarks. Delia rose immediately and responded on his behalf: "Mr. Field just wants me to remind you that the White Sale starts next Monday". It was a quip about his department store, delivered by another man's wife at her husband's own table, and the guests who subsequently repeated it characterized it as the evening everyone recognized the intimacy between those two. The ladies passing the Field residence this morning studiously avoid looking at it, and they are discussing nothing else. Arthur Caton remains unburied; everyone can anticipate the sequel, and decorum prevents anybody from articulating it.

== ending london
The walk concludes here. They waited less than a year. In 1905 they married at St. Margaret's, Westminster, in London; the license recorded Delia as forty-six and Field as seventy, and "the ceremony was quiet, and attended only by a few friends". They returned to Chicago as husband and wife, and in January 1906 Marshall Field died of pneumonia, contracted during a New Year's Day golf match in a foursome that included Robert Todd Lincoln. After three decades of discreet waiting, they shared only a few months. Delia inherited $1 million and the Prairie Avenue mansion, relocated to Washington, where her residence acquired the nickname the Pink Palace, and survived until 1937.
