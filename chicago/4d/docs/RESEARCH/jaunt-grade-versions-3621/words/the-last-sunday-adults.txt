== opening
JAUNT: THE LAST SUNDAY. Sunday 8 October 1871, early afternoon. No rain since the end of September, a dry summer behind it, and a hot wind blowing hard out of the southwest. Last night a planing mill on the West Side caught fire and took four blocks of lumber, coal and workers' houses with it; the firemen who fought it all night are still on the ruins. This morning's Tribune gave the fire three columns and then got back to business: land, money, schemes.

Nine stops, from the wholesale blocks to the marble hotels, the grain exchange, the new shops of State Street and the mansions by the Water Tower, and then, at dusk, a rented cottage and a milk barn on DeKoven Street. Start on Lake Street.

== stop lake-street
Lake Street at Clark, two in the afternoon. Until the mid-1860s this was 'the retail center of Chicago', and the wholesale houses that made the city's money still crowd the blocks down to the river. Stone fronts, arched windows, iron awnings, telegraph wires. Under some of them the ground was once several feet lower: from 1859 George M. Pullman, later the sleeping-car king, jacked whole blocks of brick and stone up to the new street grade 'without even disturbing the operations of business inside'. Nothing here stops for long, except on Sunday. The stores are shut today, and the men who own them are reading the Tribune, where a watcher of the land market summed up the mood: 'Every body seems to be swelled up with big schemes.' One block south is the grandest scheme in town, a hotel of white marble.

== stop sherman-house
The Sherman House, at Clark and Randolph. Francis C. Sherman put up a hotel on this corner in 1836-37, did well enough to serve two terms as mayor, and tore it down in 1861 for this: six storeys of 'the finest Athens marble, from the quarries at Lemont', a two-storey portico on Clark Street, and a view over the Court House square. This week a celebrated orchestra is upstairs. Theodore Thomas has brought 'his concert organization of sixty performers' to open a season of ten concerts tomorrow night. Across Randolph Street hangs a bronze bell of more than five tons, in a tower that is also the city's fire lookout.

== stop court-house
Court House Square, the block between Randolph, Clark, Washington and LaSalle. Lawns, trees and a fountain give it 'the appearance of a park' behind an iron fence. John M. Van Osdel's building of 1853 holds nearly the whole machinery of city and county, 'from the jail in its basement to the mayor's quarters': the prisoners, the debtors, the aldermen and the courts under one roof. Since then the city has added wings, a new clock and a bell of 10,849 pounds. Up in the cupola, 120 feet above the street, is the fire watchman's walk. Tonight Mathias Schaffer will stand there with a spy-glass, the city's first line of defence, reading the distance of a fire by its glow. Below him is the telegraph office that sends out the engines. Across LaSalle Street, the grain of the West is traded.

== stop chamber
The Chamber of Commerce, at the southeast corner of Washington and LaSalle. Edward Burling designed it; Charles Randolph, president of the Board of Trade, dedicated it on 30 August 1865. Its great hall is 'decorated with frescoes and paintings', and what happens under them is the Board's commodities exchange, where the grain of the prairie is bought and sold, and fortunes with it. On Sunday it is empty. LaSalle Street around it, all stone fronts and mansard roofs, has become 'the home of Insurance Companies and commission merchants', and the banks are crowding in. Two blocks east, on Washington Street, workmen are working through the Sabbath to finish a theatre.

== stop crosbys
Crosby's Opera House, on Washington Street between State and Dearborn. Uranus Crosby built it in 1865 with the architect W. W. Boyington: five storeys, a mansard roof, and statues of Painting, Sculpture, Music and Commerce over the arch, Commerce included without embarrassment. Shops fill the ground floor; the auditorium behind seats three thousand. The house has had about $80,000 spent on it this summer, and this morning's Tribune promised that 'the whole house, gallery and all, is nearly sold for the first night.' Tomorrow Theodore Thomas opens the season. Late this evening the gas jets will be lit inside for the first time, 'that its effects might be seen by gaslight'. One block east is the street Potter Palmer built.

== stop state-street
State Street at Washington. Four years ago this stretch was a frame house, an ale store and a furniture shop. Potter Palmer, who had made one fortune in trade and part of it speculating in wartime cotton, bought 'three-quarters of a mile of property along State Street south of Lake', cleared it, and persuaded his old partners to follow. On the northeast corner is their six-storey store, Field & Leiter, almost 150 feet along State. Marshall Field and Levi Leiter left Lake Street for it, and their clerks are drilled to treat the carriage trade 'like guests rather than mere customers'. A few blocks south is Palmer's new hotel, the Palmer House, under his own name. Across the river, on the North Side, the old money lives under great elms.

== stop water-tower
The Water Tower, Pine Street at Chicago Avenue. The city threw a parade for it in 1867. Across the street the Pumping Station pulls water through a tunnel two miles out under the lake, because the river the city drinks from is 'unspeakably polluted'. W. W. Boyington dressed both buildings in 'castellated Gothic' stone. South of here are the mansions, 'substantial and elegant, and shaded with grand elms'; the working families live west of LaSalle. For years the Board of Police and Fire Commissioners has warned that 'the water supply was insufficient', that the hydrants were twice too far apart, and that 'wooden cornices were an abomination'. 'None of these things was noticed by the mayor, the Common Council, or the newspapers.' The whole city depends on one set of pumps, under a wooden roof.

== stop saturday-ruins
The east end of the Van Buren Street bridge, looking across to the West Side. Last night between ten and eleven, the boiler room of Lull & Holmes's planing mill on Canal Street caught fire, in a district the insurance trade calls the 'Red Flash' for how fast it burns. By this afternoon four blocks and close to a million dollars were gone, and about one hundred families were out of their homes. 'The piles of coal and lumber burned all day on Sunday', and thousands came to stare. Much of the Fire Department worked eighteen hours straight, until three this afternoon; the men are 'nearly exhausted', short-handed, some of their equipment damaged. The weather has not changed: no rain, and a hard southwest wind. Ten blocks south, on DeKoven Street, a woman has done the evening milking.

== stop dekoven
137 DeKoven Street, at dusk. A neighbourhood of small wooden houses, sheds and fences, with 'wood shavings piled in every barn and under every house', kindling from the mills. Patrick and Catherine O'Leary live in the back of this cottage with their five children and rent the front to the McLaughlins. Behind is the barn, sixteen by twenty feet, its loft freshly packed with hay for the winter, with five cows, a calf, a horse and a wagon, and not one cent of insurance on any of it. Catherine peddles milk; she milked at half past four and will feed the horse at about seven. Next door tonight there will be a party for a cousin just arrived from Ireland, with a fiddle. The wind has not dropped.

== ending the-barn
At about nine that night Daniel Sullivan, a drayman sitting on the sidewalk across the street, saw fire in the O'Leary barn. Within thirty minutes it 'was beyond the control of the Fire Department.' In the Court House cupola Mathias Schaffer read the glow as farther off than it was and called the wrong box; when he knew it, no correct alarm went out, and engines that might have come stayed home. By Monday morning the Court House bell had fallen through the burning building, the Sherman House was 'one of the most complete wrecks of the night', and Crosby's never reopened. At about three in the morning the pumping works, under their wooden roof, became 'an utter wreck'. Theodore Thomas 'was compelled to run for his life'.

According to legend, Mrs. O'Leary's cow kicked over a lamp, and a milkwoman took the blame for a city. The historian A. T. Andreas, writing fourteen years later: 'There is no proof that Mrs. O'Leary is responsible for the fire.' The Great Chicago Fire jaunt begins in that barn.
