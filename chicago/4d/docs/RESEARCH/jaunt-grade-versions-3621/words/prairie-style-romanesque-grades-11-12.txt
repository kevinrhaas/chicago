== premise
Rusticated stone, deeply recessed round arches and turrets: four Romanesque façades between Eighteenth Street and Twenty-first.

== opening
JAUNT: ROMANESQUE STYLE. A winter afternoon, 1904, with the elms leafless and the masonry exposed. During the 1880s a Boston architect, Henry Hobson Richardson, revived the prestige of massive masonry: rough-faced blocks, profound round arches evoking a castle gateway, and towers. Chicago embraced the idiom enthusiastically, and for several years its wealthy residents commissioned fortresses.

Four residences on foot, from 1800 to 2110 Prairie. Identify rock-faced stone, arches that appear to recede into the wall, and turrets.

Commence with the residence that inaugurated the fashion, the one its neighbors resented.

== stop glessner
1800 Prairie, Glessner House, the avenue's original Richardsonian Romanesque composition. Its street elevations are brick veneered with alternating six- and eight-inch sheets of granite, cut deeper at the openings so the entire wall simulates monolithic stone, beneath a roof of unglazed red tiles. Richardson, all 370 pounds of him, maintained that the windows of an urban residence were "not to look out of", so the street elevation is nearly windowless while the family rooms open toward a courtyard behind. George Pullman, diagonally opposite, protested about "that thing staring me in the face". Across the avenue and slightly south, the idiom acquired imitators.

== stop ames
1811 Prairie, the Coleman residence of 1886, designed by Cobb and Frost, architects distinguished for their Romanesque commissions. This façade is rough-faced brown sandstone. The 1888 plate displays the idiom's signature composition: a profound round arch on clustered columns surmounting a broad stair, with a corbelled oriel projecting above it like a sentinel's lookout on a castle rampart. Observe that the arch constitutes the entrance itself: in this idiom, entering becomes a passage tunneled into the masonry. Walk south beyond Twentieth Street for two smaller fortresses.

== stop r2011
2011 Prairie, a neighbor of the Mayer residence. The district register classifies it as Romanesque: a rough ashlar façade, a round-arched entrance, and a rounded corner turret beneath a conical cap. A turret on an urban lot is essentially theatrical, a defensive tower grafted incongruously onto a townhouse. Across the avenue and one block farther, the final example compresses the idiom onto a single narrow lot.

== stop rees
2110 Prairie, Harriet Rees's narrow residence, which the district register attributes to Cobb and Frost in 1888 and classifies as Romanesque: a limestone façade beneath a tall gable addressing the avenue. Two years after the Coleman residence, the identical architects interpreted the idiom in pale stone for a woman's household. Compare the two compositions: the same firm, employing opposite palettes.

== ending finish
The Romanesque walk concludes here. Rusticated stone, round arches and towers characterized the architectural fashion of the 1880s on this avenue.

Nevertheless Richardson's fortress outlived virtually every residence surrounding it, and it remains standing. Walk back to Eighteenth Street and examine it once more in the winter illumination.
