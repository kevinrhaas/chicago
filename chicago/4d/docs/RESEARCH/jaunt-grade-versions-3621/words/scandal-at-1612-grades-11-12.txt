== premise
A carriage manufacturer's showplace has become the most scrutinized residence on the block: a society divorce, a judicial order, a violin in a disabled automobile, and a small boy positioned at the center of everything.

== opening
JAUNT: THE SCANDAL AT 1612 PRAIRIE. A Monday afternoon in November 1904, at the tranquil northern extremity of Prairie Avenue.

Four stops along a single short block. The residence at 1612 has served as a showplace for twenty years; this year it has become a battlefield. A husband has initiated litigation, a judge has awarded the residence to the wife, and in November the husband stood in open court and leveled accusations that the entire city has subsequently consumed in the Tribune. The neighbors keep their curtains drawn and their attention acutely engaged.

Begin next door, where you can envision the residence as it formerly appeared.

== stop showplace
1608 Prairie, and adjoining it the substantial square residence at 1612. Two decades ago it belonged to a Studebaker, of the Studebaker Brothers carriage works, and it was the most resplendent house on the avenue. In February 1884 approximately two hundred guests arrived from a family wedding, and "the large residence was brilliantly illuminated, and every room was elaborately decorated with smilax and cut flowers". An 1887 society column documented the traffic: "Morning and night, a showy turnout is noticed before the squarely-built house, with the number 1612 over the portals". A carriage manufacturer's residence ought to exhibit the finest equipages at its entrance, and it did. Observe it now: no carriage waits at the curb, and a neighboring maid monitors it from her steps. Walk to the gate of 1612.

== caption showplace
A maid at 1608 sweeps her steps while maintaining vigilant surveillance of the residence next door.

== stop decree
1612 Prairie. The Tribune possesses the story, as does everyone else. Last May the husband, Charles E. Brown, petitioned for divorce. His wife Catherine is twenty-seven, "the daughter of Gen. Graves, a wealthy resident of Buffalo", and the stated grounds were "habitual drunkenness and statutory charges". Judge Tuley subsequently granted Mrs. Brown possession of this residence, which she, her husband and their son had occupied, together with $200 monthly in temporary alimony pending resolution. Consequently the wife occupies the fortress while the husband remains excluded. Behind those windows reside a woman whom the newspapers have rendered notorious and a small boy. The residence will survive until its demolition around 1960. Proceed next door to learn what the husband alleged in November.

== caption decree
A lady emerges onto the porch of 1612, surveys the avenue in both directions, and withdraws before anyone can approach her.

== stop charges
1616 Prairie, the adjacent residence to the south, where the neighbors peruse the Tribune over coffee. In November Mr. Brown returned to court, alleging that his wife "had conducted herself in a disgraceful manner, had been habitually intoxicated, and had done many freakish things, such as playing a violin in a broken automobile while a man pushed the machine along a public highway, and dancing about the rear of their home clothed only in a nightrobe". He additionally identified "E. C. Edsall, an automobile salesman", with whom, the husband asserted, she absconded to an eastern summer resort and subsequently to New York City. It constitutes the most sensational scandal on Prairie Avenue in years, and two maids are relishing it. Yet a divorce never concerns only two individuals. Look for the boy.

== caption charges
Two maids on the steps of 1616 share the newspaper and cast glances toward 1612.

== stop boy
Across the avenue at 1613, look back at the western side of the block. The boy is Charles Edward Brown, the couple's only son, and he represents the genuine prize in this contest. A nurse escorts him along the sidewalk past his own entrance; his mother retains the residence, whereas his father seeks its recovery, and his. The court has not yet ruled. In newspaper columns he appears merely as a concluding sentence, but on this walk he is the sole participant in the entire affair who has done absolutely nothing. The Tribune will report the resolution in March 1905, by which time the residence, the boy and the woman will each have followed divergent paths.

== ending march
The walk concludes here. Following a bitter contest early in 1905, the husband prevailed, obtaining the divorce, the Prairie Avenue property and custody of the boy. On 8 March 1905 he conveyed the property to Charles Palmerston Anderson, the Episcopal bishop of Chicago. That same week the Tribune located Catherine Brown in a police cell, arrested at 3667 Wabash Avenue, where police reported she had been living as Kittie Casey, and fined $85 for disorderly conduct. "I am penniless", she declared. "I cannot pay the fine". From her cell she reflected, "It's all because a woman is jealous of me, I guess". The general's daughter from Buffalo had retained 1612 Prairie for less than a year, and the newspapers that manufactured her notoriety recorded almost exclusively her husband's allegations, a reminder to read every such account with deliberate skepticism.
