== premise
George Pullman put up a bronze statue of a battle from 1812 near his door. A year later soldiers came to Chicago again, this time to stop his own workers. Who gets to tell history?

== opening
JAUNT: THE BRONZE AT EIGHTEENTH STREET. It is a Monday afternoon in November 1904, at the corner of Eighteenth Street and Prairie Avenue.

There are four stops around one street corner. Under the street is an old battlefield. Near it stands a bronze statue that tells a story about the battle. Next is the house of the man who paid for the statue. Last is the house that stared back at him. At every stop, ask: what really happened, and who chose how we remember it?

Start where the street starts, on the ground where the battle was fought.

== stop ground
This is Prairie Avenue at Eighteenth Street. Long ago, before the houses, there were sand hills here by the lake. On 15 August 1812 the Battle of Fort Dearborn was fought across them. The people who died lay in the sand for four years. For a hundred years a big cottonwood tree marked the place. Now look at the street in 1904. There is brick, granite stone and gas lamps. A teacher is bringing her class to see the sights. The battle was 92 years ago, and now it is a lesson. Who wrote the lesson? Walk east along Eighteenth Street to the statue.

== caption ground
A teacher leads a line of children along Eighteenth Street to the statue.

== stop bronze
Here is the statue. George Pullman paid for it in 1893 and put it near his front door. It shows Black Partridge, a Potawatomi chief, saving Margaret Helm, an army officer's wife. It is exciting, but it may never have happened. Some historians think the story was made up later. They say Mrs. Helm rode her horse to the lake shore and found safety there. The artist did not study the real people of 1812. One historian says his figures looked more like a Wild West show. The teacher is reading the sign out loud. Now look at the house behind the statue.

== caption bronze
The teacher reads to her class at the foot of the statue. The children look up at it.

== stop strike
This is 1729 Prairie, the Pullman house. Mr. Pullman built railroad sleeping cars. South of the city he built a whole town for his workers, and they paid him rent. In 1894 he cut their pay but did not lower the rent. So the workers went on strike. Here is the part the statue does not tell. A year after the statue went up, army soldiers came to Chicago to stop the strike against Mr. Pullman. Pullman died in 1897, and his house will be torn down in 1922. Cross Prairie to the house that stared at his.

== caption strike
Two working men carrying tools stop on the far sidewalk to look at the Pullman house. Then they walk on.

== stop grave
This is the Glessner house, across the corner from Pullman. Pullman did not like it. He said he did not know what he had done to have "that thing staring me in the face" every time he went out his door. When he died, his family was afraid robbers, or angry workers, might dig up his grave. So they buried him in a box sealed in a big block of concrete, with steel rails on top. One historian says we don't really know if every part of that story is true. That is the lesson of this corner. Bronze, stone and concrete are on top. Under all of it is the old sand.

== caption grave
The class walks past the Glessner house on its way home. The teacher looks back at the Pullman house.

== ending sand
The walk ends here. In 1904 the statue still stands at Eighteenth Street, telling its story one way. Some historians doubt the rescue ever happened. Pullman lies under his concrete. People still learn about the strike he broke. The ground under this corner still holds the sand of 1812. A statue tells you who paid for it. To learn the rest, you have to dig.
