== premise
A quiet circuit of the familiar streets, passing a shopfront, the corner where the town's newspaper originated and two public houses, with nothing to count or carry.

== opening
JAUNT: AN EVENING STROLL. Dusk on 1 July 1835: the land buyers have gone in to supper, the river has fallen quiet, and for once there is nothing you are obliged to do. Chicago has existed as a town for less than two years, and when it was incorporated in August 1833, only "thirteen votes were cast by the twenty-eight legal electors". Now strangers arrive by the boatload.

Take a leisurely circuit of the familiar streets, from the Sauganash, past the village's first frame store and the corner where its first newspaper was printed, to the hotel where the town holds its meetings. Then decide where the evening concludes.

== stop the-sauganash
Begin at the Sauganash, on the corner of Lake and Market above the South Branch, white and two stories tall with bright-blue shutters and newly fitted up this June. Mark Beaubien built it, and he has led a remarkably busy life in this town. Moses and Kirkland call him "the fun of every funeral and the life of every wake", and list him "As fiddler, landlord, merchant, ferryman and a patron of horse racing". In May 1833 Father St. Cyr "celebrated his first mass in a log cabin belonging to Mark Beaubien".

== narrative the-sauganash
Set out from a public house's door.

== stop the-shopfront
Cross to the river and continue east along South Water Street to LaSalle and P. F. W. Peck's store, the first two-story frame building in the village, constructed of lumber "sawed from black walnut trees". Peck sells dry goods, hardware, groceries, crockery, boots, shoes and hats, and he is a notably careful man. Years from now he will be credited "with having $40,000 in cash locked in an iron safe in his dwelling", and considered "nearly as wealthy as John Jacob Astor".

== narrative the-shopfront
A shopfront on the trading street.

== stop the-printing-office
One block east, at South Water and Clark, John Calhoun began printing the Chicago Democrat in 1833; by 20 May its office had moved above Jones and King's hardware along the street. It appears on Wednesdays, and today's issue is No. 11 of its second volume. When the mail arrived, the town had an alternative way to obtain the news: "some one with a good voice would mount a dry goods box" and read it aloud, and if political feeling ran high, there were two boxes and two competing readers.

== narrative the-printing-office
Where the town's newspaper began.

== stop the-exchange
South to Lake Street and west to Wells stands the Exchange Coffee House, another house Mark Beaubien built, which Mr. and Mrs. Murphy have kept as a hotel since August 1834. The town's Democrats assembled here in April and again in June to select their candidates, and a dancing master collected his pupils' addresses here last winter. Choose where the evening concludes.

== narrative the-exchange
A second public house, and a choice of where to stop.

== ending at-the-exchange
You conclude the stroll at the corner of Lake and Wells in the last of the light, having passed the village's first frame store, its first newspaper's corner and two of Mark Beaubien's houses, which is a respectable inventory for a town not yet two years old.

== ending where-you-began
You walk back west along Lake Street to the Sauganash and close the circuit precisely where it began. If the windows are open, you may hear the fiddle.
