== premise
A German sergeant maintained that George Pullman was his father. For four years he pursued the family, to the office, to the funeral, to the front steps, and ultimately to the wrong end of a judge's revolver.

== opening
JAUNT: PULLMAN'S OTHER SON. A Monday afternoon in November 1904, outside the Pullman mansion at Eighteenth Street.

Four stops, encircling the Pullman residence and continuing down the avenue to the home of the family's son-in-law. George Pullman fathered twin sons whose names the entire city recognized. A third young man asserted a claim to that name. His mother disclosed on her deathbed the identity of his father, and he crossed an ocean to proclaim it to Pullman directly. The family dismissed him as deranged; the physicians repeatedly certified him sane.

Begin at the front of the mansion, where his narrative originates.

== stop mother
1729 Prairie, the Pullman mansion. Imagine a German Army sergeant named Gustave Behring, twenty-eight years old, at his dying mother's bedside. She confided that his biological father was George M. Pullman. She had been employed as a governess in Chicago "for a house on the west side", she explained, and upon becoming pregnant, Pullman allegedly dispatched her to Germany with $500. Behring traveled to Chicago, where Pullman expelled him from his office. He was subsequently confined in an asylum and arrested six additional times for loitering outside the Pullman Building. Four days before Pullman's death, their confrontation was documented: "BEHRING: You are my father! PULLMAN: You are a crazy man!" The mansion will be demolished in 1922. Walk around to the front steps.

== caption mother
A stranger in a dark overcoat pauses at the Pullman gate, scrutinizes the mansion, and continues onward.

== stop steps
The front steps. Pullman succumbed to a heart attack in October 1897, and his family interred him beneath concrete and steel. "Less than 12 hours after the funeral, Behring showed up at the family's residence on Prairie Avenue, demanding to speak to Mrs. Harriett Pullman". He never reached the entrance. On these steps he was intercepted by Pullman's twin sons and the family's attorneys, and he was arrested yet again. On 29 October 1897 a specialist in insanity examined him and pronounced him sane: not a lunatic, therefore, but merely a man who declined to vanish. The Pullmans' housekeeper, Emma Scott, continues to answer this door. Walk around the corner to the conservatory.

== caption steps
Emma Scott, the housekeeper, opens the front door, surveys the deserted steps, and closes it again.

== stop ship
The conservatory, the family's glass parlor. Behind these panes the Pullmans awaited their problem's departure, and in September 1898 it departed: detectives escorted Behring up the gangway of the Cunard liner Campania, bound for Bremen. Europe could retain him. Nevertheless he returned, because a man informed by his dying mother of his paternity does not remain deported. By 1901 the twins who had obstructed the steps confronted scandals of their own, and George Jr. would die of pneumonia that November. Florence Pullman's husband, Frank Lowden, practiced law, so when Behring reappeared, he approached Lowden. Walk south along Prairie to the Lowden residence.

== caption ship
The groom, Samuel Whatter Jr., emerges from the stable yard beside the conservatory and stands surveying the avenue.

== stop gun
1912 Prairie, where Frank Lowden resides with Florence and their children. Lowden is a methodical man, but even methodical men experience dramatic afternoons. "On June 25, 1901, Behring was again arrested at the law offices of Frank Lowden (Florence Pullman's husband) demanding $5,000 in cash and $10,000 a month for 6 months". Lowden's partner declined to await the authorities' diplomacy: "Judge Davis, Lowden's partner, held Behring at gunpoint until the police arrived". The residence will be demolished in 1939. Observe Lowden returning home this afternoon, glancing apprehensively down the avenue before entering.

== ending name
The walk concludes here. Gustave Behring was arrested, institutionalized, examined, certified sane, deported and arrested once more, and as contemporary newspapers reported it, his narrative terminates at the muzzle of a judge's revolver. "You are my father!" "You are a crazy man!" Those two sentences constitute everything surviving from the only conversation the two men ever conducted. Evaluate the evidence, consider which party possessed the wealth and influence necessary to be believed, and determine for yourself which of them was telling the truth.
