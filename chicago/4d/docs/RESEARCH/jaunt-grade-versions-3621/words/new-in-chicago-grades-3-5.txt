== premise
Find your way around, find a bed, and find a good next step on a made-up first day in town.

== opening
JAUNT: NEW IN CHICAGO. It is your first morning in a brand new town. Less than two years ago it had only twenty-eight men who could vote. This summer it has two newspapers, a land office, and more strangers than beds. You are one of them.

There are five stops, from the forks of the river to South Water Street. You will find a landmark to steer by, the old mail corner, the store everyone uses, a map of the town, and a bed for tonight, if you can get one. The inns were always full. Full meant three people in a bed.

== stop arrival
Start outside the Sauganash inn. Juliette Kinzie remembered it as a white two-story house with blue shutters. A history book calls it the nicest inn the city had. It says its owner, Mark Beaubien, with his happy fiddle, was the most popular host in town. Every new person learns this corner first.

== narrative arrival
Use this landmark as your starting point. You are finding your way, and the town is making room for you.

== stop mail-corner
This is the old mail corner. John Hogan became the postmaster in 1832. He handed out letters in a small room walled off inside his log store. The post office moved in about July 1834. Now Hogan's ad says his store is one door down from the post office. Ask for your letters there, not here.

== narrative mail-corner
Use the old corner as a landmark. But do not mix it up with the place where the mail is handed out now.

== stop store
At Peck's store, on South Water and LaSalle Streets, find a place to buy supplies. It was the town's first two-story wooden building. It was built of black walnut boards brought from Detroit by boat. His ads list cloth, tools, food, dishes, boots, shoes and hats. Upstairs, Peck gave a young minister named Jeremiah Porter a place to live and study.

== narrative store
Remember where everyday things are sold.

== stop newspaper
This building is where the Democrat newspaper first had its office. By May 1835 it was printed over a hardware store. That spring the paper had an ad for printed maps of the town. You could buy them at the paper's office.

== narrative newspaper
A map of the town is a smart next step. Read the ad.

== stop lodging
End at Brown's boarding house. A history book says Mrs Rufus Brown ran a boarding house. Her log house stood behind Peck's store, which you passed a block ago. The book says the inns were always full. Full meant three in a bed sometimes, with more people sleeping on the floor. A spot on the floor counts as a bed.

== narrative lodging
On your first day, what matters more: cost or being close by?

== label lodging by-the-week
Board by the week

== consequence lodging by-the-week
Your route note picks cost. One deal for your whole stay.

== label lodging near-the-stores
A bed close to the stores

== consequence lodging near-the-stores
Your route note picks being close. Peck's store is right in front.

== leg 0
Follow the route toward the old mail corner. Your first day is taking shape.

== leg 1
Ride along the river street toward the stores. Find where everyday things are sold.

== leg 2
Look for news, and for something to help you find your way.

== leg 3
With a map in mind, think about where to sleep tonight.

== ending settled-by-cost
Your route note ends with a weekly room behind Peck's store. You picked cost. You may share the floor, but you have an address in the fastest-growing town in the West.

== ending settled-by-convenience
Your route note ends with a bed a few steps from the stores. You picked being close, and tomorrow starts near everything.

== ending finding-your-feet
You have a few names and corners to put together. Every early settler in this town started right here: new, muddy and looking for a bed.
