== premise
A carriage manufacturer's showplace has become the most discussed house on the block: a society divorce, a judge's order, a violin in a disabled automobile, and a small boy caught in the middle of it all.

== opening
JAUNT: THE SCANDAL AT 1612 PRAIRIE. A Monday afternoon in November 1904, at the quiet northern end of Prairie Avenue.

Four stops along one short block. The house at 1612 has been a showplace for twenty years; this year it has become a battlefield. A husband has gone to court, a judge has awarded the house to the wife, and in November the husband stood up in open court and made accusations about her that the entire city has now read in the Tribune. The neighbors keep their curtains closed and their ears wide open.

Begin next door, where you can picture the house as it once was.

== stop showplace
1608 Prairie, and beside it the large square house at 1612. Twenty years ago it belonged to a Studebaker, of the Studebaker Brothers carriage works, and it was the most brilliant house on the street. In February 1884 some two hundred guests arrived here from a family wedding, and "the large residence was brilliantly illuminated, and every room was elaborately decorated with smilax and cut flowers". A society column of 1887 remarked on the traffic: "Morning and night, a showy turnout is noticed before the squarely-built house, with the number 1612 over the portals". A carriage manufacturer's house ought to display the finest carriages at its door, and it did. Observe it now: no turnout waits at the curb this afternoon, and a maid next door watches it from her steps. Walk to the gate of 1612.

== caption showplace
A maid at 1608 sweeps her steps while keeping a watchful eye on the house next door.

== stop decree
1612 Prairie. The Tribune has the story, and so does everyone else. Last May the husband, Charles E. Brown, filed for divorce. His wife Catherine is twenty-seven, "the daughter of Gen. Graves, a wealthy resident of Buffalo", and the grounds were "habitual drunkenness and statutory charges". Judge Tuley then granted Mrs. Brown possession of this house, where she, her husband and their son had lived, along with $200 a month in temporary alimony while the suit was pending. So the wife occupies the fortress and the husband stands outside it. Behind those windows live a woman the newspapers have made notorious and a small boy. The house will survive until it is demolished around 1960. Step next door and learn what the husband alleged in November.

== caption decree
A lady steps out onto the porch of 1612, glances up and down the street, and retreats inside before anyone can approach.

== stop charges
1616 Prairie, the next house south, where the neighbors read the Tribune over their coffee. In November Mr. Brown returned to court and charged that his wife "had conducted herself in a disgraceful manner, had been habitually intoxicated, and had done many freakish things, such as playing a violin in a broken automobile while a man pushed the machine along a public highway, and dancing about the rear of their home clothed only in a nightrobe". He went further and named a man, "E. C. Edsall, an automobile salesman", with whom, the husband charged, she fled to an eastern summer resort and later to New York City. It is the most delicious scandal on Prairie Avenue in years, and two maids on these steps are savoring it. But a divorce never involves only two people. Look for the boy.

== caption charges
Two maids on the steps of 1616 share the newspaper and glance across at 1612.

== stop boy
Across the street at 1613, look back at the west side of the block. The boy is Charles Edward Brown, the couple's only son, and he is the true prize in this contest. A nurse walks him along the sidewalk past his own front door; his mother holds the house, while his father wants it back, and wants him. The court has not yet ruled. In the newspapers he is merely a line at the end of each story, but on this walk he is the only person in the entire affair who has done nothing at all. The Tribune will report the conclusion in March 1905, and by then the house, the boy and the woman will each have gone separate ways.

== ending march
The walk ends here. After a bitter struggle, early in 1905, the husband prevailed, winning the divorce, the house on Prairie Avenue and custody of the boy. On 8 March 1905 he sold the property to Charles Palmerston Anderson, the Episcopal bishop of Chicago. That same week the Tribune discovered Catherine Brown in a police cell, arrested at 3667 Wabash Avenue, where police said she had been living as Kittie Casey, and fined $85 for disorderly conduct. "I am penniless", she said. "I cannot pay the fine". From her cell she added, "It's all because a woman is jealous of me, I guess". The general's daughter from Buffalo had held 1612 Prairie for less than a year, and the newspapers that made her famous told only her husband's side.
