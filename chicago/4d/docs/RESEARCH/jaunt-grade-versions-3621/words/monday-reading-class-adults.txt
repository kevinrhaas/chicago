== opening
JAUNT: MONDAY AT THE FORTRESS. Monday morning, 21 November 1904, at the corner of Eighteenth Street and Prairie Avenue.

Five stops in and around the Glessner House. Today is a Monday, which means the Reading Class: the wives of University of Chicago professors and the ladies of the South Side, gathering in the library at the invitation of Frances Glessner. Inside these walls everything runs like a clock, and the people who make it run are kept out of sight. Across Eighteenth Street, in the house of the piano king, a rich old man is dying slowly, and the street is waiting for it.

Start at the corner, where the neighbours first saw what Richardson had built.

== stop fortress
The corner of Eighteenth and Prairie. The house is granite, low and hard, with windows on the street side barely wide enough to light a corridor: a house that keeps the city out. Its architect, H. H. Richardson, died in 1886 before it was built; it went up between June 1886 and December 1887. Not everyone liked it. George Pullman, just across the way, complained: "I do not know what I have ever done to have that thing staring me in the face every time I go out of my door". Pullman is seven years dead now, and the house is still staring. What the passers-by never see is the other side: generous windows onto a sunny courtyard. The ladies are arriving on foot.

== stop hostess
The front door. In 1893 William Rainey Harper, the president of the new University of Chicago, asked Frances Glessner how the professors' wives might meet the women of the city. Her answer was the Monday Morning Reading Class: two hours in the library, every Monday from October to May, sewing while a professional reader reads aloud. Nobody joins by asking. "Membership was by invitation only", and for a professor's wife new to Chicago, on a professor's salary, an invitation from this house is the way into a society that runs on money she does not have. Mrs. Glessner greets her guests herself. Christine Nelson, the parlor maid, stands ready for their wraps. Then the door shuts.

== stop hall
Inside, the house turns its back on the street and opens to the courtyard. The door to the court has yellow glass that gives the hall "a feeling of sunlight even in cloudy weather", and today is cloudy. The plan is a puzzle box: every principal room has two or more ways in and out, and the passages are "so planned that servants rarely are in evidence". That was the point. While the needles go in and out in the library, a whole second household moves round them unseen: the cook, the maids, the man who keeps the fires, designed out of the ladies' sight. The ladies sew. The reader reads. The help keeps out of sight in the court, where only you can see them.

== stop bench
The service stair, down to the basement, the servants' level. Down here is the newest room in the house: the workbench Frances Glessner set up this year, where she makes silver with a hammer and a torch and stamps each piece with "a G encircling a honeybee". Upstairs she is the most correct hostess in Chicago. Downstairs she is a craftsman taking lessons from a Hull House jeweller, working with her hands in the part of the house built for the people who work with theirs. Both are true. But across Eighteenth Street a neighbour has been ill for a long time, and the doctor is calling again. Look out through the gates toward the house of William W. Kimball, the piano maker.

== stop kimball
1801 Prairie, across Eighteenth Street: the Kimball house. William Wallace Kimball came to Chicago in 1857 and traded his Iowa land for four pianos, which he sold from a second-floor office. Now his factory turns out about a hundred pianos and organs a week. He and his wife Evaline have no children. And he is dying. He will die "at home after a lingering illness" on 16 December, and his funeral will be held in this house. Watch the doctor going up the steps. Among his honorary pallbearers will be "Marshall Field, Robert T. Lincoln" and, from the house of the Reading Class, "J. J. Glessner": the richest men in Chicago, carrying one of their own.

== ending clock
End of the walk. W. W. Kimball died on 16 December 1904, and John Glessner crossed Eighteenth Street to stand among his honorary pallbearers. The Reading Class went on, every Monday from October to May, "until 1930". When Frances Glessner died, her husband tried to say who she had been: "She had a clean and wholesome and orderly mind, a heart overflowing with love for family and friends and for all in any need." That is the house in one sentence: clean, wholesome, orderly, generous to its own, and kept that way by people the guests were never meant to see.
