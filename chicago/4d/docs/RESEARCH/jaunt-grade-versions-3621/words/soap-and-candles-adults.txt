== opening
JAUNT: SOAP AND CANDLES. Dusk comes early now. Autumn means the long dark is close: once the lake freezes, 'during the long six months of winter the inhabitants, were cut off from the rest of the world'. Light and soap are what get a crowded boarding house through it: light against the long evenings, soap against the stink of unwashed lodgers sharing beds and a single table.

You board in the log house behind Peck's store. Look up the North Branch at the soap and candle works, make a short list on South Water Street, choose what to carry, and bring it home before full dark.

== stop the-works
Start at Wolf Point and look up the North Branch to its east bank, where the scene sets Daniel Elston & Co.'s works; no source gives the site. Their notice, printed 1833–34, offered hard and soft soap, wax and other candles, and paid cash for tallow in any quantity and for good house ashes. Soap is ashes leached for lye and boiled with rendered animal fat; a candle is the same fat dipped. Every butchered carcass and every cold hearth in town comes back to it as light and cleanliness, and the works smell exactly like what they are.

== stop the-list
Over the river to Jones's grocery on South Water Street. His November 1833 notice offered sugar and most kinds of provisions at the lowest prices; whether he kept soap or candles it does not say. Only the street is evidence. Candles mean fire, and in a town of wooden houses packed close the trustees know it: their first ordinance, in 1833, forbade a stovepipe through a roof or wall 'unless guarded by tin or sheet iron, under penalty of $5'. A careless candle in a crowded room can burn a block.

== stop lake-street
Up to Lake Street and the two-storey frame store Thomas Church built, said to be the first store building on the street. Church came in 1834, the year business 'began to extend to Lake street', and became one of the town's leading citizens. One undated sentence is all the evidence, and it names no stock, so nothing is bought here. Shift the bundle and count what you carry.

== stop home
Home to the log house behind Peck's store, which Andreas names as Mrs Rufus Brown's, one of the first-class boarding houses. In 1834, he writes, such houses were always full, three in a bed sometimes, with the floor covered besides: bodies, breath and wet wool all winter in a log room. Yet Moses and Kirkland insist the snowed-in months were 'perhaps, the most enjoyable of the entire year'. Perhaps it depended on whose bed you shared.

== ending an-armful
Soap and candles both, carried home to the log house behind Peck's. The whole list is done, whichever thing stood at the top of it. Let the winter come, and the lodgers with it.

== ending candle-lit
Candles at the top of the list, and candles carried home. Tonight the crowded room behind Peck's has light to read and mend by, and to see who is sharing your blanket. The dark comes a little later.

== ending washing-day
Soap at the top of the list, and soap carried home. The shirts can wait no longer, and neither can the lodger who sleeps beside you. Now they need not.

== ending the-other-errand
You set out for one thing and carried home the other. It will be wanted soon enough, and the list keeps until next time, though the winter will not wait for it.
