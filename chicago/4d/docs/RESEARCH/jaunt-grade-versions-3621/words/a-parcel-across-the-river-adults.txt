== opening
JAUNT: A PARCEL ACROSS THE RIVER. A summer morning in 1835, and you are thirteen, the Sauganash's errand runner, paid in scraps and the odd coin. A traveller who slept here last night has gone over the river and left his parcel behind. He told the kitchen which tavern he was making for. The kitchen has forgotten, and the parcel is your problem now.

Carry it over the South Branch and find him. The town, its taverns and its bridge are as the accounts give them; the runner is invented.

== stop sauganash
The Sauganash, south-east corner of Lake and Market, on the east bank of the South Branch. Wau-Bun's 'pretentious white two-story building, with bright-blue wooden shutters', and in June the Democrat announced it repaired, fitted up and furnished with neatness and taste. The parcel is tied with string and heavier than a parcel ought to be. Over the river stand two taverns, and the traveller is at one of them. Guess.

== stop green-tree
Over the bridge to the Green Tree, Lake and West Water. James Kinzie put it up in 1833, the first building in the place raised to be a hotel; Chester Ingersoll keeps it. It is named, Moses and Kirkland say, 'from an oak tree standing close by', 'one of the most noted in those early days'. It will outlast nearly everything around it: in 1880 it is carted to 33-37 Milwaukee Avenue, 'where it still stands, the oldest memento of early taverns'. Ask at the door for your man, then back to the bridge.

== stop wolf-point
Up to Wolf Point, the west bank where the North and South Branches meet and run east to the lake as one. The tavern is half log, half frame; Lieutenant Allen made its sign out of a piece of a box and painted a wolf on it. As to who keeps it, the accounts cannot agree: one has William Walters behind the bar from 1833 to 1836, the hotel chronology has it closed as a public house since 1834. Whoever answers the door, ask for your man, then back to the bridge.

== stop the-crossing
Back on the South Branch bridge, between Lake and Randolph. The old settlers who crossed it remembered log abutments and two bents, ten feet wide and six feet above the water, so that teams drove under it on the winter ice. Moses and Kirkland have it 'at first' as 'floating logs tied together', built by Alanson and Charles Taylor on a subscription of $486.20, 'of which the Indians of the vicinity contributed $200'. Both cannot be right; the record goes with the fixed bridge. The parcel is delivered. Back to work, or a look at the draw?

== stop back-at-the-sauganash
Back at the Sauganash, 'the most attractive hostlery the city possessed', Moses and Kirkland say, kept by a landlord 'with his merry fiddle, the most popular of hosts'. The parcel is off your hands, the morning is not half gone, and the kitchen is already shouting for you.

== stop the-draw
East along the river to the foot of Dearborn Street and the town's first drawbridge over the main river. Nelson Norton, ship-carpenter, cut the lumber through the winter of 1833-34 and began building in March 1834; the Trustees accepted it that August. Andreas: 'about three hundred feet long, and the opening for the passage of craft about sixty feet', on the 'gallows pattern', a frame at either end of the draw. Watch it a minute, then run, before the kitchen notices.

== ending green-tree-and-home
The Green Tree, first guess. The traveller paid you a penny for his parcel, which is more than the kitchen pays. You ran straight back over the bridge to three more errands.

== ending green-tree-and-draw
The Green Tree, first guess, and a penny for it. Then a few stolen minutes at the drawbridge, and the kitchen at the Sauganash noticed. The penny will not cover what they say to you.

== ending wolf-point-and-home
Wolf Point, and the traveller was glad of his parcel. You ran back over the bridge and had the morning's errands done before noon, with nothing to show for it but sore feet.

== ending wolf-point-and-draw
Wolf Point, and the parcel is his again. On the way home you watched the drawbridge far longer than you meant to. The kitchen has been shouting for you for an hour.
