== premise
Establish your orientation, secure accommodation and identify a practical next step on an imagined first day in town.

== opening
JAUNT: NEW IN CHICAGO. This is your first morning in a town incorporated less than two years ago, when it enumerated twenty-eight legal voters; this summer it supports two newspapers, a federal land office and considerably more strangers than beds, and you are among them.

Five stops lead from the forks to South Water Street: a landmark for navigation, the original mail corner, the store everyone patronizes, a map of the town, and accommodation for tonight, should any be obtainable. The houses of 1834 "were always full", and full meant three in a bed.

== stop arrival
Commence outside the Sauganash. Recollecting 1831, Juliette Kinzie described a white two-story house with blue shutters. Moses and Kirkland characterize it as "the most attractive hostlery the city possessed", and its proprietor Mark Beaubien, "with his merry fiddle, the most popular of hosts". Every newcomer learns this intersection before any other.

== narrative arrival
Let that landmark serve as your point of departure: while you establish your orientation, the town is accommodating you.

== stop mail-corner
This is the original mail corner. John S. C. Hogan, appointed postmaster in 1832, distributed correspondence "in a room partitioned off for that purpose" within his log store. Andreas reports that the office relocated around July 1834, and Hogan's advertisement now offers merchandise "one door below the Post Office". Request your letters there rather than here.

== narrative mail-corner
Retain the original corner as a navigational landmark, but do not confuse it with the town's current mail counter.

== stop store
At Peck's store, on South Water at LaSalle, identify a dependable supply establishment. It was the village's first two-story frame structure, constructed of black walnut lumber "brought from Detroit by a boat". His advertisements enumerate dry goods, hardware, groceries, crockery, boots, shoes and hats. Upstairs, Peck furnished the young minister Jeremiah Porter "a lodging place and study", an early instance of commerce sheltering religion.

== narrative store
Remember where everyday necessities are available.

== stop newspaper
This building retains the Democrat's original address, although by May 1835 the newspaper was printing above Jones and King's hardware store. That spring it continued publishing Kinzie and Forsyth's notice of lithographed town maps, which were likewise obtainable at the newspaper's office.

== narrative newspaper
A town map constitutes a practical next step, so examine the notice attentively.

== stop lodging
Conclude at Brown's boarding house. Andreas identifies Mrs Rufus Brown as a boarding-house keeper and situates her log house behind Peck's store, which you passed a block ago. He reports that the houses of 1834 "were always full; and full meant three in a bed sometimes, with the floor covered besides". A space on the floor qualifies, under such circumstances, as a bed.

== ending settled-by-cost
Your route note concludes with weekly board behind Peck's, so economy has determined the matter. You may share the floor, but you possess an address in the most rapidly expanding town in the West.

== ending settled-by-convenience
Your route note concludes with a bed several steps from the stores, so convenience has determined the matter, and tomorrow commences proximate to everything.

== ending finding-your-feet
You have accumulated several names and corners to connect. Every early settler in this town began precisely here: newly arrived, mud-spattered and searching for accommodation.
