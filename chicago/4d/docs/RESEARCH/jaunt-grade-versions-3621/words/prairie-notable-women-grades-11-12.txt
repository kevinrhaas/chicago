== premise
A spring morning walk past six residences associated with women who transformed Chicago: nursing reform, forensic science, a forty-year journal, suffrage, a celebrated fountain and the avenue's final great lady.

== opening
JAUNT: WOMEN OF PRAIRIE AVENUE. A spring morning, 1904. The men of this avenue have departed for their department stores, banks and packing houses downtown, and their names adorn everything. Their wives, sisters and daughters are prohibited from voting for president, and will remain so for another nine years.

They are productive regardless. Six residences on foot, from 1608 to 2115 Prairie, proceeding south, and at each a woman whom the documentary sources associate with the house: an advocate for professional nursing, a future forensic investigator, a diarist with a silversmith's bench in her basement, a suffragist, a philanthropist who will commission a fountain, and the avenue's last great lady.

Commence at the northern end, with two sisters who entered an institution that had never admitted anyone of their background.

== stop frank
1608 Prairie, the Frank household. Henrietta Greenebaum Frank campaigned persistently for improved nursing education at the Illinois Training School for Nurses. In 1876 she and her sister Hannah Greenebaum Solomon became the first Jewish members of the prestigious Chicago Woman's Club. Hannah subsequently founded the National Council of Jewish Women and served as its inaugural president. Two sisters, one previously closed door opened. A block south, a young mother inhabits a residence her parents constructed for her.

== stop lee
1700 Prairie, constructed by John and Frances Glessner for their daughter Frances, who married Blewett Lee in 1898. At present she is a conventional society wife. Be patient. Decades hence she will construct eighteen miniature, meticulously accurate rooms, each a death scene for students to analyze: the Nutshell Studies of Unexplained Death, which still train professional investigators. She will help establish Harvard's Department of Legal Medicine, and in 1943 she will become the first woman police captain in the United States. Her mother resides one block south, and is probably documenting her daughter in a journal at this very moment.

== stop glessner
1800 Prairie, Glessner House. Frances Macbeth Glessner commenced a journal in 1879 and maintained it for forty years, and the house museum still reconstructs the family's history from its pages. She kept bees. Since 1893 her Monday Morning Reading Class has convened in the library, women reading collectively every week throughout winter and spring. Moreover, this year, 1904, she has installed a silversmith's workbench in the basement and begun fabricating silver with her own hands. Across Eighteenth Street and along the western sidewalk, a neighbor is campaigning for something considerably larger.

== stop jones
1834 Prairie, the Fernando Jones residence. Jane Grahame Jones was, according to the city's encyclopedia, a longtime and outspoken activist for woman suffrage; her husband's scrapbook preserves clippings concerning the Illinois Woman Suffrage Association from as early as 1875. She has persisted for nearly thirty years and remains disenfranchised. In 1913 Illinois will become the first state east of the Mississippi to permit women to vote for president, a victory achieved through decades of precisely such unglamorous persistence. The residence will be demolished in 1942. Continue south, beyond Twentieth Street, to a family that will bestow a fountain upon the city.

== stop buckingham
2036 Prairie, the Buckingham residence, where the 1904 Blue Book lists Clarence Buckingham, philanthropist and businessman. His sister was Kate Sturges Buckingham, philanthropist and patron of the arts. In 1927, following his death, she will present Chicago with the Buckingham Fountain in his memory: twice the dimensions of the Versailles fountain it emulates, with a central jet projecting water 150 feet into the air. One residence remains, and the woman who persisted after everyone else had departed.

== stop armour
2115 Prairie, the Kelley-Armour residence. The 1904 Blue Book lists Mrs. Philip D. Armour here; the census of 1900 records her as Belle, born in Ohio. Her husband, the meatpacker, died in 1901. As the great families died or relocated to suburbs such as Lake Forest, she remained, year after year. The district marker records that Armour's widow was among the avenue's final residents, dying here in 1927, long after most of her neighbors' residences had been sold or demolished. Her own residence will follow in 1937.

== ending finish
The walk concludes at 2115 Prairie. Six women, six residences: an advocate for nursing, a forensic pioneer, a diarist, a suffragist, a philanthropist and a widow who declined to leave.

They are merely the individuals the surviving sources identify. Behind every one of these façades lived maids, cooks, nurses and laundresses whose names survive exclusively in census enumerations. Their histories await recovery as well, and recovering them is part of the historian's responsibility.
