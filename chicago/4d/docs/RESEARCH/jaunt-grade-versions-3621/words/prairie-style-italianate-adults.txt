== opening
JAUNT: ITALIANATE STYLE. An autumn morning, 1904. Before the mansards and the granite, Prairie Avenue was built Italianate: houses dressed as villas from a Tuscan hillside, the fashion of the 1860s, when the first big houses went up on this street. A villa on a city lot is a costume, and a costly one. The round-headed windows, the carved brackets and the high stoop all say the same thing: the man inside has arrived.

Five houses on foot, 1612 to 2031 Prairie, from the street's oldest generation. Four of the five will be torn down, and the street they stand on will be given over to offices, warehouses and factories. See them while they are still somebody's home.

Start at 1612, with the best Italianate front on the street.

== stop goodman
1612 Prairie, Joseph Goodman's house of 1869, two years before the Great Fire. Its photograph shows the Italianate formula done in stone: three bays, every window round-headed under a long keystone, a belt course marking the principal floor, and an arched portico on columns at the top of a high stoop. The stoop is a social instrument. A caller climbs well above the street and the dust of its carriages, and waits on display, in full view of the house, before the door ever opens. The house stands until it is razed around 1960. Walk south a block and the same year's style gets a famous architect.

== stop hibbard
1701 Prairie, William G. Hibbard's house, also of 1869, designed by William Le Baron Jenney for the president of a large wholesale hardware firm. The district register reads it as Italianate: a low hipped roof on bracketed eaves over a stone-based body, with a rear range added in 1874. Hardware paid for it, and Jenney would go on to build the first skyscraper. Its last years are the sad part. In 1921 the American Terra Cotta and Ceramic Company bought it and gave the architect Louis Sullivan free office space here until his death in 1924. A merchant's mansion ended as a free desk for an old architect. It is razed in 1932. Two doors south is a rarer thing: an Italianate house of wood.

== stop walker
1720 Prairie, the Tyrrell and Walker house, read by the district register as an Italianate house built of wood: a low hip on deep bracketed eaves, a central gable, a canted bay and a full-width porch. Timber dressed up as a villa, among the brick and stone fronts crowding it. The street is already turning. In 1910 wreckers will destroy it, the first of many Prairie Avenue houses torn down, as homes give way to offices, warehouses and factories. The money that built this street will not stay on it. Compare its timber brackets with the stone ones around it, then cross Eighteenth Street to a house that changed its clothes.

== stop jones
1834 Prairie, Fernando Jones's house, by John M. Van Osdel, built in 1866 for 'one of Chicago's earliest pioneers - Fernando Jones'. It was built Italianate. Twenty years later the whole street had gone mansard, and an old settler's house was starting to look like an old settler. So in 1886 Jones gave it a mansard addition 'to bring it into fashion with many of its neighbors'. On this street keeping up was not vanity; it was standing. Here is an Italianate house in a newer hat, worn until it is razed in 1942. One more stop, past Twentieth.

== stop tolman
2031 Prairie, the north house of a row of three built in 1869 with pale Lemont limestone fronts. The district register reads it as Italianate: a flat roof behind a bracketed cornice over three storeys. A row is a palace shared. Three households stand under one cornice line, so that together they look grander than any one of them could alone. But each keeps its own door, and its own address. Look for where the brackets stop and start again.

== ending finish
End of the Italianate walk. Count the brackets you passed: these are the street's oldest fronts, built before the fire and the fortunes that came after it.

By 1904 they already look old-fashioned beside the mansards and the granite, and within a lifetime most of them will be gone, the avenue handed over to warehouses and factories. Walk the Second Empire or Romanesque routes to see what the next fortunes built.
