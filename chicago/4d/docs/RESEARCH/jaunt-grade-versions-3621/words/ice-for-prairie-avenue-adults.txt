== opening
JAUNT: ICE FOR PRAIRIE AVENUE. A Monday morning in October 1904, and you are thirteen, riding the back of an ice wagon down Prairie Avenue. The iceman lifts the blocks; you do everything else, and you are paid accordingly. Your job this morning is to find the way in at houses built so that people like you are never seen coming in.

Every family here has a door of its own. The ice has another, and so do the cook, the maids and the boy from the wagon. The first house is the granite one at the corner of Eighteenth Street. Find your door before the ice drips. The houses and the people in them are real; the iceman's helper is you.

== stop the-corner
Here at the corner of Prairie Avenue and Eighteenth Street, John Glessner built his house diagonally opposite Pullman's mansion and across the street from W. W. Kimball's smaller French chateau. Its outside walls are Wellesley granite, lined with hollow brick, so there is no place for a mouse to live, and no easy way for anyone else either. Passers-by remark on the narrow slots of windows along Eighteenth Street; the generous windows face the private courtyard. From the street it is a fortress, and it means to be one.

== stop front-door
The door on Prairie Avenue is the family's, and not one you will ever use. Henry Hobson Richardson designed this house, his last work, and the Glessners have slept here since 1 December 1887. It is Monday: every Monday from October to May Mrs. Glessner's Reading Class meets for two hours in the library, by invitation only, the ladies sewing while a professional reader reads to them. Their carriages will draw up here soon, and a boy with dripping ice is the last thing this doorstep is for. An underpass runs beside you into the courtyard. Go round.

== stop the-gates
Round the back, off the alley, are the courtyard gates. Through them, on 1 December 1887, the Glessners moved every belonging they had 'before our neighbors suspected even that we were about to move', and lit the new hearth that night with fire carried from the old house. The plan let even its owners move in unseen. Above the court the dovecotes hold nearly sixty doves, and their eggs go to the family's table. Even here the ice stops short: the service entrance is round on Eighteenth Street.

== stop the-arch
Under the great arch on Eighteenth Street is the service entrance, with a separate address, 35 Eighteenth Street, so the staff do not even share the family's number. A small corridor runs to the kitchen; its window sits in the arch. The ice never comes indoors: it goes through a half-door into the cold closet, and the iceman never sets foot inside. An annunciator tells the kitchen which room has rung, and the staff eat at about three so the kitchen can turn to the family's dinner. There is one cook, Mattie Williamson, since 1892, when Mrs. Glessner dismissed her seventh cook in seven weeks.

== stop the-stable
The wagon waits by the stable's carriage doorway on Eighteenth Street, where the iceman can water his horse. Six stalls, room for as many carriages, all of it white glazed brick, and the furnace room underneath so that the dust, dirt and noise of coal and ashes never reach the family. Somebody shovels that coal. Who drives for the Glessners now is not recorded: their coachman of twenty-four years left in 1902. In two years the horses will go for motor cars, and the wall between stable and carriage house will come down.

== stop kimball
Across Prairie Avenue at 1801 is W. W. Kimball's house, built in 1890 to Solon S. Beman's design in the French Chateauesque style, a very steep slate roof and a round tower under a slate cone. Kimball began in Chicago in 1857, 'beginning with only four pianos'; by 1881 his factory was 'churning out around 100 pianos and organs every week'. He has two months to live: he 'died at his home in Chicago on December 16, 1904'. The back way in will be just as hard to find.

== ending a-regular
The back way first, the kitchen's order taken, the horse watered: the iceman says you will do, which is as near to praise as you will get. Next Monday you will go round by the alley without being told. The kitchen will learn your face at the half-door; upstairs, nobody ever will.

== ending ahead-of-the-wagon
You found the Glessners' back way on your own, and you are across Prairie Avenue ahead of the wagon, hunting for the Kimballs'. The iceman will not say he is pleased. Every house on this street has a door for the family and another for everything that serves it, and you are learning them one alley at a time.

== ending round-the-long-way
You learned the hard way that the big door on Prairie Avenue is not for the likes of you, but you learned it before the Reading Class arrived. The kitchen's order is in your pocket and the horse is watered. Next Monday you go round by Eighteenth Street, and you never knock at the front again.

== ending two-lessons
Two lessons before ten o'clock: the front door is the family's, and a wagon waits for nobody. You are across Prairie Avenue looking for the Kimballs' back way while the iceman whistles for you. Tomorrow the round starts again, and the doors on this street will not get any easier to find.
