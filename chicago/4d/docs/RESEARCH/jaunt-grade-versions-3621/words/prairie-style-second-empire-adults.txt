== opening
JAUNT: SECOND EMPIRE STYLE. A spring sunset, 1904. The mansard roof came from Paris: a steep slope that hides a whole extra storey, crowned with iron cresting and pierced with dormers. By the 1880s it dominated the street, and the Glessner House Museum calls it Prairie Avenue's most popular style. It was the roof of the street's biggest fortunes, and of everyone who wanted to be taken for one.

Six houses on foot, 1620 to 2115 Prairie, north to south: a pioneer's roof that swells into a crown, Pullman's palace, a house that bought a mansard to keep up with the neighbours, the street's last survivor, the richest man in Chicago, and the meatpacker's widow. Watch the sunset catch the slate.

Start at 1620, with a roof drawn for a magazine in 1874.

== stop law
1620 Prairie, Robert Law's house, engraved in 1874 for a series on the homes of Chicago: two storeys over a raised basement under a slated mansard with round-headed dormers, its north part rising into a taller curved crown. To be engraved was its own kind of status. A house drawn for the public was a house meant to be envied. Compare the drawing's roof with the one in front of you, then walk a block south to the grandest mansard on the street.

== stop pullman
1729 Prairie, George Pullman's house of 1870 to 1876, read by the district register as a brownstone block under a mansard with dormers and iron cresting, entered by a portico on Prairie Avenue. Inside: a theatre of about two hundred seats, a pipe organ and a bowling alley, and later a palm room and a large conservatory by Solon S. Beman. Pullman died in 1897. His widow still keeps it, a theatre and a bowling alley for one widow. It is razed in 1922. Across Eighteenth Street, a smaller house that refused to be left behind.

== stop jones
1834 Prairie, Fernando Jones's house, built in 1866 as an Italianate house with a low roof. Twenty years later his neighbours all had mansards, and a low roof on this street read as a man falling behind. In 1886 the house 'received a mansard addition to bring it into fashion with many of its neighbors': a whole new top storey, bought to keep up. That is how a style takes over a street: not by taste, but by fear of looking poorer. The hat comes off for good in 1942, when the house is razed. Two doors south stands the one Second Empire house that will outlive them all.

== stop e-keith
1900 Prairie, Elbridge G. Keith's house of 1870, by John W. Roberts. The district marker calls it 'The last surviving example of the popular Second Empire style, which dominated the street by the 1880s.' Keith was a bank president. Survival on this street will be luck, not grandeur: the palaces around it come down, and the banker's house is simply left standing. Look for the slate mansard over the corner bay, then cross the avenue to the biggest fortune of all.

== stop field
1905 Prairie, Marshall Field's house, by Richard Morris Hunt, read by the district register as a broad brick and stone mansion under a mansard, with an angular corner bay, dormers and a central portico. Field, 'the wealthiest and most powerful businessman in Chicago', lives here; in 1905 his fortune will be valued at $125 million. The money will not protect the family. On 22 November 1905 his son, Marshall Field Jr., will be shot, and on 16 January 1906 Field himself will die of pneumonia, caught after a New Year's Day golf game. The house will be razed in 1955. One more mansard, at the far end of the row.

== stop armour
2115 Prairie, the Kelley-Armour house of 1871, which the district marker calls a stately Second Empire residence: a patterned slate mansard with cresting over a central arched doorway under a pediment. Built for the lumberman David Kelley, it was for nearly 50 years the home of the family of the meatpacker Philip D. Armour, who boasted of using 'everything but the squeal'. Slate and cresting, paid for by every other part of the pig. His widow still lives here and will be among the street's last residents, dying in 1927. The house is razed ten years after her, in 1937.

== ending finish
End of the Second Empire walk. Count the mansards you passed: by the 1880s they lined the street, and even old houses bought them to keep up.

Then taste changed, the families moved north, and most of these roofs came down. The theatre, the bowling alley, the richest man's portico: all gone. Only Elbridge Keith's survives. Go back and look at it again before the light goes.
