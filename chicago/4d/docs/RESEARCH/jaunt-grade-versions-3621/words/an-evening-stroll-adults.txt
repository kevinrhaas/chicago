== opening
JAUNT: AN EVENING STROLL. Dusk on 1 July 1835. The land buyers have gone in to supper and to drink, the river has gone quiet, and for once there is nothing you need to do. Chicago is not two years old as a town: when it was incorporated in August 1833, only 'thirteen votes were cast by the twenty-eight legal electors', twelve for and one against. Now strangers arrive by the boatload, most of them in a hurry to get rich, and the few dozen who decided what this town would be are already outnumbered.

Take a slow circuit of the familiar streets: from the Sauganash, past the village's first frame store and the corner where its first newspaper was printed, to the hotel where the town's party men meet. Then decide where the evening ends.

== stop the-sauganash
Begin at the Sauganash, on the corner of Lake and Market above the South Branch, white and two-storey with bright-blue shutters and newly fitted up this June. Mark Beaubien built it, and he has made a living from every part of this town's life and death. Moses and Kirkland call him 'the fun of every funeral and the life of every wake', and list him 'As fiddler, landlord, merchant, ferryman and a patron of horse racing'. The same man kept the bar, played for the dancing and lent his cabin to the Church: in May 1833 Father St. Cyr 'celebrated his first mass in a log cabin belonging to Mark Beaubien'. Sin and sacrament under one landlord.

== stop the-shopfront
Over to the river and east along South Water Street to LaSalle and P. F. W. Peck's store, the first two-storey frame in the village, built of lumber 'sawed from black walnut trees'. Peck sells dry goods, hardware, groceries, crockery, boots, shoes and hats, and this year he married Mary K. Wythe of Philadelphia and joined the Pioneer Hook and Ladder Company: a man settling down in a town that burns. Years from now he will be credited 'with having $40,000 in cash locked in an iron safe in his dwelling', and thought 'nearly as wealthy as John Jacob Astor'. Fortunes here are made quietly, behind a counter, and kept under lock.

== stop the-printing-office
One block east, at South Water and Clark, John Calhoun began printing the Chicago Democrat in 1833; by 20 May its office was over Jones & King's hardware along the street. It comes out on Wednesdays, and today's is No. 11 of its second volume. When the mail comes in, 'often irregular and long delayed', the town gets its news the loud way: 'some one with a good voice would mount a dry goods box' and read it out, and 'If politics ran high, two dry-goods boxes and two readers of opposite parties would be utilized.' Two men shouting the same news with opposite meanings, and a crowd taking sides: politics has not changed much.

== stop the-exchange
South to Lake Street and west to Wells, and the Exchange Coffee House, another house Mark Beaubien built, kept as a hotel since August 1834 by Mr. and Mrs. Murphy. The town's Democrats met here in April and again in June to choose their candidates for the town election, and last winter a dancing master took his pupils' addresses here: politics and courtship from the same front desk. Choose where the evening ends.

== ending at-the-exchange
You end the stroll at the corner of Lake and Wells in the last of the light, having passed the village's first frame store, its first newspaper's corner and two of Mark Beaubien's houses. Inside, men are deciding the next election over a glass. Not bad for a town that is not two years old, and not innocent either.

== ending where-you-began
You walk back west along Lake Street to the Sauganash and close the circuit where it began. If the windows are open you may hear the fiddle, and the laughter of men who have made money today, and the quieter ones who have lost it.
