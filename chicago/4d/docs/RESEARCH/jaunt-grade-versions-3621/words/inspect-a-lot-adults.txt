== opening
JAUNT: LOOK BEFORE YOU BUY A LOT. Chicago in the summer of 1835 has gone land mad. 'The opening of a land office in May, 1835, added fuel to the already brilliant blaze of excitement'. 'Lots which had been sold in 1830 for from $10 to $50 soon came to be worth $200, and when the craze came on in 1835-6 brought twenty or thirty times as much.' In the summer of 1834 part of the town sold for $20,000; Charles Butler, who had declined to join that deal, bought the same ground this May, 'paying therefore $100,000'. Fortunes are being made on paper, and paper is all most of them are.

'The favorite way of making sales was at auction.' A Black crier in gaudy colours, with a scarlet flag, rides a white horse through town announcing the hour of the sale, and 'Crowds flocked around and followed him and hung upon his entrancing words.' Then 'there were no bounds to which enthusiastic, nay, mad bidders would not go.' Before you join them, look at what is really for sale: the room, the notices, the corner and the ground itself.

== stop auction
Start on Dearborn Street at John Bates Jr.'s new auction room. On 12 and 13 June the Democrat's notice put up fifty town lots of Marseilles here, with fifty more to follow, and Marseilles is another town altogether, somewhere you have never seen. In this boom, Moses and Kirkland say, 'The whole land seemed staked out and peopled on paper', and 'But few of these paper towns ever came to anything'. Men are paying real money in this room for streets that exist only as ink on a surveyor's plat. Read the notice again.

== stop notice
West to Clark. The Democrat was printed at this corner from 1833; by 20 May its office had moved over Jones & King's hardware. Your copy carries two land notices: E. K. Hubbard's lots in the School Section and the original town, and J. W. Fell's land on the canal route, and neither names a price, which tells you something. Prices are wild. This spring Gurdon Hubbard and his partners bought eighty acres for $5,000, platted them in New York and 'sold half the lots at auction for $80,000'. Sixteen times the money, for half the land.

== stop corner
On west to LaSalle, where Peck's two-storey frame store holds the corner his own advertisements name. A lot is bought by its corners and street lines, so take your bearings: the river to the north, the town's lots running south. Philo Carpenter, who came in 1832, paid for 'two lots on South Water street, forty feet frontage, at a cost of $75'. In the craze, 'Lots were frequently sold and resold, always bringing increased prices': 'Lot 1, block 2, sold in June for $5,000 and resold in August for $10,000'. A lot that doubles in two months is not being bought to build on. Each buyer is betting on the next.

== stop ground
A few steps east, a small drain runs under South Water to the river, and the street crosses it on logs. An account of the early town calls the South Division generally low and marshy, wetter still along the river, covered with tall grass, reeds and rushes. The schoolmaster Granville Sproat remembered mud 'half a leg deep, up to the hubs of the carts and wagons', and carrying the smaller scholars on his back; once he fell into the mire with a child in his arms. This is the ground under the $10,000 lots. Decide what it tells you.

== ending inquire-town
You will ask after one particular lot from Hubbard's notice and stand on its own ground before paying. In a town where lots double between June and August, that makes you the rarest buyer in Chicago, and possibly the only one who will still own anything in two years.

== ending inquire-canal
You will apply at the New York House, as Fell's notice asks, for the terms of one parcel on the canal route, and walk it before you pay. The canal is coming, everyone says. Make sure the land is too, and that the man selling it owns it.

== ending held
You keep your money. A notice, a sale room and a wet street have told you enough for one day. Remember it when the bubble bursts: 'The financial reverses of 1836 caused every buyer, except Mr. Peck, to forfeit his purchases' at the first canal-lot sale. Men who were rich on paper this summer will be ruined in fact.
