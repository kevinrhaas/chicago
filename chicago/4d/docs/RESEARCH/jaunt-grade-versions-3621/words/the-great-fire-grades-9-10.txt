== premise
From a cow barn on Sunday night, 8 October 1871, to the rain on Monday night: twelve stops at their hours, following the Great Chicago Fire across two rivers and through the heart of the city.

== opening
JAUNT: THE GREAT CHICAGO FIRE. Sunday night, 8 October 1871, at the end of a drought. Since the third of July only two and a half inches of rain have fallen on Chicago, and the West Side is a city of wooden cottages, sheds and lumber yards. A strong, steady wind is blowing from the south-west, and the fire department is exhausted: last night its companies fought a large fire near the river, and some of them worked on into this afternoon.

Twelve stops follow the fire hour by hour, from a cow barn on the West Side, across two rivers, to the far edge of the North Side, through the night and the smoky day that followed. Long rides go Instantly; every place keeps its stop.

It begins in a barn on DeKoven Street, at about nine o'clock.

== stop oleary-barn
Sunday 8 October 1871, about nine at night, on DeKoven Street. Behind the cottage at No. 137 stands the barn of Patrick and Catherine O'Leary, who keep cows and sell their milk to neighbouring families; its loft "had recently been filled with hay". According to legend, Mrs. O'Leary was milking when her cow kicked over a lamp. The inquiry, however, found the O'Learys in bed, and "no proof that any person had been in the barn after nightfall that evening." Daniel Sullivan, a drayman across the street, saw the flames; although "he has a wooden leg", he ran to the barn and dragged out a calf. William Lee, who lives two doors along, races to the drug store for the key to the alarm box, and the druggist refuses him. "The clock struck nine." Across the river, the watchman in the Court House tower has not yet seen the light.

== stop st-pauls
Half past ten, at Clinton and Mather Streets. The alarm came late, and it was misdirected. At 9:28 Mathias Schaffer, the watchman in the Court House tower, located the glow through his spy-glass and signalled Box 342, which was "nearly a mile from the fire". One engine, the Little Giant, arrived first: "For more than thirty minutes this engine battled alone." Then the gale lifted the fire over the firemen's heads. Richard Riley, a railroad contractor standing on a lumber pile, watched "A large mass of fire" go "whirling through the air for more than three blocks" and land on the cupola of this church, St. Paul's. The church is down, and behind it William B. Bateham's mills, with a thousand cords of kindling, are burning too. Bateham, an alderman, turns: "The materials from this mill will fire the South Side, and nothing can prevent it now."

== stop parmelee
Half past eleven, at Jackson and Franklin Streets, across the South Branch of the river. Since half past nine burning brands have been raining down on this side, and now one has caught. This is the new stable of the Parmelee Omnibus and Stage Company, "the finest of its kind in the country", completed at a cost of $80,000 and due to be "occupied for the first time on Wednesday". At the stable, the fire "swept down upon them with such rapidity and terrific force as to compel them to flee for their lives." By midnight the gas works on Adams Street are ablaze. Beyond stand the stone and marble blocks of LaSalle Street, a double row of supposedly "fire-proof" buildings. One writer asked: "Would these succumb to the shower of brands and the triple-heated furnace which had been thrown about them?"

== stop court-house
Half past one on Monday morning, in the Court House square at the centre of the city. Mathias Schaffer, the watchman whose mistaken alarm sent the engines astray, remains at his post in the tower. Since ten o'clock sparks have been falling on the roof; it has caught "five or six times", and each time he has smothered it with two Babcock fire-extinguishers. In the basement, Mayor Roswell B. Mason has been issuing orders since midnight: gunpowder to blow up buildings in the fire's path, and the jail to be emptied. Captain Hickey has had the cells unlocked, and the prisoners "rushed into the street"; the men accused of murder leave in handcuffs. Now the tower itself is alight. Schaffer and his relief, Dennis Denene, set the great bell swinging and run for the stairs. It weighs 10,849 pounds, and it is still ringing.

== stop sherman-house
Five past two, at the corner of Clark and Randolph. Behind you the Court House bell comes "crashing and clanging down", and Mayor Mason, who stayed until it fell, emerges to find LaSalle Street "like a furnace, all fire". Across Clark Street rises the Sherman House, seven stories high, with more than three hundred guests. Hours ago, men were tearing down its awnings to save it. A reporter for the Post watched the result: "for nearly an hour the house held good. Suddenly, a spirit of flame came from a window in the third story on the southern face". Only the night clerk and one assistant remain to wake every room, and five ladies are lying sick in bed. North of the hotel flows the main river, the last natural barrier between the fire and the North Side. The wind is already carrying the flames across.

== stop crosbys
Twenty past two, on Washington Street between Dearborn and State. From the Court House the fire has swung east. This is Crosby's Opera House, opened in 1865, with seats for 2,500; all summer it stood closed while $80,000 was spent on ornament, upholstery and frescoes. It was to reopen tomorrow night with Theodore Thomas and his orchestra, and tonight, "only an hour or two before the fire, it was lit up for the first time, that its effects might be seen by gaslight." Now the roof ignites; "In almost the time it takes to write these lines," wrote James Sheahan and George Upton, two Chicago newspapermen, the most elegant auditorium in the country is destroyed. The St. James Hotel next door, which "had two or three times before stood the test of fire", collapses with it. And on the far side of the main river, the North Side has caught.

== stop north-side
Half past two, north of the main river, where the State Street viaduct descends to Kinzie Street. The river did not stop it: "About the time that the Sherman House was ablaze", the wind carried the fire across the water, and it "broke out just north of the bridge, at State street." Now Wright's stable, on this corner, is burning; an hour earlier Judge Lambert Tree crossed the bridge when it "was then burning in spots". Mayor Mason and his son, cut off on the South Side, are circling round by the Wells Street bridge, hoping to get ahead of the fire, and will discover it here before them. North of this corner most families are asleep; many will wake to find themselves "surrounded by fire." Half a mile away on the lake shore stands the single building that pumps the water for the entire city.

== stop water-works
Twenty past three, Chicago Avenue at the lake shore. These are the Water Works: the engine house, and a hundred and fifty feet west of it the Water Tower, a stone shell around an iron column 130 feet high, up which the pumps force water for the whole city. The engineer, Frank Trautman, has his engines running at thirty million gallons a day. D. W. Fuller sees "a fire-brand, apparently twelve feet in length," spinning toward the tower until it strikes the corner of the engine house. "In an instant, the roof was aflame." The roof is wooden; Fuller checks: "exactly twenty minutes past three." A few minutes before four, the heat drives the men out. The tower will survive; the engines will not. From now on the firemen can fight only where they can draw water from the river.

== stop tribune
Half past seven on Monday morning, at Dearborn and Madison Streets, in a smoky daylight. On this corner stands the Tribune building, which its owners believe is fireproof. At daybreak William Bross, a former lieutenant governor of Illinois and one of the newspaper's owners, rode here and "found everything safe". When fire crept beneath the sidewalk into the barber's shop in the basement, Bross extinguished it with his partners, Joseph Medill, one of the chief stockholders, and Horace White, the editor, "with water and one of Babcock's fire-extinguishers." The printing forms went downstairs for the morning edition. Then the fire returned from the south, past the enormous Palmer House, to McVicker's Theatre across a narrow alley. "The iron shutters on the alley side, bent by the fire, sprung out of place". The Tribune is lost; with the Water Works gone nothing can halt what comes next.

== stop state-street
Eight o'clock, State Street at Washington. This store of white Massachusetts marble belongs to Potter Palmer, the developer who built the Palmer House hotel a few blocks south, and he leases it to Field, Leiter and Co., importers of dry goods, for $52,000 a year. At first light the firm's own employees were "showering that massive pile of pure marble and iron with water from their own pumping engines." Now the streams "had ceased to run", because the Water Works have failed, and men are hauling merchandise from the lower floor into waiting wagons. The fire attacks "from the north and eastwardly in the rear, and it soon yielded." Farther down State Street the Palmer House is already a ruin, and on the North Side the fire has been advancing since half past two, and nothing has stopped it.

== stop ogden-house
Midday on Monday, on the North Side, in front of the house of Mahlon D. Ogden, brother of William B. Ogden, Chicago's first mayor. It is built of wood, and it is still standing. Before it lies Washington Park, which filled during the night with refugees and their furniture, until the fire swept through and consumed "the accumulated furniture of fifty families." Men inside defended it: they tore up the carpets, spread them across the walls and kept them soaked, and when the hydrant failed, "Several barrels of cider were used to saturate the carpets". Around it now there is "nothing but this solitary building in an area of 2,600 acres." A mile north the fire has reached St. Michael's, the great brick church of the German parish, and its steeple is burning. The people of the North Side are fleeing toward the lake and the old cemetery.

== stop old-cemetery
Nightfall on Monday, at the old City Cemetery on the southern edge of Lincoln Park. Most of its dead were moved years ago, leaving "some old headstones scattered about" among the grass and the small oaks. "By nightfall on Monday there could not have been less than thirty thousand men, women, and children, huddled within this ghostly inclosure." They carried in beds, chairs and bundles, and many have watched their belongings "burn up before their eyes." South along the shore, "Along the beach were judges, merchants, doctors, workingmen, women and children", and "Ladies were seen moving their chairs into the water, and sitting on the top of the backs". Thousands more have escaped west to the open prairie. Here they are "blinded by smoke, weakened by hunger, and choking with thirst", and nobody can predict where the fire will finally stop.

== ending the-rain
Monday night, 9 October 1871. At about half past ten the fire reaches the northern end of Lincoln Park and burns the house of Dr. John H. Foster on Fullerton Avenue, "just twenty-five hours from the commencement of the fire, and about four miles distant from the place of its origin." There it finally "spent itself", and then "About eleven o'clock Monday night, the sky, as if in remorse at what the fiery element had done, poured down floods of rain." In the cemetery and on the prairie the refugees stand in it, drenched. The final count: 2,124 acres burned, 17,450 buildings destroyed, 98,500 people homeless, and "more than 250" dead. The mayor's first proclamation pledged the city's credit "for the necessary expenses for the relief of the suffering", and concluded: "It is believed the fire has spent its force and all will soon be well."
