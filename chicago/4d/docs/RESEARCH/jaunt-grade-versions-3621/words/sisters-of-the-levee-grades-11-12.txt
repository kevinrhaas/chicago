== premise
Three blocks west of the mansions, two sisters from Virginia operate the most notorious establishment in Chicago, appointed with gold cuspidors and a $15,000 piano. Prairie Avenue affects ignorance of the route.

== opening
JAUNT: THE SISTERS OF THE LEVEE. Dusk on a Monday in November 1904, at Eighteenth Street and Prairie Avenue.

Four stops, proceeding west from Millionaire's Row toward the Levee, the city's red-light district, and concluding at a church on its periphery. Prairie Avenue lies three blocks from the most celebrated address in Chicago, and it is not the Glessner House. Two sisters who adopted the surname Everleigh opened a club on Dearborn Street in 1900, and some of the wealthiest men in the world have discovered its entrance.

Begin at the intersection, and observe which direction the cabs turn.

== stop corner
Eighteenth and Prairie, as the gas lamps are illuminated. Eighteenth Street operates as a boundary: northward and eastward stand the mansions; southward and westward, "several blocks away from Prairie Avenue", commences the Levee, the district extending from Eighteenth Street to Twenty-second and from Wabash to Clark, which accommodates the city's most infamous brothels. The ladies of Prairie Avenue never acknowledge its existence. Nevertheless one of the two preeminent establishments in Chicago, Vic Shaw's, operated temporarily on Prairie Avenue itself. The two gentlemen hastening around this corner are not returning home. Follow them westward.

== caption corner
Two gentlemen in evening attire round the corner and proceed briskly west along Eighteenth Street.

== stop sisters
Indiana Avenue, one block west. Continue three blocks farther to Dearborn Street, where at 2131 and 2133 operates the Everleigh Club. Its proprietors are two sisters, Ada and Minna, who claim a Kentucky upbringing, which was "another story concocted by the sisters". They were born Simms, in Virginia, in 1864 and 1866. Utilizing an inheritance of approximately $35,000, they managed an establishment in Omaha before relocating to Chicago and purchasing an existing business on Dearborn. They derived their new surname from their grandmother, who concluded her correspondence "Everly yours". The club opened on 1 February 1900, deliberately refusing numerous callers that first evening. The Everleighs comprehended that in their enterprise, exclusivity established the price.

== caption sisters
A gentleman descends the steps of an Indiana Avenue residence, consults his pocket watch, and turns west toward the Levee.

== stop rooms
Farther south along Indiana, with the Levee two blocks west. The Everleigh Club comprises fifty rooms across two adjoining mansions: a Japanese Throne Room, a Mirror Room, a Silver Parlor, "a $15,000 gold-leafed piano for the Music Room" and "$650 gold cuspidors". Champagne costs $12 a bottle, "in an era when an entire meal could be had for fifty cents", and admission alone requires $50. Ada administers the accounts and recruits the women; Minna receives the clientele. In March 1902 Prince Henry of Prussia visited, and when a dancer's slipper dislodged and spilled champagne, a member of his entourage drank from it, initiating an enduring fashion. The establishment employs fifteen to twenty-five cooks and maids, and its women are designated "butterflies".

== caption rooms
Two maids, released from duty, emerge from the side passage onto Indiana Avenue and hurry northward, conversing animatedly.

== stop church
First Presbyterian Church, at Indiana and Twenty-first Street, positioned at the Levee's eastern perimeter, near enough to hear it on summer evenings. According to persistent accounts, the club's distinguished clientele includes the actor John Barrymore and the authors Ring Lardner and Theodore Dreiser, and gossips append young Marshall Field Jr., who resides several blocks away. Subsequently "a rival madam maliciously accused the Everleigh sisters" of considerably worse, and that rumor proved ineradicable. The minister's entrance is illuminated, as is the club's. Tonight, as every night, the Levee is the more frequented of the two, a juxtaposition that exposes the distance between the city's professed morality and its actual conduct.

South along Indiana Avenue, with the Levee's commotion two blocks westward.

South to Twenty-first Street and the church at the Levee's perimeter.

== ending close
The walk concludes here. The Everleigh Club endured eleven years. On 24 October 1911 Mayor Carter Harrison Jr. ordered its closure, and Minna accepted the decision with characteristic composure: "If the Mayor says we must close, that settles it...I'll close up shop and walk out with a smile on my face". The building was demolished in July 1933. Prairie Avenue never conceded that it knew the route to Dearborn Street; it acknowledged only the route back, a selective memory that respectable society cultivated with remarkable consistency.
