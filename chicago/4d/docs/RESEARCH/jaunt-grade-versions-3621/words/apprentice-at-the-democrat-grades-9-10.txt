== premise
Play the Chicago Democrat's newest apprentice on publication day, 1 July 1835: decide where the paper goes first and where you linger, and return to the office with an account of the morning that is entirely your own.

== opening
JAUNT: APPRENTICE AT THE DEMOCRAT. Wednesday, 1 July 1835, and you are thirteen years old. Six weeks ago the Chicago Democrat printed a notice: 'Wanted immediately, an apprentice at this office to the printing business'. You answered it, and the printing office took you on. Your days are spent sweeping, inking the type and folding the printed sheets; on Wednesdays, when the paper comes off the press, you carry it through a town that grows a little larger every week.

Today's number is folded and tucked under your arm. Where it travels first, and where you allow yourself to linger, is your decision. The places and the newspapers are genuine; the apprentice is you.

== stop old-corner
At this corner of South Water and Clark, John Calhoun printed the first number of the Democrat on 26 November 1833. The office has since moved: by May its colophon placed it upstairs over Jones & King's hardware store, a short distance along South Water Street, which is where you now work. The paper appears every Wednesday, and today's is No. 11 of its second volume. It also has competition. Since 8 June the town has supported a second weekly, the Chicago American, published on Saturday mornings.

== narrative old-corner
West to the river, or east toward the competition?

== label old-corner west-first
Westward to the Sauganash

== consequence old-corner west-first
The river end of town reads the news before anyone else.

== label old-corner east-first
Investigate the rival

== consequence old-corner east-first
You will study the rival paper before anyone has seen yours.

== stop sauganash
West along the river to Lake and Market, where the branches meet, stands the Sauganash. Juliette Kinzie remembered 'a pretentious white two-story building, with bright-blue wooden shutters'. Your own paper inspected it this June and reported it repaired, fitted up and furnished with neatness and taste. Moses and Kirkland call it 'the most attractive hostlery the city possessed', and remember Mark Beaubien as 'the fiddler par excellence of the town'. Before Chicago had a newspaper, news arrived with the mail, and somebody with a good voice read it aloud standing on 'a dry goods box'. Now it arrives under your arm.

== narrative sauganash
Where the town's travellers gather to read the news.

== stop rival
East along South Water Street, near the drawbridge, is the office of the Chicago American. T. O. Davis established it on 8 June, so it is barely three weeks old. Its most recent number, of 27 June, carries a druggist's advertisement for the Fire King's Specific, a cholera elixir offered as a certain preventive and cure. The Democrat's own number of 24 June had collected the cholera reports from Alton and Springfield, a Springfield paper and the Alton Spectator among them, and pronounced Chicago uncommonly healthy, promising the facts if the disease appeared. Two newspapers in one small town, each reading the other every week.

== narrative rival
Where the competing paper is set and printed.

== stop the-wharf
On to the river and Newberry & Dole's warehouse. The firm advertised in the Democrat's very first issue as forwarding and commission merchants, and its card appears in today's number as well, on page four: storage, forwarding and commission merchants, agents for the Merchants' Line, wholesale and retail dealers in dry goods, groceries and provisions. When the schooner Illinois, the first vessel through the new harbour cut, came upriver on 12 July 1834, she was cheered at their wharf. Which bank the warehouse occupied is still disputed. A few copies remain, and the morning is slipping away.

== narrative the-wharf
Linger by the river, or complete the round?

== label the-wharf watch-sails
Wait for a sail

== consequence the-wharf watch-sails
You will observe whatever comes upriver and carry it back in your memory.

== label the-wharf finish-round
Hurry to the Tremont

== consequence the-wharf finish-round
The hotel's readers receive their paper before noon.

== stop the-draw
East to the foot of Dearborn Street and the town's first drawbridge, which the ship-carpenter Nelson R. Norton began building in March 1834. Moses and Kirkland describe it as '300 feet in length, by sixteen in width', with 'a double draw'; Andreas gives an opening of about sixty feet for passing craft, with gallows frames at either end of the draw. Vessels repeatedly struck it, and it was paid for repairs in September 1834 and mended again this year. Every sail bound for the wharves upriver must pass through this gap, and a newspaper wants to know about each one. Remain on the south bank.

== narrative the-draw
Where the river is crossed by people and passed by vessels.

== stop the-tremont
Up to Lake and Dearborn and the Tremont House, the frame hotel Alanson Sweet built in 1833, kept since 1834 by Ira Couch. Its brick successor of 1850 would be considered so 'absurdly large' that people nicknamed it Couch's Folly. Last week's Democrat announced a new auction and commission house on South Water Street, with its first public sale scheduled for 'the 1st day of July'. That is today, and the hotel's guests will certainly want to know where to go.

== narrative the-tremont
Where the final copies are delivered.

== leg 0
The river end of town comes first.

== leg 1
Keep the bundle tucked tightly: not one of these copies belongs to the competition.

== leg 3
Back past your own office, running.

== ending two-papers
Back up the stairs over Jones & King's, with ink on your fingers and two newspapers' worth of information in your head: what the American is advertising, and what came upriver through the draw. Nobody instructed you to examine either. A capable apprentice notices anyway, and one day you may be the person who writes it up.

== ending the-river-news
Back to the office with empty arms and the river in your head: the Sauganash supplied first, and the sails at the draw observed last of all. The Democrat appears again next Wednesday, and whatever arrived on the river this week will need reporting.

== ending sale-day
Every copy delivered, and both newspapers read before noon: the American's elixir at one end of the morning, the auction house's first public sale at the other. Upstairs over Jones & King's there is type to clean and an entire week to fill before next Wednesday.

== ending the-round-done
The round is complete: the Sauganash first, the Tremont last, and every copy delivered where it belongs before noon. Upstairs over Jones & King's, next week's type is waiting to be set. Six weeks ago you answered a notice in the newspaper; this morning the whole town read the newspaper you carried.
