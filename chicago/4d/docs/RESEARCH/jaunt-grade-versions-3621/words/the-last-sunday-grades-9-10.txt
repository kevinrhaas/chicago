== premise
Sunday 8 October 1871, from mid-afternoon to dusk: the busiest, proudest city in the West on its last ordinary day, from the wholesale blocks of Lake Street to State Street, the Water Tower and a barn on DeKoven Street. Nine stops.

== opening
JAUNT: THE LAST SUNDAY. Sunday 8 October 1871, early afternoon. Not a drop of rain has fallen since the end of September, and a warm wind is blowing hard out of the southwest. Last night a fire in a West Side planing mill burned four blocks near the river, and the exhausted firemen are still pouring water on the ruins. This morning's Tribune gave that fire three columns, then turned with evident pleasure to the city's ambitions.

Nine stops: the wholesale blocks of Lake Street, the Court House and the marble hotel beside it, the grain exchange, a theatre preparing for Monday night, the new stores of State Street, the Water Tower across the river, and finally, as the light fails, a small cottage on DeKoven Street. Start on Lake Street.

== stop lake-street
Lake Street at Clark, two in the afternoon. This was once the main commercial street of Chicago: until the mid-1860s it was 'the retail center of Chicago', and the big wholesale houses still crowd the blocks between here and the river. Stone fronts with arched windows line both sides, with iron awnings over the walks and telegraph wires strung overhead. Many of these buildings once stood several feet lower. Beginning in 1859, George M. Pullman, later famous for his sleeping cars, raised entire blocks of brick and stone on jack-screws to the new street grade, 'without even disturbing the operations of business inside'. Today is Sunday, and the stores are shut. In this morning's Tribune a man who follows the land market remarked: 'Every body seems to be swelled up with big schemes.' One block south stands the grandest of those schemes, a hotel of white marble.

== stop sherman-house
The Sherman House, at Clark and Randolph. Francis C. Sherman, who later served two terms as mayor, built his first hotel on this corner in 1836-37. The present building opened in July 1861: six storeys of 'the finest Athens marble, from the quarries at Lemont', with its main entrance on Clark Street through a two-storey portico. Its Randolph Street front overlooks the Court House square. This week it shelters a celebrated guest. The conductor Theodore Thomas and his touring orchestra are staying here, and tomorrow night they open a series of ten concerts with 'his concert organization of sixty performers'. Across Randolph Street, in the square, a bell weighing more than five tons hangs in the Court House tower.

== stop court-house
Court House Square, the entire block between Randolph, Clark, Washington and LaSalle. Its lawns, trees and fountain give it 'the appearance of a park', and an iron fence encloses it. The building at its centre houses nearly every city and county office, 'from the jail in its basement to the mayor's quarters'. John M. Van Osdel designed it in 1853, and since then the city has added wings, a new clock and a bronze bell of 10,849 pounds. Look up at the cupola. A spiral staircase climbs to an observation balcony 120 feet above the street, which serves as the fire watchman's walk. Tonight the watchman on duty will be Mathias Schaffer, scanning the city with his spy-glass. Beneath him is the fire-alarm telegraph office that dispatches the engines. Across LaSalle Street, the grain of the West is bought and sold.

== stop chamber
The Chamber of Commerce, on the southeast corner of Washington and LaSalle. Edward Burling designed it, and it was dedicated on 30 August 1865, when Charles Randolph, president of the Board of Trade, delivered the principal address. Inside is a great hall 'decorated with frescoes and paintings', and the business that matters most here is the Board's commodities exchange, where the grain of the prairie farms changes hands. On a Sunday the hall stands silent. LaSalle Street around it, a row of stone fronts under mansard roofs, has become 'the home of Insurance Companies and commission merchants', and the banks are moving steadily closer. Two blocks east, on Washington Street, workmen are busy even today, putting the finishing touches on a theatre for Monday night.

== stop crosbys
Crosby's Opera House, on the north side of Washington Street between State and Dearborn. Uranus Crosby opened it in 1865, designed by the architect W. W. Boyington: five storeys under a mansard roof, with allegorical statues of Painting, Sculpture, Music and Commerce above the entrance arch. Shops occupy the ground floor, and the auditorium behind them seats three thousand. All summer it has been renovated, at a cost of about $80,000. This morning's Tribune reported that 'the whole house, gallery and all, is nearly sold for the first night.' That first night is tomorrow, when Theodore Thomas opens the season. Late this evening the gas will be lit inside for the first time, 'that its effects might be seen by gaslight'. One block east lies the newest shopping street in Chicago.

== stop state-street
State Street at Washington. Four years ago this stretch held a frame house, an ale store and a furniture shop. Then Potter Palmer, who had made his first fortune in trade, purchased 'three-quarters of a mile of property along State Street south of Lake', intending to make it the city's principal retail street. On the northeast corner stands the six-storey store of Field & Leiter, its front almost 150 feet long. Marshall Field and Levi Leiter abandoned Lake Street to move here, and their trained clerks treat the fashionable customers who shop here 'like guests rather than mere customers'. A few blocks south is Palmer's own new hotel, the Palmer House. Across the river, on the North Side, the city's oldest and wealthiest families live in large houses under great elms.

== stop water-tower
The Water Tower, on Pine Street at Chicago Avenue, on the North Side, dedicated with a great celebration in 1867. Across the street the Pumping Station draws clean water through a tunnel two miles out under the lake, far from the 'unspeakably polluted Chicago River'. W. W. Boyington, the architect of Crosby's, designed both buildings in 'castellated Gothic' limestone. South of here, toward the river, stand the homes of the old families, 'substantial and elegant, and shaded with grand elms'. Yet for years the Board of Police and Fire Commissioners has warned that 'the water supply was insufficient', and that the city should not depend on a single set of pumps. The whole city depends on exactly that, and the roof over the machinery is made of wood.

== stop saturday-ruins
The east end of the Van Buren Street bridge, looking across to the West Side. Last night, between ten and eleven, a fire broke out in the boiler room of Lull & Holmes's planing mill on Canal Street, in a district insurance brokers call the 'Red Flash' because everything in it burns so readily. By this afternoon four blocks and nearly a million dollars of property were gone. 'The piles of coal and lumber burned all day on Sunday', and thousands of people came to look. Many of the firemen worked eighteen hours without rest, until three o'clock this afternoon; they are 'nearly exhausted', and some of their equipment is damaged. No rain has fallen in more than a week, and the wind is still strong from the southwest. Ten blocks south, on DeKoven Street, a woman has finished milking her cows for the evening.

== stop dekoven
137 DeKoven Street, on the West Side, at dusk. This is a working neighbourhood of small wooden houses, with 'wood shavings piled in every barn and under every house'. Patrick and Catherine O'Leary and their five children live in the rear of this cottage and rent the front to the McLaughlin family. Behind it stands a wooden barn, sixteen by twenty feet, its loft recently filled with hay for the winter. Inside are five cows, a calf and a horse. Catherine O'Leary sells milk to the neighbours; she milked at half past four, and at about seven she will feed the horse beside the fence and shut him in. Tonight the McLaughlins are holding a party for a cousin newly arrived from Ireland. Mr. McLaughlin is a fiddler, and there will be music. The wind has not dropped.

== ending the-barn
That night, at about nine, Daniel Sullivan, a drayman sitting on the sidewalk across DeKoven Street, saw fire in the O'Leary barn. Within thirty minutes it 'was beyond the control of the Fire Department.' In the Court House cupola Mathias Schaffer saw the glow, misjudged its distance, and signalled the wrong alarm box; when he realised his mistake, no corrected alarm was sent. By Monday morning the Court House bell had crashed through the burning building. The Sherman House was 'one of the most complete wrecks of the night', and Crosby's Opera House never reopened. At about three in the morning the pumping works became 'an utter wreck'. Theodore Thomas 'was compelled to run for his life'.

According to legend, Mrs. O'Leary's cow kicked over a lamp. Even Andreas, the city's great historian, admitted: 'There is no proof that Mrs. O'Leary is responsible for the fire.' The Great Chicago Fire jaunt begins in that barn.
