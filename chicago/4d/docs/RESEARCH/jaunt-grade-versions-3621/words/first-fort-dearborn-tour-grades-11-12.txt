== premise
The last full day of the first Fort Dearborn, 14 August 1812, from daybreak to sunset: every building on Captain Whistler's 1808 draught, surveyed while the garrison prepares to evacuate. Twelve stops.

== opening
JAUNT: FORT DEARBORN, BUILDING BY BUILDING. Friday, 14 August 1812, is the last full day of the first Fort Dearborn, a military post the United States established in 1803, when Captain John Whistler's company marched in from Detroit and constructed it in logs on the south bank of the Chicago River. In 1808 Whistler drew every building for the War Department, and since 1810 the commander has been Captain Nathan Heald, with 'fifty-four regulars and twelve militia'. On 18 June the United States declared war on Britain, although the news penetrated this remote frontier only in July, and five days ago an order arrived from the general at Detroit: abandon the post. Tomorrow at nine the garrison marches out of the gate, so today everyone is packing, distributing and systematically destroying whatever cannot be transported, and waiting apprehensively. 

Twelve stops, from daybreak to sunset, with the clock advancing methodically through the day. By tomorrow night every building you are about to see except one will be ash, so watch attentively for the solitary survivor.

== stop the-fort
14 August 1812, daybreak. This is the first Fort Dearborn, observed from outside its pickets. Captain John Whistler's company reached this bend of the river in 1803, 'at two o'clock on the afternoon of August 17', where the bank stood 'eight feet high', and constructed the entire post in logs with their own hands. A visitor from Fort Wayne in 1809 characterized it as 'the neatest and best wooden garrison in the United States'. Its design comprises barracks surrounding a square, two gun blockhouses at opposite corners, and a double ring of pickets. Nine years later, the order has arrived to abandon it, and tomorrow morning everyone inside will depart, so today is their last full day here, beginning, like every day in this fort, at the gate.

== stop the-gate
Six in the morning, at the main gate, positioned in the middle of the southern side. A girl who resided here remembered that sunset was 'when they used to shut the gates of the fort'. Observe the two concentric rows of pickets: an attacker who surmounted the first 'would only find himself within a narrow inclosure', exposed to fire from the blockhouses at the corners. Whistler engineered this fort to withstand a protracted siege, yet nobody intends to endure one now, because the order is to evacuate, and this is the gate they will use. Continue through the gate into the quarters where the enlisted soldiers reside.

== stop soldiers
Seven in the morning, just inside the gate. The soldiers' barracks flank it on both sides, two storeys with 'shingled Roofs and Galliaries fronting the parade'. The first company stationed here constructed the fort themselves, and when their enlistments ran out 'they had all re-enlisted'. Whistler's correspondence reveals the post's extraordinary isolation: he had 'sixty-six men to supply' with fifty-six suits of clothes, and 'two fifers but the only fife has been lost.' The garrison now numbers 'fifty-four regulars and twelve militia', and this morning they are packing for the long march to Fort Wayne, while the commander who will lead them resides across the parade, where five days ago he made a decision his own officers implored him to reconsider.

== stop commandant
Half past eight, on the parade. In the middle stands the 'lofty flagstaff'; on the east side, the commanding officer's quarters. Captain Nathan Heald has commanded here since 1810, and in June 1811 he brought his bride Rebekah, 'the niece of Captain William Wells' of Fort Wayne. Wau-Bun has her and Mrs. Helm 'playing at battledoor' in the quarters last spring. In May, Heald wrote, 'we had a son born dead for the want of a skilful Midwife.' General Hull's order to abandon the post arrived on 9 August and 'was read next morning upon parade', here. The officers' 'unanimous advice was, to remain where they were, and fortify themselves as strongly as possible.' Heald nevertheless elected to obey his orders, and those officers, who live directly opposite, will march out behind him tomorrow.

== stop officers
Ten o'clock, the officers' barracks, facing the commandant. Three officers share these quarters, each with his own connection to the fort's turbulent politics. Lieutenant Linai Helm, Heald's second in command, requested a transfer from Detroit for 'the cheaper cost of living'; his wife Margaret is the 'step-daughter of old John Kinzie', the trader across the river. Ensign George Ronan, the junior officer, is 'a young cadet direct from West Point'. And the surgeon's mate, Dr. Isaac Van Voorhis, twenty-two, who wrote last October that this place would one day be 'the seat of millions yet unborn'. By tomorrow noon Ronan and Van Voorhis will be dead and Helm a prisoner, while on the northern side of the parade stands the solitary building in this fort destined to survive until next spring.

== stop magazine
Eleven o'clock, the north side. Ahead is the magazine, where gunpowder is stored: 'a small structure made of brick', in a fort built of logs. Beside it, the north-west blockhouse and its cannon, and 'A small wicket gate' through the pickets. To the right, the north range, part barracks and part 'the contractor's store of supplies'. When the fort burns, this modest masonry building 'alone defied the fire', and seven months from now a British agent, Robert Dickson, will find that 'The powder magazine was in a good state of preservation'. The fort's spare ammunition, however, is no longer anywhere in these buildings, because last night it was secretly carried to the north-east corner and dropped down a well.

== stop covered-way
Noon, the north-east corner. From here a covered way descended to the river, enabling the garrison to obtain water 'in time of attack', although Whistler listed it on his drawing while omitting it from the plan: 'Omited in their places'. Wau-Bun calls it a sally-port, with a well excavated inside. Last night the ammunition and liquor 'were carried, part into the sally-port, and thrown into a well'; the muskets 'were broken up and thrown into the well' afterward, and the whiskey barrels were staved in and emptied into the river, because Heald feared the warriors 'would make bad use of it'. The destruction was hardly secret, since by this morning 'the taste of the water' was 'strong grog'. Beyond the south-east corner, the traders are calculating whatever remains.

== stop merchants
Half past one, outside the pickets on the south-east. Here stood the merchant's shop, the sutler's garden, and the armourer's shop, where the garrison's weapons were maintained. The sutler is the private merchant licensed to supply the soldiers; since January that has been 'Messrs. Kinzie & Forsyth', John Kinzie's firm. Behind you rises the south-east blockhouse, the fort's second gun corner, although Whistler's drawing places the blockhouses north-west and south-east; Wau-Bun, written forty years later, remembered both 'on the southern side', illustrating how memory reshapes physical detail. South of here, the gardens are full of a summer's harvest that the garrison will not remain to eat.

== stop gardens
Three in the afternoon, the commanding officer's gardens, south of the fort, where in the early years 'melons and other small fruit and vegetables were raised'. Whistler ordered the underbrush cleared 'all within one Fourth of a Mile of the Garrison', so that no approaching enemy could advance undetected. Beyond lies what he described as 'a large parraria', open grass, and the line of woods along the South Branch. Tomorrow the column will travel in the opposite direction: east to the lake, then south along the beach, 'with the lake on our left, and a high sand bank on our right'. West of here, at the government's trading house, the last of the merchandise is being distributed.

== stop agency
Half past four, west of the fort, among the government houses: the factor's trading house, the agent's house, the interpreter's, the bake house and the stables. The factor manages the government's own store, and Heald's report for today states: 'On the 14th, I delivered the Indians all the goods in the factory store, and a considerable quantity of provisions which we could not take away with us.' Out went 'blankets, broadcloths, calicoes, paints'. In April the apprehensive townspeople had fortified the agency house, 'an old-fashioned log building, with a hall running through the centre', and its piazzas 'were planked up, for greater security, port-holes were cut, and sentinels posted at night.' Now the shelves are bare, and the day's last light is falling on the river below the fort.

== stop wharf
Six in the evening, at the wharf, below the bank on the river side, where boats tied up and the fort's stores came ashore. In 1808 Whistler assessed the garrison as 'perfectly secure from any ambuscade', except for one thing: the houses across the river, 'somewhat in the way'. He had already removed a picket fence over there that 'might serve as a Barrier against the Garrison'. The largest of those houses, directly across the water, belongs to John Kinzie, whose family will not march with the soldiers tomorrow but will travel by boat instead. Walk along the bank and face it as the sun sets on the fort's final night.

== stop across-the-river
Sunset. Across the river stands 'John Kinzie Esq. House on the opisite side River', accompanied by three additional houses to the west. Kinzie supplies the garrison commercially and resides within shouting distance of the gate, an intimacy of trade and residence characteristic of the frontier. Behind you, the gates are closing at sunset for the final time. Tomorrow 'at 9 o'clock in the morning' they will open, the band will commence 'the Dead March', and the garrison will proceed out. The following morning the fort is deliberately set on fire, and only the brick magazine survives, until the army returns in 1816 to construct a second fort on this identical ground.

== ending burned
On 15 August 1812 the garrison marched out of the gate, and Heald wrote afterwards that the warriors 'set fire to the fort and left the place, taking the prisoners with them.' Seven months later Robert Dickson found the brick magazine standing and 'The powder magazine was in a good state of preservation'. Everything else Whistler constructed had been reduced to ash, and for four years 'over Chicago silence and desolation reigned supreme', until the army came back in 1816 and built again on the same ground. To follow the garrison out of the gate on the morning of the 15th, take the jaunt The Battle of Fort Dearborn.
