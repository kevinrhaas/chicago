== premise
Pretend to write a letter home. Read the paper's list of letters nobody picked up. Then figure out where to mail yours.

== opening
JAUNT: A LETTER HOME. You have been in Chicago a few days. You owe the people you left behind a letter. Mail is very important to this town. In winter, one history book says, nothing could come in and nothing could be sent out. When the mail did come, the whole town gathered around. Someone would stand on a box and read it out loud.

Decide what your letter is for. Find paper and ink. Learn how the town handles its mail. Then plan where to mail your letter.

== stop purpose
Start at Mrs Rufus Brown's log boarding house, behind Peck's store. Sit down at the big table everyone shares, if you can find a corner of it. First decide what your letter is for. It could be a quick note that you got here safely. It could be a long letter about the town, for anyone thinking of moving west too. Or it could be a page of questions that someone at home will need to answer.

== label purpose safe
Tell them you arrived safely

== consequence purpose safe
A short letter about the trip, the town, and how you are well.

== label purpose prospects
Describe the town's prospects

== consequence purpose prospects
A longer letter, for anyone at home who might want to move here.

== label purpose news
Ask for news from home

== consequence purpose news
A letter of questions. The answer will need to find you here.

== stop paper
In front stands Peck's store. His ads list cloth, tools, food and dishes. But for paper, the newspaper from 20 June 1835 points somewhere else. Frederick Thomas has a new drug store on Water Street. It sells letter paper, wax, and black and red ink. You fold the paper and seal it with a drop of wax. Nobody here uses envelopes yet.

== narrative paper
So you get your paper from the drug store.

== stop notice
Go to the corner where the Democrat newspaper began. Read its notices. On 27 May 1835 it printed a list of letters still waiting at the post office. These were letters nobody had come to pick up. Every name is someone who moved on, or never came, or never thought to ask.

== narrative notice
Ask your family to write your name clearly on their answer.

== stop mail-corner
End at the old mail corner, where Lake Street and South Water Street meet. John Hogan became the town's postmaster in November 1832. He handed out the mail from a small room walled off inside his log store. The post office stayed here until about July 1834. This spring Hogan's ad says his store is one door down from the post office. The paper still calls him the postmaster.

== narrative mail-corner
The post office counter is one door along. Where will you look for it?

== label mail-corner south-water
Look for it beside Hogan's store

== consequence mail-corner south-water
You will ask along South Water Street, one door down from Hogan's.

== label mail-corner keep
Keep the letter until someone shows you

== consequence mail-corner keep
You fold your letter away until a neighbor can show you the counter.

== leg 0
Take your plan around to the street side.

== ending safe-and-well
Your letter says you got here safely and you are well. It is folded and sealed, ready for the post office counter on South Water Street.

== ending come-west
Your letter tells about a busy, muddy, fast-growing town and its chances. Your family will read it out loud at home. Somebody will be on the next boat.

== ending write-back
Your letter asks for news from home. Now you know what happens to a letter nobody comes to pick up. It is ready to send.

== keepsake text
A made-up folded letter, written in town and not mailed yet.
