# Hogan's Store — research dossier

**Record:** `data/structures/hogan_store.json` · **Scene status:** standing on 1835-07-01 on a
continuity argument, in store use, **not the post office by then** · **Milestone 1, south bank**

The log building at the west end of the Lake Street block in which the United States established
a post office at Chicago on 31 March 1831. It is the first record in this dataset whose
**footprint is evidence**: Andreas gives the building's size twice, twenty by forty-five feet,
and gives it as an aside about how little room the town's mail needed.

---

## 2. The load-bearing passages

Andreas, *History of Chicago* vol. 1, "Postal Affairs":

> "The post-office in 1833, John S. C. Hogan, Postmaster, was kept in a small log building near
> the corner of Lake and South Water streets. … **The building was twenty by forty-five feet in
> size, was partitioned off so as to serve as a post-office on one side, and as the store of
> Brewster, Hogan & Co., on the other.**"

Andreas, "Government Appointees — Post-office and Postmaster", written separately and agreeing:

> "The first Postmaster of Chicago was an Indian trader named Jonathan N. Bailey, who was
> appointed March 31, 1831, and opened the post-office in **a log house occupied as a store by
> John Stephen Coates' Hogan**, near the present corner of Lake and South Water streets. …
> Some idea may be formed of the limited accommodations that were ample for the post-office of
> those days, when it is stated that **the store only occupied an area of forty-five by twenty
> feet.** In the summer of 1834, John L. Wilson became second assistant Postmaster, and **about
> July of this year, the post-office was moved to near the corner of Franklin and South Water
> streets**."

And in the postal narrative: "**In July, 1834, the office was removed to the corner of Franklin
and South Water streets**, where it remained until after the expiration of Hogan's term of
office."

J. Seymour Currey, *Fort Dearborn Magazine*, December 1922, transcribed by chicagology:

> "The office of the postmaster was situated in **a log building about where South Water street
> intersects Lake street, near the east end of the bridge**. John S. C. Hogan kept a store there."

## 3. Correction to this project's own dossier

`docs/research/03-structures-north.md` § 4 tabulates the post office's homes and gives
**2 Nov 1832 – 3 Mar 1837** at the **south-west corner of Franklin and South Water**, tagged
`[DOC]`, with the note "**This is the 1835-07-01 post office**". Two things in that row are
wrong, and both come from reading Currey's page, which makes Hogan's appointment and the move one
sentence:

1. **The date is the postmaster's, not the building's.** Hogan succeeded Bailey on 2 Nov 1832 and
   stayed put; Andreas says twice, in two independently written passages, that the office was
   still at Lake and South Water through 1833 and moved **about July 1834**. The 1835 post office
   is the Franklin Street one, so the dossier's conclusion survives — the twenty months in between
   do not.
2. **"South west corner" is Currey's alone.** Andreas says only "near the corner" and "to near the
   corner". No source reached names a side of either street at Franklin.

The dossier is a committed input and is not edited; the correction lives here, on the source
record, and in `docs/STATUS.md`.

## 4. The Franklin Street post office is NOT modelled, and that is the point of § 3

On the scene date the town's post office is a **different building**, near Franklin and South
Water, roughly 100 m east of this one. Nothing reached describes it: not its construction, not
its size, not its side of either street, not whether Hogan owned or rented it. A record for it
today would be a footprint invented at a corner that is itself one source's word against silence
— the most invented building in the dataset, standing for the one institution this slice can
actually document. It is left unbuilt and written down instead.

**To resolve it:** Andreas vol. 1 at page-image level around the postal chapter; the *Chicago
Democrat* for 1834-35 (Hogan advertised mail arrivals in it — his notice of 19 Sept 1835 is
transcribed in Andreas); and the 1834/1835 Wabansia and Kinzie's Addition plat, which is
contemporaneous but covers the north side rather than this block.

## 5. A disagreement about street width, recorded rather than averaged

Currey's page states that Thompson's 1830 plat laid the town out with "**straight streets
uniformly 66 feet wide** (the length of a surveyor's chain) with alleys 16 feet wide bisecting
each block". Every position in this dataset that is offset from a modern intersection centre uses
**half of an 80 ft street**, 12.2 m, which comes from the widths annotated on Hathaway 1834 (see
`docs/STATUS.md`, S1 carry-forward).

The difference is 2.1 m per offset — an order of magnitude inside the ±20 m the georeference
already carries, so nothing moves on account of it. But the two figures cannot both be right about
the same street, and the reconciliation worth testing is that they are **not** about the same
street: 66 ft was Thompson's general width and the riverfront and market streets were platted
wider. Read the widths off Hathaway 1834 at Lake, South Water and Market specifically, rather than
taking the sheet's annotation as a single town-wide number.

## 6. What is documented, what is not

| aspect | tag | why |
|---|---|---|
| footprint 13.716 × 6.096 m | **documented** | "twenty by forty-five feet", given twice |
| construction: log | **documented** | three statements, two of them Andreas's |
| which axis runs along the street | *inferred*, via the facade bearing | no source says which way it faced |
| position | *inferred* | both sources say **near** / **about** a junction; neither gives a corner |
| standing on 1835-07-01 | *inferred* | attested to about July 1834 and no further — **the weak point** |
| function on the scene date | *inferred* | a store before and beside the post office; nothing describes it after |
| storeys, wall height, roof, pitch, chimney | *inferred* | typological, exactly as for the Walker meeting house |

**Nothing in this record is conjectural**, which is a first here and is not a boast: it means the
gaps in it are gaps in the *evidence's* precision rather than holes filled with invention. The
building's survival to the scene date is the claim to attack.

## 7. Open threads

- **Andreas at page-image level.** The full-text index is lossy; the postal chapter's pages carry
  the Bates interview of 31 Oct 1883 and may carry more about the building itself.
- **When was it built, and what became of it?** Neither end of its life is attested. Hogan came to
  Chicago "as early as 1830" and was elected justice of the peace on 24 July 1830 at James
  Kinzie's house.
- **Was the partition still there in 1835?** The post office left in 1834; a 6 m deep log building
  divided down its length for three years may well have kept the division. Unattested either way,
  and the archetype models neither, so it costs nothing today.
- **Philo Carpenter's log drug store** stood on Lake Street "immediately adjacent to the
  Sauganash's public bar" (`docs/research/03-structures-north.md` § 1) — the next candidate on
  this block, and one with no dimensions at all.
