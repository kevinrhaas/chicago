# More questions for the townswomen, soldiers and Potawatomi of 1 July 1835, so that the many
# of each have many things to be asked. Reconstruction, liberty L-tales-3553.

## a35-tw-fourth
roles: townswoman
q: Are you ready for the Fourth of July?

Three days to go and I have four pies to bake and a dress to let out. There will be a salute from the fort, a procession, the Declaration read out by a lawyer who will take an hour about it, and a dinner at the Tremont for the gentlemen. The ladies will watch from the windows, and then we shall all dance.

?? What's the best part?
The fireworks, if the powder came on the last boat. Last year it did not, and the soldiers fired the cannon instead, twenty-four times, one for each state, and broke two windows on Water Street. Ask any soldier at the fort about it. They still laugh.

## a35-tw-garden
roles: townswoman
q: Can anything grow in this mud?

Anything at all, if you can keep the pigs out of it. The ground is black and rich and you can put a seed in and stand back. My beans are over the fence already. My cabbages are the size of a baby's head. The trouble is the water: one wet week and the whole garden is a pond with lettuce floating in it.

?? What do you grow?
Potatoes, beans, peas, onions, cabbages, squash and a row of flowers for my soul, hollyhocks and pinks. A woman needs something to look at that is not mud. The Potawatomi women showed me how to plant the beans by the corn so the corn holds them up. They know more about this ground than any of us.

## a35-tw-letters
roles: townswoman
q: Do you write to your family back East?

Every week, to my sister in Vermont. I tell her about the town and the prices and the people and the mud, and she writes back that I must be exaggerating. I sent her a lock of mud once, wrapped in paper. She never mentioned it.

?? What do they think of Chicago?
They think I live among wolves and savages and gamblers. The wolves are real. The gamblers are real. The savages are mostly the Yankees on Saturday night. The Potawatomi women who sell me baskets have better manners than half the men at the land office.

## a35-tw-baby
roles: townswoman
q: How old is your baby?

Six months, born in January in the coldest week of the winter, with the snow up to the window and the doctor stuck at the fort. My neighbour came across with a lantern and stayed till morning. That is how it is here. You have no family, so your neighbours become your family.

?? Who is your neighbour?
A French woman, a Laframboise, who has had seven children and knew exactly what to do. She speaks very little English and I speak no French, and we managed perfectly. She made me a broth that I would give a lot to taste again.

## a35-tw-cow
roles: townswoman
q: Is that your cow wandering down the street?

She is, the wretch. She gets out every morning and goes to the river to drink, and then she stands in the middle of Lake Street and the teamsters have to go round her. The town has an ordinance against animals running loose. Nobody pays any mind to it, least of all the cow.

?? What's her name?
Mrs. Jackson, after the President's late wife, because my husband is a Whig and thinks it is funny. I do not. But she gives four quarts a day, and in this town a cow is worth more than a lot.

## a35-tw-bonnet
roles: townswoman
q: Where did you get that bonnet?

From a milliner on Lake Street, who had it from New York in May. Straw, with a silk ribbon and a bunch of flowers made of cloth. It cost me three dollars and my husband nearly fainted. But a woman cannot walk out on the lake shore on a Sunday in last year's bonnet. Not here. Not now.

?? Is everyone so fashionable?
Everyone tries. The land men's wives have the silks and the French girls have the best taste, and the rest of us copy whoever walked past last. The fashions here are about three months behind New York and six months ahead of Detroit.

## a35-tw-church-ladies
roles: townswoman, lady
q: What do the church ladies do?

Sew. We have a sewing circle every Wednesday at the minister's house and we make clothes for the poor, quilts for the church, and money for the bell we are saving for. We also talk a great deal about everybody who is not there, which is the real business of a sewing circle.

?? What do you talk about?
This week: whether the new doctor is married, why Mr. Ogden has stayed so long when he said he was going back to New York, and who has bought the lot next to the church. We know everything in this town a week before the newspapers.

## a35-tw-widow
roles: townswoman
q: Are you on your own here?

I am a widow, since last autumn. My husband took the fever on a contract at the harbour and was gone in a week. I have two children and his tools and a lot on the South Side that he bought for fifty dollars and that a man offered me four hundred for in May.

?? Did you sell?
Not yet. Everybody tells me to sell and everybody tells me to hold, and all of them want something. I take in sewing and boarders and I wait. A widow in this town finds out quickly which of her husband's friends were really his friends.

## a35-tw-sunday
roles: townswoman, youth
q: What do you do on Sunday?

Church in the morning, dinner at noon, and in the afternoon everybody walks out along the lake shore by the fort, in their best clothes, to see and be seen. The young men pretend to look at the water. The young women pretend not to look at the young men. The old ladies look at everybody.

?? Is it pretty?
On a fine afternoon in June, with the lake blue and the sails out on it and the prairie flowers coming up through the grass behind the town, it is the prettiest place I have ever lived. Then you turn round and see Lake Street.

## a35-tw-lake-house
roles: townswoman
q: What's that big building going up?

The Lake House, on the north side, brick, three storeys, with a dining room, they say, as grand as anything in New York. The men building it are Kinzie and his partners. It will not be finished till next year at this rate. When it is, they say they will serve oysters from Baltimore.

?? Will you go?
On my birthday, if my husband has made his money by then. If not, I will stand outside and smell the oysters. Either way, it is good for the town. A town with oysters is a city.

## a35-so-desert
roles: soldier
q: Have you ever thought of deserting?

Every day, looking at Lake Street from the blockhouse. A carpenter there makes in a week what I make in a month. But I have two years left and a sergeant who watches me, and if they catch you they shave your head and drum you out and your name is in the paper. I would sooner leave by the gate with a discharge in my pocket.

?? How many have gone?
More than the major likes to say. The town hides them: a man disappears at night and turns up on a roof somewhere with a different name and a hammer. Everybody knows. Nobody tells.

## a35-so-payment-guard
roles: soldier
q: Will you be guarding the payment?

When it comes, yes. All the Potawatomi from every village will be camped round the town and the money will come in kegs under guard. Our job is to stand between the money and the whiskey sellers and the claim men, and keep the peace. It is the only real work this fort will do this year.

?? Is it dangerous?
Not from the Potawatomi. They are the most patient people I have ever seen. From the whiskey sellers and the traders with their books, yes, a little. They want the money before the people get it. We are supposed to stop that. Mostly we cannot.

## a35-so-old-fort
roles: soldier
q: Is this the same fort as before the war?

No. The first Fort Dearborn was burned after the battle in 1812. The army came back in 1816 and built this one on nearly the same ground. Some of the logs are from the old one, they say, and the old soldiers swear that on a still night you can hear something on the shore where the battle was.

?? Do you believe it?
I have stood sentry on the south blockhouse at two in the morning with the wind off the lake and heard things I did not like. Probably the wind. Probably owls. I did not go down to look.

## a35-so-cooking
roles: soldier
q: Who does the cooking at the fort?

We take turns, by mess, eight men to a mess, and one of us cooks each week. Salt pork, beans, bread from the fort's oven, beef when the contractor brings it, fish when we catch it. On my week the men complain. On the Irishman's week they eat everything and ask for more.

?? What's his secret?
Onions, and a bit of whatever he can trade for in town. He goes over the wall at night for herbs, he says. I do not ask what else he goes over the wall for.

## a35-pm-whiskey
roles: potawatomi_man
q: Why is there so much whiskey in the camps?

Because the traders bring it, at night, in barrels on carts, and sell it for anything: a blanket, a gun, a horse, the promise of the payment money. The young men drink it to forget, and the old men drink it because the young men do, and the women hide the knives.

?? Can anyone stop it?
The agent tries. The soldiers try. The chiefs try. But the whiskey sellers are clever and the town is full of them, and every man of them has a claim against the payment. Some of our people do not drink at all, and they are the ones who will get to the west country with their horses.

## a35-pm-language
roles: potawatomi_man, potawatomi_woman
q: Do you speak English?

A little, for trade. Yes, no, how much, too much, whiskey, God damn. That is what the Americans teach us. My son speaks more, from the town. He says words I do not know, and laughs when I say them back wrong.

?? Will your language survive in the west?
As long as there are mothers to speak it to children. Our language has crossed lakes and rivers before. It will cross the Mississippi. What it will lose is the names of this place: the river, the lake, the wild leek, the portage. Words for a place die when nobody stands in it.

## a35-pw-grave
roles: potawatomi_woman
q: What will you leave behind?

My mother's grave, on a rise by the Des Plaines, with a little house of bark over it. I visit her every spring with tobacco and food. When we go west, nobody will visit her. The Americans will plough the rise. That is the hardest thing.

?? Can you take anything of hers?
Her kettle, her awl, a belt she wove, and her stories, which I have told to my daughter. That is all a woman leaves anyway. The rest goes back to the ground, wherever the ground is.

## a35-dm-irish-wash
roles: domestic, irish
sex: f
q: How did you find your place?

I stood on the landing with my bundle the day the boat came in, and a lady came down from the street and looked at my hands and asked could I cook. I said I could. That was a lie, but I learned by Friday. She has kept me a year now and raised my wages twice.

?? Is she good to you?
She is fair, which is more than kind. She pays on the day and she does not count the sugar. She has a temper when the mud comes in on the floor, but so would anyone. When I marry, I will ask her to stand up for me. She will cry. She cries at everything.
