# Questions for the men of Chicago on 1 July 1835, two years a town and two weeks into the
# government land sale: labourers, mechanics, merchants, clerks, lawyers, keepers of houses.
# Reconstruction, liberty L-tales-3553. Nothing here is said that the town could not yet know.

## a35-tm-boom
roles: townsman, labourer
q: Why is everyone in such a hurry?
meet: ogden_w_b

Because the land office opened this spring and the government is selling the country round here at a dollar and a quarter an acre, and lots in town that went for a hundred dollars three years ago are going for thousands. Every boat brings a hundred more men off the lake with money in their boots and the same idea in their heads.

?? Is it real money?
Some of it. Some of it is paper from banks in Michigan that may or may not be there next year. A man buys a lot on Lake Street for a note, sells it the same afternoon for a bigger note, and goes to supper rich. Mr. Ogden came from New York last month to sell his brother-in-law's land and found it worth ten times what they paid. Go and ask him if he believes it yet.

?? Have you bought any?
A forty out on the prairie and a quarter-lot off Clark Street, on credit. My wife thinks I am mad. If the canal comes, I am a rich man. If it does not, I have a lot of very wet grass.

## a35-tm-mud
roles: townsman, labourer
q: Is the mud always this bad?

This is a dry week. In April a team of oxen went down on Lake Street up to the yoke and they had to dig it out with planks. Somebody put up a sign in the slough at Clark Street: No Bottom Here. Somebody else stuck an old hat on a stick in the middle of the street with a board beside it: The Shortest Road to China.

?? Why not drain it?
Drain it where? The whole town is about four feet above the lake on a good day. Rain comes and has nowhere to go. They talk about raising the streets, one day, when the town is rich. I say they had better raise the people first.

?? Any advice?
Boots to the knee and a plank to stand on. And never wear your good trousers on a Tuesday. Tuesday is when the teamsters come in.

## a35-tm-canal
roles: townsman, labourer, merchant
q: What canal does everyone keep talking about?
meet: hubbard_gurdon

The Illinois and Michigan canal: a ditch from the South Branch over the old portage to the Des Plaines and down to the Illinois River, so a boat can go from the lakes to the Mississippi without anybody carrying it. The French talked about it a hundred and fifty years ago. The state passed a law this winter to borrow the money for it.

?? When will they dig?
Next year, they say, or the year after. Every year they say next year. But this time the state has the land from Congress and the commissioners are laying out the line. When the digging starts there will be work for a thousand men, and every one of them will need boots and bread and whiskey. Mr. Hubbard is a canal man. Ask him how deep it will be. He will draw it for you in the dust.

## a35-tm-ships
roles: townsman, labourer
q: How did you get to Chicago?
meet: allen_lieut_james

By steamboat from Buffalo to Detroit, and by schooner round the top of Michigan to here, two weeks and a half with forty people sleeping on the deck and the cook drunk from Mackinac on. When we came in there was no harbour, only a sandbar across the river mouth, and they rowed us ashore in a lighter and dropped us in the lake up to our knees.

?? Is there a harbour now?
The government is building one. Two long piers going out into the lake, and last year the spring flood cut a channel straight through the bar and the schooners can come right up into the river now. Lieutenant Allen of the army is in charge of the work. Go and look at his piers. They are the best thing in Chicago.

## a35-tm-fever
roles: townsman, labourer
q: Do people get sick here?

Every summer. Fever and ague: you shake till your teeth chatter, then you burn, then you are well for a day, then it comes again. They say it comes off the marshes. Half the men in this town have been yellow at some time. They take quinine and whiskey and go back to work.

?? And the cholera?
That was in thirty-two, when General Scott's soldiers came through for the Black Hawk war. They brought it on the boats and it went through the fort like a scythe. People still do not like to talk about it. They buried the dead on the lake shore and moved on.

## a35-tm-wolves
roles: townsman, labourer, youth
q: Are there wolves out there?

On the prairie past the last houses, yes, prairie wolves, and you hear them most nights. Last winter the town turned out for a wolf hunt: men on horseback in a great ring, closing in, with dogs and guns and a lot of shouting. They got a few. They got more of each other's toes.

?? Do they come into town?
After the garbage. A wolf came right up behind the Sauganash in February and took a goose off the woodpile. Mark Beaubien fired at it with his fiddle in the other hand. He missed. He says it was the fiddle's fault.

## a35-tm-irish
roles: labourer, irish
sex: m
q: What brought you across the water?
meet: rc_mahoney_patrick

Hunger, mostly. Four of us on half an acre in County Cork, and the rent going up and the potatoes going black. I worked my passage to Quebec and walked to the States and followed the work: a canal in New York, a road in Ohio, and now Chicago, where they say there will be a canal that will need ten thousand of us.

?? Is it better here?
A dollar a day for a man with a shovel, when there is work, and nobody to touch his cap to. Better, yes. The Yankees look at you as if you smelled, but the money is the same colour in an Irishman's pocket. Patrick Mahoney came the same road I did, and he has the luck of the devil. Ask him about the hay wagon.

?? Do you go to church?
Saint Mary's, the little Catholic church on Lake Street, with Father St. Cyr. Wood and nails and not much of either. On a Sunday you hear Irish and French and Potawatomi and a bit of German, all saying the same prayers in different voices.

## a35-tm-harbour-work
roles: labourer
q: What work are you doing?
meet: allen_lieut_james

Harbour work for the government: driving piles, filling cribs with stone, building the north pier out into the lake. It is cold work even in July. The lake comes in green over the cribs and you are wet to the waist from seven until six.

?? Who is in charge?
Lieutenant Allen, a young engineer from the army. He knows his business. He is up on the pier at sunrise looking at the sand, because the sand is the enemy: it drifts in along the shore and fills the channel, and he has to keep it out. Go and ask him about it. He talks about sand the way other men talk about women.

## a35-tm-board
roles: labourer, townsman
q: Where do you sleep?

In a boarding house on Lake Street, eight to a room and two to a bed, for two dollars a week with meals. The landlady counts the potatoes. The man I share with snores and the man in the next bed sings in his sleep. When a boat comes in, they put more men on the floor.

?? Is there nowhere better?
Not until you build it. Every house in Chicago has a family downstairs and boarders up the ladder. They put up frame houses in a week, now, with these new light frames of sawn studs nailed together, no posts and beams at all. The old carpenters say they will blow away. So far they have not.

## a35-tm-frame
roles: mechanic, townsman
q: How do they build houses so fast here?
meet: peck_philip

A new way of framing, with thin sawn studs and joists, two by fours and two by sixes, nailed together instead of mortised and pegged. Four men can put up the frame of a house in a day or two. Carpenters from back East laugh at them and say a good wind will carry them off. This town has no time for the old way.

?? Who started it?
People argue. Some say Mr. Taylor, the carpenter who built Saint Mary's. Some say the idea came from all over and nobody owns it. The nails are what make it possible: cut nails, cheap, by the keg. Mr. Peck built the first frame store, on South Water Street. Go and ask him what he paid for his nails.

## a35-tm-blacksmith
roles: mechanic
q: What's your trade?

Blacksmith, at the forge on Lake Street. Shoeing horses and oxen, mending wagon tyres, making hinges, nails, chain, ploughshares, and whatever else a new country needs. The teamsters bring me broken things all day long. On a good day I hear the anvil in my sleep.

?? Is it a good trade here?
The best. Everyone needs iron and nobody can wait for it to come from Buffalo. I came with my tools and a hundred dollars. I have three men working for me now and a house with a floor. My father would not believe it.

?? What's the hardest job?
A prairie plough. The prairie sod is so thick with roots that an ordinary plough breaks on it. You need a breaking plough with a long iron share, filed sharp, and six yoke of oxen to pull it. The first breaking is the hardest work there is. After that, the black ground just opens.

## a35-tm-cooper
roles: mechanic
q: What do you make all day?

Barrels. Pork barrels, beef barrels, flour barrels, whiskey barrels, fish barrels. Mr. Dole and Mr. Hubbard pack pork and beef every autumn, and every pound of it goes east in a barrel I made or a man like me made. Oak staves, iron hoops, and a good tight head.

?? Is it hard to learn?
Seven years an apprentice in Vermont. The trick is the bend: you heat the staves over a fire and pull them in with a windlass till they make the shape. Too hot and they crack, too cold and they will not bend. I can tell by the smell.

## a35-tm-tailor
roles: mechanic, townsman
q: Are clothes expensive here?

Everything is expensive here. Broadcloth comes from New York by lake and costs twice what it does there. A gentleman's coat from me is eighteen dollars and he will need a new one every year, because the mud eats the bottoms of everything. The land men all want to look rich, and the rich all want to look like land men.

?? Who are your best customers?
The lawyers. There are more lawyers in this town every week, and every one of them wants a black coat that looks as if it has been to Washington. Young Caton is a good customer. He pays on time, which is rarer than a good coat.

## a35-tm-merchant
roles: merchant
q: What do you sell?

Dry goods, groceries, hardware, crockery, books, boots, whatever came off the last schooner. A man comes in for a plough and goes out with a plough, a bonnet for his wife, a pound of tea, a set of plates and a Bible. Half my customers pay in cash and half pay in promises.

?? Where do you get it?
From New York, by the Erie Canal to Buffalo and by lake to here. It takes a month if the weather is good and two if it is not. I order in the spring for the autumn and pray the boat does not go down. Mr. Gale has the bookshop on Lake Street. He gets the newspapers first. He knows the New York prices before anybody.

?? Is business good?
Business is so good it frightens me. Everybody is buying everything. When a whole town is buying and nobody is making, somebody is going to be sorry. I keep my own money in gold. Do not tell my partner.

## a35-tm-fur
roles: merchant, canadien
q: Is there still a fur trade here?
meet: hubbard_gurdon

What is left of it. The American Fur Company still sends goods to the Potawatomi and takes their furs, but the furs are thinner every year and the people are being moved west. The old traders like Mr. Hubbard have gone into pork and land and warehouses. The fur trade is a dead man still walking.

?? What happened to it?
The beaver are gone from the near rivers. The muskrat are being drained out with the marshes. The Potawatomi signed their land away two years ago and are going across the Mississippi. A fur trade needs wild country and the people who live in it. This is a town now.

## a35-tm-lawyer
roles: professional
q: Is there much law in Chicago?
meet: caton_john_dean

More every day. Land titles, mostly: a lot sold four times in a week by men who never saw it, and then two of them turn up on the same morning with deeds. I could fill my office with land cases and never see a thief or a murderer.

?? Who are the best lawyers?
Young Caton is clever and hungry. Mr. Hamilton holds every office in the county and is the most useful man to know. There are new men from New York every week with letters of introduction and no clients. Go and see Caton. He will tell you how he won his first case, which is a story he likes very much.

## a35-tm-doctor
roles: professional
q: What's the most common complaint you treat?
meet: kimberly_edmund_s

Fever and ague, as everybody calls it. A man shakes in the morning and burns in the afternoon and is weak as a kitten for a week. Quinine is the cure, if the patient can afford it. Next are broken bones, from wagons and timber and the mud, and after that drink.

?? Do you have many patients?
Too many and too few that pay. Doctor Kimberly and Doctor Egan have the best practices. Kimberly has been here since thirty-two and has seen the cholera. Go and ask him about it if you have a strong stomach.

## a35-tm-editor
roles: professional
q: Which newspaper do you read?
meet: davis_t_o

Both of them, now. The Democrat, which Mr. Calhoun has printed since thirty-three, and the American, which Mr. Davis started this June. One is for Jackson and one is against him. Between them you get the truth, if you read the one and then the other and believe neither.

?? Is it a good fight?
The best entertainment in town. Each says the other is the mouthpiece of a ring of land men, and both are probably right. The town reads them aloud in the taverns and cheers the insults. Go and find Mr. Davis. Ask him what Mr. Calhoun called him this week.

## a35-tm-clerk
roles: clerk
q: What do you do for the store?
meet: wright_john_s

Everything the owner does not want to. I open at six and sweep out the mud, weigh out sugar and tea and nails, measure calico, write up the accounts, chase the debts and sleep under the counter to guard the stock. Eight dollars a month and my keep.

?? Do you want to own a store?
Every clerk in this town wants to own something. Young Wright clerked when he came and now he is buying lots and talking about schools and newspapers as if he owned the place. Go and ask him how he did it. He will tell you it was hard work. It was also his father's money. Both are true.

## a35-tm-innkeeper
roles: innkeeper
q: Is your house full?
meet: couch_ira

Full is not the word. Every bed, every floor, every table after supper is pushed together and three men sleep on it. A boat came in yesterday with sixty people and I fed all of them. My wife cooked until midnight.

?? Who keeps the best house?
The Tremont, the Couch brothers' place at Lake and Dearborn, is the newest and the grandest, with real beds. The Sauganash is the merriest, because Mark Beaubien plays the fiddle and dances with the guests. I keep the cleanest, though nobody gives me any credit for it. Go and see Ira Couch. He will show you his new bedsteads as if they were his children.

## a35-tm-youth-errands
roles: youth
sex: m
q: What do you do all day?

Errands for Mr. Wright's store: carry a letter to the post office, run a parcel to the landing, fetch the boss's dinner from the Sauganash. Sometimes I hold a horse while a gentleman goes in somewhere. A penny for that. When the boats come in, I carry bags. Two pennies a bag.

?? Do you go to school?
In the winter, a bit. In summer there is too much money to be made. Miss Chappel had a school in a room off the church. She married the minister this summer, Mr. Porter. Go and ask her if she remembers me. She does. I put a frog in her desk.

?? What do you want to be?
Rich. Like everybody here. Or a steamboat captain. Or both. A steamboat captain who owns the steamboat.

## a35-tm-youth-bridge
roles: youth
q: Have you crossed the new bridge?
meet: beaubien_mark

The drawbridge at Dearborn Street? Everybody has. It goes up in the middle so the schooners can get through, and when it is up everybody stands on both sides shouting at each other. Last week a man was so busy shouting he walked his horse into the river.

?? Was there always a bridge?
Before last year there was only a ferry, and before that a canoe. Mark Beaubien used to row people across for a few pennies, and he still says the river was better before. He says the bridge ruined a perfectly good business. Go and ask him. He will play you a tune about it.

## a35-tm-auction
roles: townsman, merchant
q: What's all the shouting down the street?
meet: newberry_walter_loomis

The auctioneers, selling lots. A man stands on a barrel outside the land office or on the porch of a tavern with a map and a hammer, and sells a piece of ground that half the bidders have never seen and the other half could not find. Going, going, gone, and somebody has bought a piece of wet prairie for five hundred dollars.

?? Do the buyers ever see the land?
Some ride out to look. Some do not bother; they will sell it next week to somebody else who will not see it either. Mr. Newberry bought land on the north side years ago, when it was nothing. Ask him what it was worth then and what it is worth now. Then sit down.

## a35-tm-politics
roles: townsman, professional
q: Who's in charge of this town?

The board of trustees, five men elected each year, since the town was incorporated two summers ago. They pass ordinances about pigs in the streets and fires and the market, and nobody pays them any mind until somebody's pig eats somebody's cabbages. Then there is a meeting.

?? Are you for Jackson?
I am for Chicago. Jackson's men are the ones who hold the land office and the post office, so most of the town is for Jackson at least until the lots are paid for. The Whigs say the President is a tyrant. The Democrats say the Whigs are bankers. Both are partly right.

## a35-tm-teamster
roles: labourer
q: Where are those wagons going?

West, to the Fox River and the Rock River and Galena, with goods, and coming back with lead and grain. Every day a dozen teams go out on the Ottawa road and a dozen come in. I drive one of them, four yoke of oxen and a wagon full of salt and iron and whiskey. Three days to the Fox, if it does not rain.

?? What's the road like?
There is no road. There is a line of ruts across the prairie, and when the ruts get too deep we drive beside them and make new ones. In spring the sloughs are axle deep and you unload, carry the goods across, drag the wagon through empty and load again.

## a35-tm-butcher
roles: mechanic, merchant
q: Where does the meat come from?
meet: clybourne_archibald

Cattle are driven in from the prairie and from as far as Indiana and Ohio. Mr. Clybourne has the slaughterhouse up the North Branch and sells beef to the fort and the town. In the autumn they kill hogs by the thousand and pack them in salt in barrels for the boats east.

?? Is it a good business?
The best in Chicago after land, and land does not smell as bad. A man who can feed a town of three thousand that has no farms of its own will never be poor. Go and see Mr. Clybourne. He started selling beef to the soldiers before there was a town at all.

## a35-tm-gambler
roles: townsman
q: What do men do at night here?

Drink, play cards, argue about land, and drink. There are more taverns than churches and more card tables than both. On a Saturday night you can hear the fiddles from three houses at once, and a fight starts somewhere about eleven and finishes about half past.

?? Is there anything else?
There is a debating society that meets at the Lyceum and argues about whether the canal or the railroad is the future. There is a temperance society that meets on the same night, and loses members to the debating society, which serves punch. And there is the church, for those who are tired of the rest.

## a35-tm-prairie-farmer
roles: labourer, townsman
q: Are there farms around here?

A few, out along the North Branch and the Des Plaines and south towards the Calumet. The ground is the richest I have ever seen, black and deep, but it is so wet in spring and so hard to break that most men here would rather buy and sell it than plough it.

?? Will they farm it one day?
When the canal is dug and the land is drained and the price comes down, maybe. Right now a man can make more money selling a field in an afternoon than growing corn on it in five years. That is the trouble with this town in one sentence.

## a35-tm-fire
roles: townsman, mechanic
q: What happens if a house catches fire?

Everybody runs with buckets. There is no engine yet, and no company, only a law that every householder must keep two leather buckets and turn out when the alarm is given. The houses are all wood and close together and the wind off the prairie is strong. One bad night and the whole town could go.

?? Has it happened?
Not yet. Small fires, a chimney here and a shed there. But every winter somebody's stovepipe sets a roof going and the whole street forms a line from the river. One day they will need more than buckets. Most men here would rather not think about it.

## a35-tm-letters
roles: townsman, labourer, clerk
q: How do you get news from home?
meet: hogan_john_s_c

From Mr. Hogan's post office, which is a room behind his store. The mail comes overland from Detroit or round by the lake, once or twice a week if the roads are open. When it comes in, half the town is at the door, and he calls the names out like a schoolmaster.

?? Do you write home?
To my mother, every month. It costs twenty-five cents to send a letter that far, and she has to pay to receive it, so I write small and fill the page both ways. I tell her the town is a fine town. I do not tell her about the mud. Go and see Mr. Hogan. He knows everybody's business by the handwriting on their letters.

## a35-tm-tannery
roles: mechanic
q: What's that terrible smell?
meet: miller_john

The tannery, up the river. Hides soaking in lime and bark and worse in big pits, for weeks. They make leather for boots and harness and belts. A town of horses and oxen needs a lot of leather. A town of noses would rather it were made somewhere else.

?? Who owns it?
Mr. Miller, up on the North Branch, has one, and Mr. Hall has another. Good men, but nobody sits next to them in church. Go and see Mr. Miller if you can stand it. He will tell you that a man stops smelling it after a week. His wife says otherwise.

## a35-tm-church-building
roles: townsman, mechanic
q: Whose church is that?

That one is the Presbyterians', built two years ago, and the minister is Mr. Porter, who came with the soldiers from the Sault two summers ago. The Baptists meet down by the fort, the Methodists in a house, and the Catholics have Saint Mary's. The Episcopalians are building. Everyone has a church in Chicago except the people who most need one.

?? Do you go?
When it rains. When it is fine I go and look at lots. My wife says I have the worship of the wrong thing. She may be right.

## a35-tm-jackson
roles: townsman, labourer
q: What do you think of the President?

Old Hickory? He beat the British at New Orleans and the Bank in Washington, and he says the common man ought to have his say. Most of us here are common men, so most of us like him. He also wants every Indian east of the Mississippi moved west, and that has made a good deal of the land here available. Some men are glad of it. Some are quieter about it.

?? Will he be President long?
Until next year's election, and then his man Van Buren, they say. The Whigs here hate him. The Democrats here hold all the government posts. In Chicago, a man's politics are generally his job.

## a35-tm-schooner
roles: labourer, canadien
q: Are you a sailor?

Two years on the schooners, from Buffalo to Chicago and Green Bay. Salt, flour, whiskey and settlers out; hides and lead and grain back. The lake is harder than the sea, the old salts say: short steep waves, no room to run, and a lee shore all round.

?? What's the worst you've seen?
A November gale off the Manitous that put us on our beam ends for an hour. Two schooners went ashore that night. One of them was never found. Every sailor on these lakes knows a man who went down. We do not talk about them on board.

## a35-tm-carpenter-canadien
roles: mechanic, canadien
q: Where did you learn your trade?
meet: rc_laframboise_charles

In the Detroit shipyards, from my uncle, building bateaux and schooners. A boat and a house are not so different: keel and frame, ribs and planks. When the boats stopped needing me, the houses started. Now I build frame houses for Yankees who cannot pronounce my name.

?? Do you know the Laframboise family?
Everybody does. Charles is a carpenter like me, and his sister Madeleine keeps a boarding house, and Archange makes dresses for the Yankee ladies. Their family has been here since before the fort. Go and ask Charles. He will tell you where the old houses stood.

## a35-tm-black-resident
roles: black_resident
sex: m
q: How long have you lived in Chicago?

Three years. I came up from Kentucky by way of Indiana, with free papers in my pocket that I show to anybody who asks and some who do not. Illinois is a free state, they say, but it makes a man of my colour post a bond and carry a certificate. Free is a word with a lot of small print.

?? Is it better here?
Better than where I came from, yes. Nobody here can sell me. I have work, I am paid, and there is a man at the barber's and a woman at the laundry who know my name. The first man who ever lived here at this river was a man of colour, they say: Point de Sable, from the Indies. People forget that. I do not.

?? Do people treat you fairly?
Some do. The work treats me fairly: a wall I build stands as straight as any other man's. The rest, I keep my own counsel about, and I keep my papers dry.

## a35-tm-surveyor
roles: professional, townsman
q: What's that man doing with the chain?

Surveying. Every lot in this town has to be measured, staked and written down, and every one of them is being sold three times this summer. The surveyor goes out with his chain and his compass and his boy with the stakes. Half of them are measuring land that is under water half the year.

?? Is it accurate?
Accurate enough to start a lawsuit. Every stake is somebody's fortune, and every surveyor's mistake is somebody else's. The old settlers' fences were put where the cows wanted them, and the new lines go where the map says. When the two meet, there is always a fence in somebody's yard.

## a35-tm-saloon
roles: labourer, irish, canadien
sex: m
q: Where's the best whiskey in town?

Depends what you want from it. If you want company, the Sauganash, where Mark Beaubien will play the fiddle. If you want it cheap, any shanty by the river. If you want to stay alive, avoid the fellow with one eye down by the landing who sells it out of a barrel he swears is from Kentucky.

?? What's in his barrel?
River water, raw spirit, a plug of tobacco for the colour and a handful of red pepper for the bite. Men drink it and swear it is the best. In the morning they swear otherwise.

## a35-tm-old-settler
roles: townsman
q: Who was here before the boom?
meet: kinzie_john_h

The Kinzies, the Beaubiens, the Ouilmettes, the Laframboises, a few soldiers, the traders and their families. A hundred people, perhaps, in thirty-one. Now there are three thousand. The old settlers stand at the corner and watch the new ones arrive like a man watching a flood rise over his field.

?? Do they like the newcomers?
The newcomers made them rich, so they cannot complain. But young Kinzie remembers when this was all fields and the Potawatomi camped by the river. Ask him what he thinks of it all. He is too polite to say all of it.

## a35-tm-stage
roles: townsman, labourer
q: Is there a stage coach?

There is one to Ottawa and one to Detroit, by way of Niles, when the roads let it go. It is a mud wagon on springs with leather curtains. You sit on a board, you are thrown against the roof at every rut, and when the wheels stick the passengers get out and push.

?? Would you recommend it?
Only to an enemy. Take a schooner if you are going east. Take a horse if you are going west. Take the stage only if you have no friends and no money and want to lose both.

## a35-tm-loan
roles: merchant, professional
q: Can a man borrow money here?

Easily, and that is the trouble. There is no bank in Chicago yet, but there will be, and meanwhile every merchant gives credit and every land buyer gives notes. A man who owes a thousand dollars and owns lots worth five thousand feels very rich indeed. A man who owes a thousand dollars and owns lots that nobody wants is in trouble.

?? When will the bank come?
They are talking about a branch of the State Bank this year. The merchants want it. The Jackson men do not trust banks. Either way, one day this town will have to pay what it owes. I hope I am somewhere else that day.

## a35-tm-portage-old
roles: canadien, townsman
q: Did you ever cross the old portage?
meet: hubbard_gurdon

Many times, as a young man, with the fur brigades. From the South Branch up through Mud Lake to the Des Plaines. In spring you paddled most of the way. In summer you waded and pulled and swore. It is the same line where the canal will go, they say. Somebody has finally listened to the voyageurs.

?? Who else crossed it?
Every trader who ever came this way. Mr. Hubbard did it for years with the American Fur Company before he went into pork. Ask him about the time the boats froze in on the Illinois. He tells it with his hands.
