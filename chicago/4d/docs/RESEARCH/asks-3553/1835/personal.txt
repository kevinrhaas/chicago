# The people of 1835 answer for themselves, on 1 July. Their own stories
# (tales-3553/1835/personal.txt) are not retold here; these questions reach for what is left.
# Reconstruction, liberty L-tales-3553: what is known of each life, in a voice of our making.

## a35-mark-sauganash
for: beaubien_mark
q: Why did you name your hotel the Sauganash?
meet: caldwell_billy

For Billy Caldwell, my friend, whom the Potawatomi call Sauganash, the Englishman. He is the best-read man in this country and the worst dancer, and he has stayed at my house more nights than he has at his own. When I painted the name on the front, he stood in the mud and read it twice and did not say anything, and then he bought everybody a drink.

?? Do the guests like it?
The guests sleep three to a bed and two on the floor, and in the morning they come down and eat, and I play them a tune, and they forget about the beds. I have had a governor in my house and a horse thief, sometimes on the same night. Go and ask Billy what he thinks of the name. He will say he is honoured. He means: why not the Caldwell House?

?? Will you build a bigger one?
Everybody else is building bigger. The Couch brothers have the Tremont with its fine beds. Let them. Nobody ever went to the Tremont for the fiddle.

## a35-mark-ferry
for: beaubien_mark
q: Did you really run the ferry?
meet: beaubien_jean_baptiste

For a while, across the river by my house, in a scow. A few pennies a man and more for a wagon. I would row them over and play them a tune on the other side if they paid double. Now there is the drawbridge and I am a poor man again, which is to say I am exactly as poor as before.

?? Is your brother proud of you?
My brother the Colonel thinks I should be more serious. I think he should be less. He has the whole fort reservation in a receipt in his pocket and the major breathing on his neck, and I have a fiddle. Ask him which of us sleeps better. I can tell you: it is not him.

## a35-jb-families
for: beaubien_jean_baptiste
q: How long have you been in Chicago?
meet: beaubien_josette

I first came through as a young trader for the Mackinac houses, more than twenty-five years ago, and I have lived here since before the war ended. My first wife was Ottawa, and my second is Josette, whom you may know. Between them I have so many children that I count at supper and still miss one.

?? How did you meet Josette?
She was the Kinzie children's nurse, in the old fort's time, a girl of the Laframboise family. After the war I came back and there she was, grown, with that look she has that tells you she has already decided. Go and see her. She will tell you I proposed. I remember it the other way round.

?? What's in your receipt?
That on such a day this spring, Jean Baptiste Beaubien entered the land called the Fort Dearborn reservation at the land office of the United States, and paid for it in full. Ninety-four dollars and sixty-one cents. The major says it is worth nothing. Then why is he so angry?

## a35-josette-today
for: beaubien_josette
q: What do you think of the town now?
meet: rc_laframboise_madeleine

It is too loud and too fast and full of people who do not say good morning. When I was a girl at the old fort, I knew every face on this river. Now I walk down Lake Street and do not know one in ten. But my children will be rich in it, perhaps, so I do not complain too loudly.

?? Are your relatives still here?
My Laframboise cousins keep boarding houses and make dresses and build houses. Madeleine has a house full of young men who eat like wolves. Go and see her. She is the only person in Chicago who can make a land speculator wash his hands before supper.

## a35-johnh-agent
for: kinzie_john_h
q: What does an Indian agent do?
meet: caldwell_billy

I was the government's agent at Fort Winnebago for the Winnebago nation: hand out the annuity money and goods, settle quarrels, keep the peace, write a great many reports. Now I am here in Chicago, in business, and helping where I can with the payment and the removal of the Potawatomi.

?? Is the removal fair?
I have known these people all my life. I sat in a boat with my nurse at the river mouth in the summer of 1812 while a Potawatomi chief stood guard over us. I will not tell you the treaty is fair. I will tell you it was the best that men like Caldwell could get from a government that wanted all of it. Go and ask him. He will be more honest than I can afford to be.

?? Do you miss Fort Winnebago?
I miss the quiet. I do not miss the winters. My wife says the quiet was worth it. She wrote everything down there. She says one day she will make a book of it.

## a35-juliette-supper
for: kinzie_juliette
q: Who comes to your suppers?
meet: hubbard_gurdon

Everyone who matters and some who think they do. The officers from the fort, the doctors, Mr. Hubbard, the Beaubiens, any traveller with a letter of introduction, and once a missionary who stayed six weeks. I give them venison and whitefish and wild rice and whatever came on the last boat. And I make them sing.

?? What does Mr. Hubbard sing?
Voyageur songs, in French, with his eyes shut, because he learned them in the canoes when he was sixteen. Then he tells the story of the ride to Danville and every time the miles get longer. Go and ask him how far it was. Then ask me afterwards.

## a35-juliette-mother
for: kinzie_juliette
q: What did your husband's mother tell you?
meet: beaubien_josette

Everything about the old fort and the summer of 1812: the council, the medal, the boat, the chiefs who protected the family. She made me promise to write it down one day, all of it, so it would not be lost. I have been taking notes ever since. Some of what she said I can hardly bear to write.

?? Are there others who remember?
Josette Beaubien was the children's nurse in that boat. She remembers more than she says. Go and ask her, gently. She will tell you about the morning on the water. She will not tell you about the afternoon.

## a35-caldwell-justice
for: caldwell_billy
q: What's it like being a justice of the peace?
meet: beaubien_mark

I marry people, I hear small quarrels about pigs and debts and whose fence is where, and I fine drunks. It is the most American thing that has ever happened to me. A man who rode with Tecumseh, now telling a Yankee he must pay for his neighbour's cabbages.

?? Will you go west with your people?
I have a reservation here on the North Branch, by the treaty, and I could stay. But I spoke for the bands, and I signed for them, and I think I should go with them, to see that what was promised is given. That is what a man owes the people he spoke for. Go and see Mark at the Sauganash. He has been trying to make me stay with music for a month.

## a35-robinson-mackinac
for: robinson_alexander
q: How did you get the wounded captain to Mackinac?
meet: kinzie_john_h

In a canoe, my wife and I, after the battle in 1812, with Captain Heald wounded and his wife wounded worse, up the whole length of the lake, three hundred miles. We paddled in the day and hid at night. The British commandant at Mackinac took them in. They lived. I was paid in thanks.

?? Why did you help them?
Because they were alive and somebody had to. I was a trader; I knew the Healds; it was a canoe and a lake. Afterwards some people on both sides said I should have done otherwise. Young Kinzie remembers those days. Go and ask him who else helped. There were more of us than the stories say.

## a35-hubbard-packing
for: hubbard_gurdon
q: What's in your warehouse?
meet: dole_george_w

Pork, in barrels, salted and packed for the boats east. Hides. Some furs still. Flour. Salt from Syracuse. Whatever the town needs and whatever the country sends. I built the first brick building of any size in Chicago for it, and they laughed at me. They are not laughing now.

?? How did you start packing?
George Dole packed the first barrels out of here in thirty-two and showed it could be done. I packed hogs on a bigger scale, and they called my warehouse Hubbard's Folly because nobody could imagine so many hogs. Go and ask Dole what he thinks of my hogs. He will say mine are fatter. He is being kind.

## a35-calhoun-paper
for: calhoun_john
q: What do you think of the new paper in town?
meet: davis_t_o

The American? Mr. Davis started it last month and calls it the voice of the people, by which he means the Whigs. Good luck to him. There is room in this town for two papers and fifty opinions. There is not room for two papers' worth of advertisements, and he is learning that already.

?? Are you friends?
We are rivals, which in the newspaper business is better than friends. We drink at the same tavern and we insult each other in print and we borrow each other's type when the boat is late. Go and ask him how his paper supply is holding up. Mine is low. His is lower.

## a35-hogan-letters
for: hogan_john_s_c
q: What's the strangest letter you've had?
meet: rc_mahoney_patrick

A letter addressed To My Son, Chicago, Illinois, in an old woman's hand from Ireland, with no name at all. I put it in the window with a note. Four men came to claim it. None of them could say what was in it. In the end a young Irishman named Mahoney read the first line over my shoulder and started to cry, and I gave it to him.

?? Was it really his?
I believe so. He said nobody else's mother called her son a gom. Go and ask him. He may tell you what she said. He may tell you she sent a holy medal, which I am sure she did not, because I felt the envelope.

## a35-carpenter-sunday
for: carpenter_philo
q: How many children come to your Sunday school?
meet: watkins_john

Forty or fifty, some Sundays, from every kind of family: Yankee, French, Irish, a few Potawatomi from the camps. I teach them their letters from the Bible, and some hymns, and how to sit still for an hour, which is the hardest lesson of all in Chicago.

?? Who helps you?
Mr. Porter, the Presbyterian minister, and his new wife, who was Miss Chappel, the schoolteacher. Between us we have taught half the children of this town to read. Mr. Watkins, the first schoolmaster, sends me his scholars on Sundays. Go and ask him which of them learned their letters from the Bible and which from his charcoal boards.

## a35-watkins-pupils
for: watkins_john
q: What became of your first pupils?
meet: beaubien_mark

Some are clerks now, some are married, some have gone west with their families. One of the Beaubien boys keeps the books in a store now, if you can imagine it. Mark Beaubien sent three of his children to me, and every one of them could play the fiddle better than he could read.

?? Do you still teach?
A little. There are more teachers now, and a real school house is promised. I am glad to be the first and gladder not to be the only. Go and ask Mark what he thinks of schooling. He will tell you a fiddle never failed him.

## a35-dole-store
for: dole_george_w
q: What's the secret of a good packing house?
meet: clybourne_archibald

Clean salt, tight barrels, cold weather, and buy your cattle before everybody else does. I buy from the drovers who bring them in from the prairie and from Mr. Clybourne up the North Branch. He was supplying the fort before I came. Go and ask him what a steer cost in twenty-nine and what it costs now.

?? Will Chicago be a big meat town?
The prairie is grass and the grass feeds cattle and the cattle walk here to the lake. Once there is a canal and a railroad, every animal from here to the Mississippi will come through this town. I may not live to see it, but I can see it from here.

## a35-clybourne-treaty
for: clybourne_archibald
q: What was it like feeding the treaty?
meet: caldwell_billy

Every day for two weeks I killed cattle from dawn and my men cut and carried. Eight thousand people, Potawatomi and Ottawa and Ojibwe, camped all round the town, and the government paid me to feed them while the chiefs and the commissioners talked. I never saw so much meat go so fast, nor so many people in one place.

?? Did you see the signing?
I saw the tent and the chiefs going in and out, and Caldwell and Robinson walking up and down talking to everybody. When it was signed there was a great deal of noise and then a great quiet. Go and ask Caldwell what it felt like inside the tent. I only know how it felt at the slaughterhouse.

## a35-ogden-water
for: ogden_w_b
q: Are you staying in Chicago?
meet: hamilton_richard_j

I came for a month and I have stayed a month and I am beginning to think I will stay longer. I sold a third of my brother-in-law's land last week for more than he paid for the whole. The rest I am holding. I wrote to him: send me more land.

?? What does the town need most?
A railroad, a canal, a drained street and an honest county record. Mr. Hamilton keeps the records, and he is honest, and he has six jobs. Go and see him. Ask him how many deeds he recorded today. Then ask him if he has eaten.

## a35-newberry-north
for: newberry_walter_loomis
q: Why buy land where there's nothing?
meet: kinzie_john_h

Because there will be something. The town is crowded on the south side by the fort and the river, and it has nowhere to go but north and west. Kinzie's addition on the north bank is already being sold off. My acres are beyond that, out on the prairie. In ten years they will be streets.

?? What if you're wrong?
Then I will have a very large farm. A man who buys at a few dollars an acre can afford to be wrong. The men buying on Lake Street at two thousand a lot cannot. Go and ask young Kinzie. His father's land on the north side is being cut into lots this summer, and he knows what it was.

## a35-hamilton-offices
for: hamilton_richard_j
q: How do you do six jobs at once?
meet: caton_john_dean

By writing very fast and never sleeping. In the morning I record deeds, at noon I hear probate, in the afternoon I am school commissioner, in the evening I am the county clerk. I have one desk and I change hats. Sometimes I forget which hat I have on and sell a school lot to a dead man.

?? Who brings you the most work?
The lawyers, and young Caton most of all. He brings me deeds with three owners and wills with no witnesses and smiles as if it were a gift. Go and see him. He owes me a dinner.

## a35-kimberly-ague
for: kimberly_edmund_s
q: What actually causes the ague?
meet: egan_william_b

Nobody knows, though everybody has a theory. Bad air off the marshes, the doctors say: miasma. The old settlers say it is the new ground being broken, the smell of the turned earth. The Potawatomi say it is the season. All I know is quinine helps, and that it comes worst in late summer when the sloughs go low.

?? Do you and Dr. Egan agree?
We agree about nothing except the price of quinine, which is too high. He thinks I am too cautious and I think he is too cheerful. Go and ask him. He will make a speech about it and sing a song at the end.

## a35-egan-irish
for: egan_william_b
q: Do you treat the Irish workers?
meet: rc_flynn_mary

I treat everybody, and the Irish come to me because I am one of them. They pay in eggs and promises and sometimes a bottle. A girl came to me last week with her hands so cracked from the washing that they bled. Mary Flynn, a good girl in a lawyer's house. I gave her goose grease and told her to complain. She will not.

?? Is it hard being Irish here?
For me, a doctor with a good coat, it is a joke at dinner. For the men with the shovels it is no joke. The Yankees want their work and not their company. That will change. There will be too many of us to ignore.

## a35-greene-desertion
for: greene_john
q: How many men have you lost to the town?
meet: allen_lieut_james

Seven this spring. They go over the pickets at night with their boots in their hands and by morning they are carpenters on Lake Street calling themselves something else. The town hides them. A carpenter is worth more to Chicago than a private is to me.

?? What will happen to the fort?
The War Department will close it, I expect, and soon. It guards nothing but itself now. When it goes, Lieutenant Allen's harbour will be the only thing the army has here. Go and talk to him. His work will last. Mine will be a story.

## a35-allen-sand
for: allen_lieut_james
q: What's the biggest problem with the harbour?
meet: greene_john

Sand. The current along this shore carries sand south, and every storm piles it against the north pier and pushes it round the end into the channel. I dredge, I extend the pier, the sand follows. It is a race between the government's money and the lake's patience, and the lake has more of both.

?? Will you win?
In the end, yes, if Congress keeps paying. A harbour here will make this the greatest port on the lakes. I write that to Washington every month. The major says I write more letters than he does. He is right. Mine have estimates in them.

## a35-peck-early
for: peck_philip
q: What was Chicago like when you came?
meet: rc_bardwell_otis

Four years ago it was the fort, a few dozen log houses, some French families, and a great deal of nothing. My store was the first frame building on South Water Street and people walked out to look at it. Now I cannot see it for the buildings round it.

?? Who carries your goods?
Otis Bardwell and his horses, mostly. He is the best teamster in town and he will tell you so. His horses are better than most men's children. Go and ask him their names. You will hear about them for an hour.

## a35-flynn-brothers
for: rc_flynn_mary
q: Where are your brothers now?
meet: rc_mahoney_patrick

One on the harbour, driving piles for Lieutenant Allen. One gone to the lead mines at Galena. The youngest, God help him, buying and selling lots like a Yankee, with money he does not have. I see the eldest at Mass on Sundays. The others I hear about.

?? Do you know Patrick Mahoney?
Everybody knows Patrick Mahoney. He is the luckiest man in Chicago and the worst at keeping his money. He fell off a roof into a hay wagon and found a dollar in the mud the same week. Go and ask him to tell it. He tells it better every time, and I correct him.

## a35-mahoney-medal
for: rc_mahoney_patrick
q: Can I see your lucky medal?
meet: hogan_john_s_c

Here it is, round my neck, the Blessed Virgin, a bit worn from my thumb. My mother gave it to me on the quay at Cork. I have touched it every morning since. I touched it before I fell off that roof, and look what happened. Landed soft as a baby.

?? Did your mother write?
She did, and the letter found me, which is a miracle in itself. Mr. Hogan at the post office will tell you how. She said I was a gom and to keep away from roofs. I have not been on one since. Go and thank Mr. Hogan for me. I never did it properly.

## a35-bardwell-horses
for: rc_bardwell_otis
q: What makes Dan and Duke so special?
meet: rc_mahoney_patrick

They are matched bays out of Kentucky, sixteen hands, and they pull together like one horse. Dan is the thinker; he looks at a mud hole and goes round it. Duke is the strong one; he goes through it. Between them they have never left a load behind, except the bricks, and that was the road's fault.

?? Is it true about the hay wagon?
I was the man driving it. I did not notice a thing. A man fell two storeys into my hay and I drove him half a mile before he climbed out and thanked me. That is Patrick Mahoney. Go and ask him for the rest. Tell him I want a share of his luck.

## a35-caton-jury
for: caton_john_dean
q: What's the most ridiculous case you've had?
meet: hamilton_richard_j

A man sued his neighbour because the neighbour's pig ate his deed. The deed was in his coat and the coat was on a fence and the pig was hungry. We had to prove the deed existed without the deed. Mr. Hamilton had recorded it, thank God, or the pig would own the lot.

?? Who won?
My client got his lot and the neighbour paid for a new coat. The pig was not charged. Go and ask Mr. Hamilton to show you the record. He keeps everything. Even a pig's appetite is a matter of public record in Chicago.

## a35-davis-american
for: davis_t_o
q: Why start a second newspaper?
meet: calhoun_john

Because a town of three thousand with one paper hears only one opinion, and the opinion of the Democrat is that whatever the President does is right. The American is for the canal, for improvement, for a harbour and a railroad, and against men who hold every office and still want more.

?? Is it working?
We have subscribers and advertisers and enemies, which is everything a paper needs. Mr. Calhoun is a gentleman and a printer and I respect him. Go and tell him I said so. He will not believe you.

## a35-couch-tremont
for: couch_ira
q: How did you build the Tremont?
meet: beaubien_mark

My brother James and I put it up two years ago at Lake and Dearborn: three storeys of frame, painted, with a real dining room and beds with real mattresses. People said it was too grand for Chicago. It has been full every night since the land sale started.

?? Who are your rivals?
Mark Beaubien at the Sauganash, who has a fiddle where I have a mattress. We send each other our overflow. When I am full, I send them to him, and he plays them to sleep. Go and see him and tell him I have three empty beds tonight. He will laugh. I never do.



## a35-st-cyr-church
for: st_cyr_john_mary
q: How did St. Mary's come to be built?
meet: beaubien_jean_baptiste

The French Catholics of this town wrote to the Bishop at St. Louis two years ago asking for a priest, and the Bishop sent me. We built a little frame church, twenty-five feet by thirty-five, by the river, for four hundred dollars. The Beaubiens gave most of the money and the Irish gave their backs.

?? Who comes to Mass?
The French, the Irish, a few Germans, some Potawatomi, and the occasional Yankee who has lost a bet. I say the Mass in Latin, preach in French and English, and hear confession in four languages, badly in two of them. Go and thank Colonel Beaubien for me. He has never let me want for wood in winter.
