## a04-md-day
roles: maid
q: What time do you start in the morning?

Half past five, before the family stirs. Fires laid in the grates even in summer for the damp, the front steps scrubbed, the brass on the door polished, the dining room swept and the breakfast table laid, all before eight. Then the bedrooms when the family has gone down, and the stairs, and the silver.

?? When do you finish?
When the family has gone up and the last tray has come down, ten or eleven. Thursday afternoon and every other Sunday are mine. On Thursday the whole Avenue's maids are out on Michigan Avenue at once, in our best hats. The mistresses on the Avenue have no idea how much we know about each other's houses.

?? What do you do on your afternoon?
The Lake Front, if it is fine, or the shops on State Street just to look, or the vaudeville. Since the Iroquois burned last December, none of us goes near a big theatre. I go to the park and sit by the water and do nothing at all, which is the finest thing in the world.

## a04-md-iroquois
roles: maid, cook, laundress, lady
q: Did you hear about the Iroquois Theatre fire?
meet: pa1904_shortall_john_g

Everyone in Chicago knows somebody. It was the thirtieth of December, a matinee, the house packed with mothers and children for the Christmas pantomime, when the scenery caught fire and the curtain did not come down and the doors opened inwards. Six hundred people died in a quarter of an hour. Mostly women and children.

?? Did you know anyone?
A girl who was in service two houses down took her mistress's children. They came home. She did not; she went back in for the youngest and the crowd took her. Her mistress paid for the funeral. I still pass the house and look up at the window where she used to sit.

?? What has changed since?
Every theatre in the city was shut while the inspectors went round, and they have found exits nailed shut and no water in the pipes and fire curtains made of nothing. Mr. Shortall of the Humane Society has made the children of this city his work for years. Ask him what has been done since. He will tell you not enough.

## a04-md-bells
roles: maid, manservant
q: How do you know when you are wanted?

The bells. There is a board in the servants' hall with a little flag or a bell for every room, and when the mistress pulls the cord in the library, the library bell rings and its flag drops. In the newer houses there are speaking tubes, and now the telephone. But the bells are what I hear in my sleep.

?? Do they ring at night?
When somebody is ill, or wants a hot drink, or has heard a noise. The master's bell is the one we fear. Two short rings means come at once. One long means he has lost his spectacles again.

## a04-md-irish
roles: maid
age: -40
origin: irish
q: Where are you from?

County Mayo, by way of Queenstown and New York, six years ago. My aunt was in service on the North Side and she found me my first place. Now there are four of us from the same parish on Prairie Avenue, and the cook in the house across the street is my second cousin.

?? Do you send money home?
Every month, to my mother, by the money order at the post office. Three dollars of my sixteen. My brother is coming over next spring, I hope, and I am saving for his passage. He wants to be a policeman. Half the police in Chicago are from Mayo, so he may manage it.

?? Do you go to church?
Every Sunday morning, the early Mass, before the family wakes. Then I come back and serve the family breakfast before they go to their own church. God gets me first.

## a04-md-swedish
roles: maid, laundress
sex: f
origin: swedish
q: Is it hard to learn English?

Very hard, at first. I came from Småland four years ago and I knew only yes and thank you. The mistress spoke to me slowly, as if I were deaf, and pointed at things. Now I can read the newspaper. The cook is Irish and laughs at my words, and I laugh at hers.

?? Are there many Swedes here?
On the North Side, a whole city of Swedes: churches, shops, newspapers in Swedish, dances on Saturday. On Prairie Avenue the girls are Irish and Swedish and German and Norwegian, and we talk to each other in a sort of English that nobody upstairs could follow.

## a04-md-glessner
roles: maid
q: What's the house at the corner of Eighteenth like?
meet: glessner_williamson_martha_l

The Glessner house? Like a castle that turned its back on the street. From the Avenue it is all granite and small windows, and the girls who go by say it must be very dark inside. It is not. Everything opens onto the court behind, full of light and ivy. It is the strangest house on the Avenue and the best to work in, they say.

?? Who works there?
The cook, Mattie Williamson, who knows the mistress's book of dinners better than the mistress does. The maids. The men in the stable. Mrs. Glessner writes everything in her journal, the girls say: who came, who said what, what was eaten. Go and ask Mattie what Mr. Thomas the musician likes for dinner.

## a04-md-wages
roles: maid, manservant, laundress
q: Is it good money, service on the Avenue?

Four dollars a week for a housemaid with her room and board. A cook gets more, six or seven. A butler more again. A girl in a factory gets as much, but she pays her own rent and her own dinner, and she breathes the factory all day. Here at least the air is the lake's.

?? Would you rather work in a shop?
At Field's? The shop girls stand all day and have their wages docked if they are a penny short. But they go home at night and nobody rings for them. Some days I envy them. Most days I eat better than they do.

## a04-md-stairs
roles: maid
q: Where do the servants sleep?

At the top of the house, in the little rooms under the roof, two to a room, hot in summer and cold in winter. We go up and down by the back stairs, never the front, and we are never to be seen on the front stairs except to clean them, before the family wakes.

?? What's in your room?
A bed, a washstand, a chair, a chest of drawers, a picture of home and a photograph of my mother. The window looks over the alley to the carriage houses. On summer nights I can hear the horses moving in their stalls, and the trains on the lakefront, and nothing else.

## a04-md-fair
roles: maid, manservant, young_lady, young_man, lady, gentleman
age: 15-
meet: pa1904_kimball_william_w
q: Are you going to the World's Fair at St. Louis?

Everybody wants to go. It opened in April for the Louisiana Purchase, a hundred years of it, and they say it is bigger than our own Fair of ninety-three. A palace of electricity, a giant bird cage, and Mr. Ferris's own wheel from our Fair, carried down and set up again, and people from the whole world in villages you can walk through.

?? Who's been already?
Some of the families on the Avenue went down in May in their private cars. The servants who went came back with picture postcards and stories about ice cream served in a rolled waffle, which you eat cone and all. Mr. Kimball took prizes for his pianos at our own Fair in ninety-three. Ask him if he has sent any to St. Louis.

?? Do you remember the Fair of ninety-three?
The White City! I went three times. The lights at night on the lagoon were like nothing in the world. Everybody in Chicago says St. Louis will never match it. People from St. Louis say otherwise. They would.

## a04-md-silver
roles: maid, manservant
q: How long does it take to clean the silver?

A whole morning every week, two of us, with whiting and ammonia and soft cloths, in the butler's pantry with the door shut. Forks, spoons, salvers, candlesticks, the tea service, the epergne for the centre of the table. Every piece is counted out and counted back.

?? Does anything go missing?
Not in this house. In a house two doors down, last year, a spoon was missed, and a girl was turned off without a character. The spoon was found in the soup tureen a month later. Nobody went to tell the girl. I think about her.

## a04-md-phone
roles: maid, manservant
q: Does the house have a telephone?

In the back hall, on the wall, with a crank and a little earpiece on a cord. The master uses it to call his office and the mistress to call the grocer. I answer it. You say the number of the house and then wait while somebody shouts at you from the other end. I was frightened of it for a month.

?? Who calls most?
The young ladies, calling each other about dances. Their mother says it is not ladylike to speak on the telephone. They do it anyway, in the back hall, in whispers, and I am to say they are out if their mother asks.

## a04-ck-kitchen
roles: cook
q: What's for dinner tonight?

A clear soup, a fish, a joint of lamb with new peas, a salad and a lemon ice, for six, with the master's brother and his wife. The fish came from the market on South Water Street at six this morning on the grocer's wagon. The ice is in the box. The peas the scullery maid is shelling now, and eating one in three.

?? What's your best dish?
A chicken pie that the master asks for every Sunday and is not supposed to have, because the doctor says he is too stout. On Sunday I make it anyway. The mistress pretends not to notice. Upstairs is full of things everyone pretends not to notice.

?? Do you have a gas range?
A big coal range, and a gas stove beside it for quick things, which the mistress had put in last year. I was afraid of it at first. Now I would not give it up. The coal range takes an hour to come up; the gas comes up with a match.

## a04-ck-ice
roles: cook
q: How do you keep food cold in summer?

The ice box, an oak chest lined with zinc, with a block of lake ice in the top that the ice man brings every morning, a hundred pounds on his back with tongs. The cold falls down onto the butter and the milk and the meat. The water drips into a pan that the scullery maid empties, if she remembers.

?? Where does the ice come from?
From the lakes and ponds north of the city, cut in the winter in great blocks and stored in ice houses packed in sawdust. In July the ice man is the most popular man on the Avenue. The children follow his wagon for the chips.

## a04-ck-market
roles: cook
q: Where do you buy food?

The grocer and the butcher come to the back door every morning for the order, and deliver by noon. For fish and fruit and the best vegetables I go to the South Water Street market myself once a week, early, with the coachman to drive me. It is a mile of wagons and shouting and crates and horses.

?? Do you haggle?
Of course. The market men know me and I know them. A cook who pays the first price is a cook who does not care for her master's money, and he hears of it. A cook who drives a hard bargain is a cook the market respects.

## a04-ck-dinner-party
roles: cook
q: What's the biggest dinner you've ever cooked?

Twenty-four, for a dinner after the opera, with nine courses, oysters to ices. I had three girls extra for the day and a man from the caterer's for the ices. We started at six in the morning. The last plates came down at one. I sat on the back stairs and could not lift my arms.

?? Who was there?
Half the Avenue and some men from the orchestra. Mr. Thomas the conductor was one. He came down to the kitchen afterwards to thank me himself, which no guest had ever done. I have been his servant ever since, in my heart.

## a04-cm-horses
roles: coachman
q: How many horses do you keep?

Six in the stable behind the house: two pairs of carriage horses, bays, matched to the hair, a cob for the light trap, and the master's saddle horse that he does not ride as often as he should. I feed them at five, groom them by seven, and harness by nine for the master's drive to his office.

?? What do you drive?
A brougham for the master in town, a victoria for the mistress on fine afternoons in the park, and a big closed carriage for the opera and the theatre. Each has its own harness and its own lamps, and every bit of brass must shine like a mirror. A dirty carriage is the coachman's shame.

?? Are the automobiles a threat?
There are a few now on Michigan Avenue, smelling and banging and frightening the horses. One of the young men on the Avenue has one. The horses hate them, and so do I. The master says they are a fad. I hope he is right. A motor car needs a mechanic, not a coachman.

## a04-cm-coach-house
roles: coachman
q: Where do you live?

Over the carriage house, behind the main house, with my wife and two children, in four rooms over the stalls. Warm in winter from the horses below, and on summer nights the smell comes up through the floor. My boys go to sleep to the sound of the horses chewing. They think it is the best sound in the world.

?? Do the families' coachmen know each other?
We are a club. Every carriage house along the alley has a coachman, and we stand in the alley in the evening, smoking, and talk about horses and masters. We know whose horse is lame, whose groom drinks, and whose master is going broke before his banker does.

## a04-cm-park
roles: coachman
q: Where's the nicest drive in Chicago?
meet: pa1904_field_marshall

Along the lake on the boulevards, out to Washington Park and Jackson Park, where the Fair was. On a summer afternoon every carriage on the Avenue is out there, the ladies in their summer gowns, the coachmen in their summer livery, all going at a walk so everybody can see everybody else.

?? Who has the finest turnout?
Mr. Field's, of course: a pair of blacks that cost more than my house. But Mr. Field himself prefers to walk to his store every morning, three miles, in all weathers. A man with the finest horses in Chicago who walks. That is a man who knows what he is.

## a04-cm-winter
roles: coachman
q: What's the worst part of the job?

Waiting. Outside the opera, outside a dinner, outside a ball, in January, on the box, with a buffalo robe and a lantern, until two in the morning. The horses stand with their blankets on and their breath smoking and I stand with them. You get to know a horse well on those nights.

?? And the best?
Driving a pair of good horses fast on the lake shore on a crisp morning, with the sun coming up out of the water, and nobody in the carriage at all because I am exercising them. Then the horses and I are the masters of the Avenue.

## a04-cm-groom
roles: coachman
age: 30-
origin: irish
q: How did you learn about horses?

On my father's farm in Kerry, and then as a stable boy for an English family in Dublin, and then in the livery stables of New York, and then here. Twenty years of horses. I can tell a horse's age by its teeth and its temper by its ears, and a bad groom by the horse's coat.

?? What makes a good carriage horse?
Steady nerves above all. A carriage horse in Chicago must stand still for cable cars, elevated trains, fire engines, brass bands and the motor cars. He must go at a walk among a thousand other horses without kicking any of them. A horse like that is worth more than a racehorse.

## a04-ld-laundry
roles: laundress
q: What day is wash day?

Monday, every Monday, in the laundry in the basement, with three set tubs, a copper boiler on the range, a wringer, and a drying room with racks for when it rains. The whole house's linen, every sheet, napkin, collar and petticoat. Tuesday is ironing. Wednesday is mending.

?? How long does it take?
From six in the morning to six at night, two of us. The white things boiled, the coloured things in warm water, the woollens in cool, the master's collars starched stiff enough to cut a finger. By evening the steam has taken the curl out of my hair and the skin off my knuckles.

## a04-ld-irons
roles: laundress
q: How do you iron so many shirts?

With six flat irons heating on the stove at once, using one while the others warm. A shirt front takes a quarter of an hour if it is to be done properly, with the starch. The master wears a fresh shirt every day and two on the days he dines out. That is ten shirts a week for him alone.

?? Have you seen the new electric irons?
In the shop window at Field's. They plug into the wall where the light goes. The mistress says perhaps next year. I say I will believe it when it does not set the house on fire.

## a04-nu-children
roles: nursemaid
q: Whose children do you look after?

Two little ones, a boy of four and a girl of two, in the nursery on the third floor. I wake them, wash them, dress them, give them their breakfast, walk them in the park, give them their dinner, put them down for their sleep, and bring them down to the drawing room at five to see their mother for half an hour in their best clothes.

?? Do they love their mother?
They love her the way you love a beautiful lady who comes to see you. They love me the way you love bread. When the boy falls down, he calls for me. I try not to be proud of that.

?? Where do you walk them?
To the lake shore, or down the Avenue to the statue at Eighteenth Street, the bronze of the Indian chief saving the lady. The boy asks about it every time and I tell him what the policeman on the corner told me: that it was a battle long ago, when Chicago was a fort, and the chief was a good man.

## a04-nu-governess
roles: nursemaid
q: Do you teach the children too?

Their letters, their prayers, their manners, a little French. When they are six they will have a governess for lessons, and then school. My job is to make them good children; the governess's will be to make them clever. I think mine is the harder.

?? Are they good?
They are children. The boy bit his sister on Tuesday and she pulled his hair on Wednesday, and on Thursday they were best friends again. That is not badness. That is practice.

## a04-hk-keys
roles: housekeeper
age: 35-
q: What does a housekeeper do?

Everything that keeps the house running, and nothing that anyone sees. I keep the keys to the linen, the china, the stores and the wine. I hire and train the maids, order the coal and the soap and the candles, keep the accounts, and see that every room is ready before anyone wants it.

?? What's the hardest part?
The maids. A good girl is hard to find and harder to keep. She marries, or goes to a better place, or goes back to Ireland to bury her mother. In fifteen years I have trained thirty girls. Some of them are cooks now in houses grander than this one. I count that as my wages.

## a04-ms-butler
roles: butler
q: What does a butler do all day?

Answer the door, receive the cards, announce the guests, serve the wine, carve at the sideboard, keep the silver, wind the clocks, hire the footmen, see that every man in the house does his work. A good butler is like a good clock: nobody notices him until he stops.

?? Have you served anyone famous?
I have announced a Vice-President, three senators, two English lords and the President of the University. And Mr. Pullman, before he died, who walked in as if he owned the house as well. The great ones are easier than the rich ones. The great ones say thank you.

## a04-ms-furnace
roles: houseman
q: What do you do in this house?

I am the house man. I shovel coal into the furnace in winter, carry the ashes out, wash the windows, beat the carpets, carry the trunks, mend what breaks, and do whatever heavy work the maids cannot. In summer I wash the house down and paint the iron fence.

?? Do you have a family?
A wife and three children in a flat off Twenty-second Street, a half hour's walk. I see them on Sunday. My boy is ten and he wants to work on the railroad. I tell him to stay in school. He says I did not. I say that is why.

## a04-gt-business
roles: gentleman
q: What line of business are you in?
meet: glessner_john_j

The Board of Trade, mostly: grain, which is to say, I buy wheat that does not yet exist from men who do not own it, and sell it to men who do not want it, and somehow everybody eats. My father did it before me. It is either the most important business in the world or the silliest, depending on the day.

?? Who's the most respected man on the Avenue?
Glessner at the corner of Eighteenth, for his sense, and Field for his money, and Kimball for his music. Glessner has been in the reaper trade since the war, and two years ago they joined all the reaper companies into the International Harvester and he is on its board. Go and ask him about it. He thinks it a great thing and a sad one.

## a04-gt-club
roles: gentleman
q: Which club do you belong to?

The Chicago Club, on Michigan Avenue, where I lunch every day, and the Union League, where I argue about politics, and the Commercial Club, where I argue about the city. A man in my position belongs to clubs as a ship belongs to harbours: he calls in, takes on news, and goes out again.

?? What are they talking about this summer?
The convention, Mr. Roosevelt, the war in the Far East between the Japanese and the Russians, which the Japanese are winning against all expectation, and Mr. Burnham's plan for the lake front. And the price of beef, always the price of beef.

## a04-gt-neighbourhood
roles: gentleman, lady
q: Is the Avenue still the best address in Chicago?
meet: pa1904_jones_fernando

It was for thirty years. Now the young people are moving north, to the Lake Shore Drive, where Mrs. Palmer built her castle. The railroads have come closer, the smoke is worse, and there are boarding houses on Michigan Avenue a few blocks south. But the old families stay. Mr. Field stays. Glessner stays.

?? Will it last?
Not as it is, I think. Nothing in Chicago lasts as it is. Fernando Jones has lived here since the town was a few hundred people in the mud. Ask him how many times he has seen the best address in Chicago move. He will count them on his fingers.

## a04-gt-strike
roles: gentleman
q: What happened in the great strike ten years ago?
meet: pa1904_pullman_mrs_george_m

The Pullman strike of ninety-four. Mr. Pullman cut wages at his works and would not cut the rents in his town, and the men struck. Then the railway union refused to handle any train with a Pullman car, and the whole country's railroads stopped. The President sent in the army. There was burning in the yards and men were killed.

?? Who was right?
That depends on which club you ask. I will tell you this: Mr. Pullman thought he was a good father to his workmen, and they thought he was a landlord. Both were partly right. Mrs. Pullman lives at Eighteenth and Prairie still. She does not speak of it. Do not ask her unless she raises it.

## a04-gt-fire
roles: gentleman, lady
age: 48-60
q: Do you remember the great fire?

I was twenty years old in seventy-one. I stood on the lake shore with my mother and my sisters and our silver in a pillowcase, and watched the city burn behind us for two days. The heat was so great that the people waded into the lake. In the morning there was nothing between the river and Fullerton but chimneys.

?? How did the city come back so fast?
Because everyone who had lost everything had nothing to do but build. The money came from the East and from Europe, the bricks came by the trainload, and in two years you could hardly tell. That is Chicago. It does not mourn. It borrows.

## a04-gt-auto
roles: gentleman, young_man
age: 18-
meet: pa1904_glessner_george_m
q: Will you buy an automobile?

I have been offered one. They say a motor car will go twenty miles an hour on a good road, of which there are about three in Illinois. It needs a mechanic, it frightens the horses, and it smells. I suspect in ten years everyone will have one, and I shall have to give up my coachman.

?? Has anyone on the Avenue got one?
A few of the young men have, and they race them on the boulevards at dawn and the police chase them on bicycles. George Glessner up the Avenue has read everything about them. Ask him if he thinks they will last.

## a04-gt-university
roles: gentleman
age: 42-
meet: pa1904_field_marshall
q: What's become of the old Fair grounds?

The University of Chicago stands beside them, on the Midway, grey stone in the English style, a dozen years old and growing every year. Mr. Rockefeller gave the money and Mr. Field gave the land. The Field Museum is in the old Fine Arts building in Jackson Park, full of everything the Fair left behind.

?? Is it a good university?
They say it will be the best in the West. Dr. Harper has hired professors from everywhere at double wages. My son goes to Yale, of course. But I am told the young men at Chicago work harder, which may be because they have nothing else to do on the Midway.

## a04-ly-charity
roles: lady
q: What good works do you do?
meet: pa1904_armour_mrs_philip_d

The Visiting Nurse Association, the Home for the Friendless, a sewing circle for the Children's Hospital, and every winter a committee for the coal fund. The poor of this city freeze in the winter and boil in the summer, and a lady with time and means must do what she can.

?? Have you been to Hull House?
Twice, with Miss Addams herself showing us round: the nursery, the kindergarten, the classes for working girls, the gymnasium, the coffee house, all in the middle of the worst of the West Side. It made me ashamed of my drawing room. Mrs. Armour gives to the poor more quietly than anyone on the Avenue. Ask her about her husband's institute.

## a04-ly-clubs
roles: lady
q: Do you belong to a club?
meet: glessner_frances_m

The Fortnightly, which meets in its rooms on Michigan Avenue to hear papers on literature and history; and the Woman's Club, which does good works and argues about the vote. We have the vote for the school board and for the University of Illinois trustees, and nothing else. We are told to be content.

?? Are you content?
No. But I am patient. Mrs. Glessner has a reading class on Monday mornings in her library that is better than any club, and harder to get into. Go and ask her if there is a vacancy. There never is.

## a04-ly-orchestra
roles: lady
q: Do you go to the orchestra?
meet: glessner_frances_m

Every Friday afternoon in the season, at the Auditorium, in our box. Mr. Thomas plays Beethoven and Wagner and the new Germans, and the ladies of Chicago listen in their hats. Some of us understand it. All of us enjoy being seen to.

?? What is the new hall?
Mr. Thomas has wanted his own hall for years, smaller than the Auditorium, built for music. The guarantors raised the money last winter and it is going up now on Michigan Avenue across from the Art Institute. They say it will open by Christmas. The Glessners were among the first to give. Ask her about it, and watch her change the subject.

## a04-ly-summer
roles: lady
q: Where do you go for the summer?

Away, as soon as we can. The city in July is all dust and heat and smoke from the railroads. Some families go to Lake Geneva, some to the north shore, some to Maine or New Hampshire. We go to our cottage at Lake Forest at the end of the week, and the servants close up the house with sheets over everything.

?? Who stays behind?
The men, mostly, who come out on the train at the weekend. And a few servants to keep the house, and the coachman with the horses. And the Avenue gets very quiet, and the maids sit on the front steps in the evening when nobody is looking, which they think nobody knows.

## a04-ly-gowns
roles: lady, young_lady
q: Where do your gowns come from?

The best from Paris, from Worth or Doucet, ordered on our trip abroad every second spring. The rest from Field's dressmaking rooms, which are as good as anything in New York, and cost nearly as much. A lady needs a morning dress, a walking dress, an afternoon dress, a dinner dress and a ball gown, and a different set for each season.

?? Is fashion changing?
The sleeves are smaller than they were ten years ago, thank heaven, and the skirts are longer and the figures more S-shaped, which is to say the corsets are worse. The young girls play golf and tennis in shirtwaists and short skirts. Their mothers are shocked. Their mothers did the same with bicycles.

## a04-ly-servants
roles: lady
q: How many servants do you keep?

Eleven: a butler, two footmen, a cook and a kitchen maid, two housemaids, a parlour maid, a laundress, a nurse and the coachman, with a groom in the stable. It sounds a great many until you see the size of the house. A house like this one is a small town, and the servants are its government.

?? Are they happy?
I hope so. I pay fair wages and I give them their afternoons and I do not count the spoons. A girl who is happy in your house stays, and a girl who stays knows your ways. My cook has been with me eleven years. I would sooner lose my husband's business partner.

## a04-ym-college
roles: young_man
age: 18-22
q: Where do you go to college?

Yale, class of nought-six. I am home for the summer. In the autumn it is football, which my mother thinks will kill me, and Greek, which my father thinks will not do me any good. Both may be right. The man I room with is from New York and has never seen a stockyard. I am taking him to see one this week.

?? What do you want to do?
My father wants me to go into the business. I want to be an architect, like Mr. Burnham or Mr. Sullivan. My father says architects are artists, and artists starve. I said Mr. Burnham does not look as if he starves. That was a mistake.

## a04-ym-baseball
roles: young_man, child
sex: m
q: Do you follow the baseball?

The Cubs, of course, at the West Side Grounds. Frank Chance at first, Tinker at short and Evers at second. They are going to win the pennant one of these years, everybody says, if the Giants ever lose. My father prefers the White Sox, on the South Side, because they are nearer and because Comiskey once sold him a horse.

?? Do you go to the games?
Whenever I can get away. You take the elevated to the West Side and sit in the sun with ten thousand men in straw hats, shouting. It is the best afternoon in the world. My mother thinks I am at the library.

## a04-ym-cycling
roles: young_man, young_lady
age: 16-
q: Do you cycle?

Everyone did ten years ago, when the safety bicycle came in: Michigan Avenue on a Sunday morning was a river of wheels. Now it is golf and tennis and motor cars. But I still ride to the lake in the mornings before it is hot. On a bicycle nobody can tell whether you are rich or poor.

?? Is it different now?
The bicycle craze is over. The girls who fought to ride in bloomers are married now and their daughters play tennis in skirts. It always goes like that: the mothers fight, the daughters take it for granted.

q: Have you come out in society yet?

Last winter, at a dance my mother gave at the house, with the ballroom on the third floor opened up and the orchestra in the hall. I wore white, of course, with my grandmother's pearls. I danced every dance and broke the heel off my slipper and had to finish in my stockings. Nobody noticed. Everybody noticed.

?? What happens now?
Calls and teas and dinners and dances, all winter, until somebody suitable proposes. My mother has a list. I have a different list. Neither of us has shown the other. I would rather go to college. My mother says that is what I will say until I fall in love.

Golf, at the clubs out in the country, at Wheaton or Lake Forest. Tennis at the Kenwood courts. Riding in the park in the mornings. Theatre parties, now that the theatres have been inspected and opened again. And in the summer, sailing on Lake Geneva, where half of Prairie Avenue goes in July.

## a04-yl-vote

age: -30
q: Do you think women should have the vote?

I do. My mother does not, and my grandmother is furious about it. In Illinois we can vote for the school trustees and nothing else. In Wyoming and Colorado and Utah women vote for everything, and the sky has not fallen. My father says politics would make women coarse. I have heard my father talk politics. I think it would make women bored.

?? Will you march?
Ladies do not march. Not yet. We sign petitions and go to lectures and argue at dinner. When I am my mother's age, perhaps, we shall march. Perhaps I shall be at the front.

## a04-ch-fourth
roles: child
q: Are you excited about the Fourth of July?

Three more days! Firecrackers and Roman candles and sparklers, and a parade on Michigan Avenue, and lemonade, and my father takes us to the lake shore at night for the rockets. Last year my brother lit a firecracker in the stable and the horses kicked their stalls. He was not allowed out for a week.

?? Are fireworks safe?
My mother says no. She reads in the paper every year about boys who lost their fingers. My nurse says I may hold a sparkler if she holds my wrist. I am going to hold it myself. Do not tell her.

## a04-ch-zoo
roles: child
q: Have you been to the zoo?

To Lincoln Park, on the North Side, on the streetcar with my nurse. There are bears in a pit, a lion that yawns, monkeys that throw things, and a buffalo, a real one. My nurse says there used to be buffalo on the prairie right here. I do not believe it. Where would they go?

?? What's your favourite?
The bear. He sits up and begs for peanuts, and when he gets one he looks at you as if to say: is that all? I gave him my whole bag. My nurse was cross. The bear was happy.

## a04-ch-school
roles: child
sex: m
q: Do you go to school?

To a school for boys on Michigan Avenue, with a master who has a moustache and a ruler. Latin, arithmetic, geography, penmanship. I am bad at penmanship. My letters lean over as if they were tired. In the autumn my sister goes to a school for girls where they learn French and the piano.

?? What do you like best?
Geography. We have a big map of the world and the master puts pins in it for the war in Manchuria. The Japanese are winning. I am for the Japanese because they are smaller. My father says that is a very good reason.

Ice cream, from the confectioner's on Twenty-second Street, in a dish with a wafer. My cousin went to the Fair in St. Louis and she says they put it in a cone made of waffle that you eat too. I do not believe her. Who would eat the dish?

They know about the cook. They do not know about the box. If I fall off they will find out. So I will not fall off.
