# The people of Prairie Avenue answer for themselves, on 1 July 1904. Their own stories
# (tales-3553/1904/personal.txt) are not retold; these reach for what is left.
# Reconstruction, liberty L-tales-3553: what is known of each life, in a voice of our making.

## a04-glessner-court
for: glessner_john_j
q: Why does your house face away from the street?
meet: glessner_williamson_martha_l

Because the street is the noisiest and dirtiest thing about Chicago, and the sun is the best. Richardson put the rooms we live in along the court, on the south, so the light comes in all day, and the servants' wing along the north wall where it shelters us from the wind. The kitchen looks onto the court too. Our cook has a better view than most of my neighbours.

?? Did Richardson see it finished?
He did not. He drew it in eighty-five and died in April of eighty-six, before the walls were up. His men finished it from his drawings. Every time I come home I think of him. Go and ask Mattie in the kitchen what she thinks of the court. She will tell you the truth about the north wind.

?? What's your favourite room?
The library, at the end of the evening, with the fire lit and my wife reading and the house quiet. A man can run a great company all day and come home to that, and know what all the business is for.

## a04-glessner-harvester
for: glessner_john_j
q: What is the International Harvester company?
meet: pa1904_glessner_george_m

Two years ago the five great harvester makers, McCormick, Deering, our own Warder, Bushnell and Glessner and two others, joined together into one company, so that we stopped ruining each other in price wars and built the best machines together. Mr. Morgan's bank arranged it. I am a vice-president and on the board.

?? Was it the right thing?
It was the sensible thing. Whether it is right, the Government will decide; there are men in Washington who call such companies trusts and want to break them up. My old firm's name has gone. George, my son, saw the whole negotiation from the side of the room. Ask him what he made of the McCormicks.

## a04-frances-journal
for: glessner_frances_m
q: Is it true you keep a journal of everything?
meet: glessner_john_j

Since 1879, every week, here and in our old house before it: who came to dinner, what we ate, what was said, what the children did, the concerts, the weather, the servants' comings and goings. Thick books bound in leather, on the shelf in my room. Mr. Glessner says I am writing the history of Prairie Avenue whether it wants one or not.

?? Why do you keep it?
Because life goes so fast, and everything one cares about is gone before one notices. My children will want to know. Their children may. Someday someone may want to know what a dinner on Prairie Avenue was like in 1904. Ask my husband. He pretends not to read it. He reads it.

?? What did you write today?
That it was very warm, that Mattie made a lemon ice, and that Fanny brought the children to lunch and the little ones ate everything but the vegetables. That is the history of Prairie Avenue today. The rest is in the newspapers.

## a04-frances-silver
for: glessner_frances_m
q: Do you make things yourself?
meet: pa1904_lee_frances_glessner

Needlework all my life, embroidery and lace in the old ways, and lately silver: I have begun lessons in silversmithing, with a bench and tools upstairs. It astonishes my friends that a lady should hammer metal. It astonishes me how much I enjoy it.

?? What else do you make?
I keep bees at The Rocks, our place in New Hampshire, in the summer, and I write down every hive. My daughter Fanny has the same restless hands, and a sharper eye than mine for how a thing is made. Go and ask her what she is working at. She is more exact than I am.

## a04-mattie-thomas
for: glessner_williamson_martha_l
q: What does Mr. Thomas like for dinner?
meet: glessner_frances_m

A good soup, plain, and a roast, and something with cream, and he does not care for anything fussy. He likes his coffee very strong and his cigar after it in the library with Mr. Glessner. When he comes, the mistress tells me the day before, and I make his favourites, and he always eats a second helping and says it is Beethoven in a pudding.

?? Do you plan the meals together?
Every morning, the mistress and I, at the kitchen table or in her sitting room, with her book. She writes down what we decide and who is coming, and after the dinner she writes down what they thought. If a dish fails she writes that too. Go and ask her to show you the book. I am in it more than her friends are.

## a04-mattie-kitchen
for: glessner_williamson_martha_l
q: What's the kitchen like in this house?
meet: pa1904_glessner_john_j_c1900_christine_nelson

The kitchen is on the north side, along Eighteenth Street, with its windows onto the court so I have the light and not the dust. A big range, the scullery beside it, the pantries, the servants' hall where we eat. Christine and Mary come through all day for the trays. I was born in Quebec to Irish parents, and I can feed a Frenchman and an Irishman and a Yankee and please all three. I hear every step on the back stairs and I know whose it is.

?? Who do you eat with?
The other servants, at the long table in the servants' hall: the maids, the men from the stable, whoever is helping. Christine, the parlour maid, talks the most. Go and ask her what the family said at lunch. She hears everything and repeats half.

## a04-christine-parlour
for: pa1904_glessner_john_j_c1900_christine_nelson
q: What does a parlour maid do?
meet: pa1904_glessner_john_j_c1900_mary_reynolds

I answer the door, show in the callers, wait at table, bring the tea in the afternoon, and keep the parlour and the library in order: dust the books, arrange the flowers, polish the tables, never move anything Mrs. Glessner has placed. I see everyone who comes into this house, and they all see me, and none of them remembers my face.

?? Is that a sadness?
No. It is a kind of freedom. Mary, the house maid, works upstairs where nobody sees her at all. She says I have the better place because I hear the conversation. I say she has the better place because she can sing while she works. Go and ask her what she sings.

## a04-mary-reynolds
for: pa1904_glessner_john_j_c1900_mary_reynolds
q: What do you sing while you work?
meet: pa1904_glessner_john_j_c1900_christine_nelson

Hymns and old songs, under my breath, making the beds and turning the mattresses, whatever my mother sang. Mrs. Glessner heard me once on the landing and said I had a pretty voice and to go on. I stopped anyway. A house maid should be heard by nobody.

?? Do you get on with Christine?
We have shared the back stairs since the family moved in, and we know each other's step and each other's moods. She has the parlour and the callers, I have the bedrooms and the linen, and at night in the servants' hall we put the day together between us. Go and ask her who came to call this week.

## a04-fanny-miniatures
for: pa1904_lee_frances_glessner
q: What do you do with your days?
meet: glessner_frances_m

I run a household and mind my children, and I read: law, which my husband brings home, and medicine, and detective stories, which I am not supposed to admit. My brother George went to Harvard and I was kept at home. I have never stopped wanting to know how things are proved.

?? What would you study, if you could?
How a careful person can look at a room and tell what happened in it. One day, when the children are grown, I may do something with that. My mother says that is what she said at my age, and now she is hammering silver.

## a04-blewett-railroad
for: pa1904_lee_blewett
q: What do you do for the Illinois Central?
meet: pa1904_lee_frances_glessner

I am one of its lawyers: contracts, land, lawsuits, the endless paper of a railroad that runs from Chicago to New Orleans. Every mile of track is a hundred agreements and every agreement is a quarrel waiting. It is not glamorous work. It is very necessary work.

?? How did you meet your wife?
In Chicago, at her parents' table, which is where everyone of interest meets everyone else. Fanny asked me a question about the law of evidence that I could not answer, and I spent the evening trying. I have spent the years since trying to keep up with her. Ask her what the question was. She remembers. I pretend not to.

## a04-field-walk
for: pa1904_field_marshall
q: Is it true you walk to the store every morning?
meet: pa1904_field_marshall_c1900_thomas_benson

Every morning I can, up Prairie and Michigan to State and Washington, about three miles. A man who sits in a carriage misses the city. On foot I see the shop windows, the crowds, what people are wearing and carrying, which streets are busy. By the time I reach my desk I know more about trade than my managers.

?? Who keeps your house?
Thomas Benson, my butler, and a household that runs as well as the store. I ask the same of both: honesty, order, and no surprises. Go and see him. He knows what time I leave each morning to the minute. He has the door open before I reach it.

## a04-field-art
for: pa1904_field_marshall
q: What will you do with your fortune?
meet: pa1904_armour_mrs_philip_d

Some of it is already done: the land for the University, the museum in Jackson Park that bears my name. A man who has made money from a city owes it something back. I have told the museum's men that I intend to do more, when the time is right, and they hope it will be soon. I do not hurry.

?? Do you give to charities?
Quietly. I do not care for my name in the newspapers for giving money away. Mrs. Armour, down the Avenue, gives more quietly still, and has built something better than most men have with twice the money. Go and ask her about the Institute.

## a04-pullman-statue
for: pa1904_pullman_mrs_george_m
q: Why did your husband put the statue at Eighteenth Street?
meet: glessner_john_j

Because he believed the battle of 1812 was fought near here, along the lake shore south of the old fort, and he wanted the city to remember it, and Black Partridge, the chief who saved Mrs. Helm. He had a Danish sculptor make it in bronze, and set it up in ninety-three, the year of the Fair, in front of our wall. He called it his gift to the city.

?? Do people look at it?
The children do; their nurses bring them. Visitors stop. Our neighbours have stopped seeing it, the way you stop seeing your own front door. Mr. Glessner, across the way, takes his guests to it after dinner sometimes, and tells them the story. Ask him to tell it. He tells it well.

## a04-pullman-town
for: pa1904_pullman_mrs_george_m
q: Do you ever go to the town of Pullman?
meet: pa1904_lowden_mrs_frank_orren

Rarely, now. It belongs to the company and the courts have made the company sell the houses, and it is not the town it was. When my husband was alive, we went down in his car and he would walk the streets pointing at things he had built: the market hall, the arcade, the church, the hotel he named for Florence.

?? What do you remember most?
The flowers. He insisted on flower beds everywhere, and lawns, and trees, in a place that had been prairie and marsh. He thought if men lived among beautiful things they would be better men. Some of them were. Florence understands it better than I do. Go and ask her what she thinks of her father's town.

## a04-florence-governor
for: pa1904_lowden_mrs_frank_orren
q: Were you disappointed when your husband lost?
meet: pa1904_lowden_frank_orren

Disappointed for him, because he wanted it so. Relieved for us, a little, because I did not much want to live in the Executive Mansion at Springfield. Three weeks of ballots and cigar smoke and men who promised one thing at midnight and did another at noon. He came home thin.

?? Will he try again?
I expect so. He is a farmer at heart; he has bought land out at Sinnissippi by the Rock River and he talks of cattle more than politics. But politics gets into a man like a fever. Go and ask him which he likes better, the farm or the convention. Watch his face.

## a04-lowden-farm
for: pa1904_lowden_frank_orren
q: What is your farm like?
meet: pa1904_lowden_mrs_frank_orren

Sinnissippi, out on the Rock River near Oregon, a hundred miles west: rolling land, good black soil, the river and the bluffs. I grew up on a farm in Iowa and in Minnesota, and I never got it out of my blood. In the city I am a lawyer and a politician. On the farm I am a man looking at a field of corn, which is the best thing to be.

?? What do you grow?
Corn and oats and hay, and cattle to eat them. I want to show that a farm can be run scientifically, with good breeding and good accounts, the way a business is. My wife says I want to show it to the cows. She is right.

## a04-armour-institute
for: pa1904_armour_mrs_philip_d
q: What is the Armour Institute?
meet: pa1904_meeker_arthur

A school on Thirty-third Street, for young men and women of the working classes, where they can learn engineering, architecture, chemistry, electricity, the trades that will build this country. My husband built it with Dr. Gunsaulus, the minister, and gave it millions. Since he died I have carried on what he began.

?? Did you know your husband's business?
The packing houses? I visited the yards once, as a young wife, and never again. Mr. Meeker, who lives along the Avenue, ran a great part of it with my husband for years. Go and ask him. He will tell you about the business. I prefer to tell you about the school.

## a04-kimball-organ
for: pa1904_kimball_william_w
q: How many pianos do you make?
meet: pa1904_kimball_william_w_c1900_ernest_sibbald

Thousands a year, at the works on the West Side, pianos and reed organs and great pipe organs for churches and halls. The reed organ was our bread and butter: every farmhouse in the West wanted one. Now the farmers are richer and they want pianos, and their daughters want to play Chopin.

?? Do you play yourself?
Not a note. I am a salesman, and I learned from the start that a man does not need to play the piano to know who wants one. My man Ernest is the musical one in this house. Ask him to play something on the parlour piano when I am out.

## a04-jones-1835
for: pa1904_jones_fernando
q: What do you remember of Chicago in 1835?
meet: glessner_john_j

The mud, the land sales, the Potawatomi camped by the river waiting for their payment, Mark Beaubien's fiddle at the Sauganash, the boats being rowed ashore. I was a boy of fifteen and I thought it was the most exciting place in the world. It was. Everything was beginning.

?? What do people forget?
That the Potawatomi were here, by the thousand, and that they were made to go. I learned some of their language. I knew boys my age who went west in the removal and I never saw again. When I see the bronze at Eighteenth Street, I think of them, not of the battle. Go and ask Mr. Glessner what he thinks of that statue. He is a thoughtful man.

?? Are you the oldest settler here?
Nearly. There are not many of us left who saw the town before the land sales. When I die, the last memory of the little town goes with me. That is why I talk so much. Somebody should listen.

## a04-shortall-abstracts
for: pa1904_shortall_john_g
q: Why do title abstracts matter so much?
meet: pa1904_jones_fernando

Because in a city where every record burned, the only proof of who owns what is in private books like ours. After the fire the legislature made our abstracts lawful evidence, and every title in Chicago rests on them. A city is only paper underneath: who owns this lot, who owes that debt.

?? Who else saved their books?
Two other firms, by the same kind of luck and running. Fernando Jones was one of the men in the abstract business in those days. Go and ask him about the night of the fire. He tells it differently from me, which is how you know we were both there.

## a04-meeker-yards-question
for: pa1904_meeker_arthur
q: How many animals go through the stockyards?
meet: pa1904_armour_mrs_philip_d

Millions a year: cattle, hogs, sheep, more than any place on earth. They come in by train from the whole West and leave as meat in refrigerator cars for every city in the country. Mr. Armour said we use everything but the squeal. He meant it. Hair, horn, blood, bone, fat, everything has a buyer.

?? Is the work hard?
For the men on the killing floor, it is the hardest work in America, and the pay is not what it should be. There is talk of a strike this summer: the butcher workmen's union and the packers do not agree on wages. I hope it can be settled. Mrs. Armour cares about the families. Ask her what her husband believed about the men.

## a04-wood-eye
for: pa1904_wood_casey_a
q: Can you tell much from someone's eyes?
meet: pa1904_field_marshall

More than they would like. A man's eyes tell me if he has a disease of the blood, or the kidneys, or the nerves, before he knows it himself. I have seen the first sign of illness in an eye at a dinner table and said nothing until the man came to my office a year later.

?? Who are your patients?
Everyone, rich and poor. I teach and I see the poor at the clinic, and I see the Avenue in my office. I will not say who. A doctor's patients are his own business. But a great many men in this city read the newspapers through spectacles I prescribed. Ask Mr. Field whose spectacles he reads his accounts through. He will not tell you either.
