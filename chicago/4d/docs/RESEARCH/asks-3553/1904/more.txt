# More questions for Prairie Avenue on 1 July 1904. Reconstruction, liberty L-tales-3553.

## a04-to-ice
roles: toddler
q: What's that in your hand?

Ice! From the ice man. It is cold. It drips.

?? Can I have some?
No. It is mine. You can have a little bit. There. That is the little bit.

## a04-to-bed
roles: toddler
q: Are you sleepy?

No. Yes. Nanny says nap. I do not want nap.

?? What do you do at nap time?
I look at the ceiling. It has a crack. The crack is a river. A boat goes on it.

## a04-md-callers
roles: maid
q: Who comes calling on the Avenue?
meet: glessner_frances_m

Ladies, every afternoon in the season, with their cards in little silver cases. They leave a card with the corner turned down if they called in person, and we put them on the salver in the hall. The mistress reads them at tea and decides who is to be called on back. It is a kind of war, with paper.

?? Who calls most?
The ladies of the reading class at the Glessners', on Mondays, all together, so the whole street knows where they are going. In summer nobody calls at all. They are all away, and the cards gather dust on the salvers.

## a04-md-sweetheart
roles: maid
q: Do you have a young man?

A policeman, Irish, with a beat on Michigan Avenue, who walks past the area steps at a quarter past nine every evening and does not look up. I am at the area window at a quarter past nine every evening and do not look out. We have been courting this way since Easter.

?? Will you marry him?
When he makes sergeant, he says. A married girl cannot stay in service, and a policeman's wages are not much for two. So I save, and he studies for the examination, and we both pretend not to be in any hurry. The cook knows. The cook knows everything.

## a04-md-fire-escape
roles: maid
q: Are you afraid of fire, living up under the roof?

Since the Iroquois, all of us are. Our rooms are at the top, and the back stairs are narrow and wooden. The mistress had a rope ladder put in each of our rooms in January, coiled under the window, and the butler made us practise. I went down it once in my nightgown and swore I never would again.

?? Would you use it?
If I had to, I would go down it like a sailor. Every girl in service on the Avenue lies awake some nights listening for the smell of smoke. We do not tell the family. They have enough to worry about, they think.

## a04-ym-fair-work
roles: young_man
q: What will you do this summer?
meet: pa1904_field_marshall

My father has found me a place for the summer in the wholesale house, counting stock and learning the business from the bottom, as he says he did. Mr. Field started as a clerk sleeping under the counter, they tell me; every young man on the Avenue has heard that story a hundred times.

?? Do you mind?
I mind the hours. I do not mind the money, which is my own, and I do not mind the girls at the lunch counter. Go and ask Mr. Field if he really slept under the counter. I have always wanted to know.

## a04-ym-motor
roles: young_man
q: Is that your motor car?

My father's, but I drive it, because he is afraid of it. It does twenty miles an hour on the boulevard if the police are not looking, and it breaks down every second time out. I carry a toolbox, a spare tyre, a can of petrol and a great deal of patience.

?? Do the horses mind?
They hate it. The coachmen hate it more. Ours will not speak to me since I went past his greys at Thirty-first Street and they bolted halfway to the lake. I paid for the harness. I have not yet paid for his feelings.

## a04-yl-college
roles: young_lady
q: Would you go to college if you could?
meet: pa1904_lee_frances_glessner

I would go tomorrow. Vassar, or Smith, or the University here on the Midway, which takes women and lets them sit beside the men. My mother says a girl with a degree frightens off husbands. I say I should like to meet a husband who is frightened of a degree.

?? Do you know anyone who went?
Not on the Avenue, hardly. Fanny Glessner wanted to, I have heard, and was not allowed, and she has never forgiven anyone for it. Go and ask her. She will tell you exactly what she would have studied.

## a04-gt-children
roles: gentleman
q: What do you want for your sons?

What every man on this Avenue wants: that they should have what we had to fight for without having to fight for it, and still turn out men. It cannot be done, I suspect. A boy who never slept under a counter does not know what a dollar is. I send mine to work in the summers and they think me a tyrant.

?? And your daughters?
That they should marry well and be happy, in whichever order they can manage. My wife says they should go to college. My eldest says she will vote before I die. She may be right. She usually is.

## a04-gt-stockyards
roles: gentleman
q: Is it true the whole city smells of the stockyards?
meet: pa1904_meeker_arthur

When the wind is from the south-west, yes, even here. Packingtown is four miles off and on a hot day you would think it was next door. The packers say it is the smell of money. Their wives say it is the reason they live on the lake.

?? Is there trouble at the yards?
There is talk of a strike, the men wanting more for the work on the killing floors. Arthur Meeker, up the Avenue, will know more than any of us. Go and ask him. He will talk about it to a stranger more freely than at the club.

## a04-ly-hospital
roles: lady
q: Do you visit the hospitals?

St. Luke's and the children's hospital, every week in the season, with the committee. I read to the children and bring oranges and talk to the mothers. It is the most useful two hours of my week, and the hardest. Some of those children came out of the Iroquois with burns that will never heal.

?? Does it change how you see the city?
It makes me ashamed of my own grumbling. A woman on this Avenue complains that the coal smoke ruins her curtains. A woman on the West Side has no curtains, and no coal. One must keep both in mind at once, or not be honest.

## a04-ly-daughter
roles: lady
q: How old are your children now?

My eldest girl is twenty and out in society, and wants to go abroad and study music. My boy is at school in the East and writes once a month, for money. The little one is still in the nursery, the darling of the house, ruled entirely by her nurse.

?? Are you a strict mother?
Strict about manners, lenient about everything else. My own mother was strict about everything, and I learned to do as I liked out of her sight. My daughters do as they like in my sight, which I think is an improvement.

## a04-ms-footman
roles: manservant
q: What do you wear on duty?

Livery: a dark coat with brass buttons, striped waistcoat, black trousers, white gloves, and, for dinners, knee breeches and silk stockings, which I hate. The family's crest is on the buttons. I am a walking advertisement for the house.

?? Do you like the work?
I like the dinners, when the house is lit and the silver shines and the talk is good. I do not like standing behind a chair for three hours while a gentleman talks about railroad bonds. But a good footman can hear a great deal while looking at nothing.

## a04-cm-funeral
roles: coachman
q: What's the saddest drive you've made?

The funeral of a lady on the Avenue, last winter, with black plumes on the horses and the hearse ahead, at a walk all the way to Graceland in the snow. Every carriage on the Avenue behind us. The horses knew. They walked like mourners, heads down, and never put a foot wrong.

?? Do horses grieve?
I believe they do. When the old coachman two doors down died, his horses would not eat for three days. The new man fed them by hand. They came round in the end. So do we all.

## a04-ck-irish
roles: cook
q: Where did you learn to cook?

In my mother's kitchen in Limerick, on an open fire, and then in a hotel kitchen in Boston under a French chef who threw pans. He taught me sauces and how to duck. Then I came west for better wages and a quieter kitchen. I found the wages.

?? Is the family hard to please?
The master likes plain food and the mistress likes French, so I cook French and call it plain. The young ladies want everything from the magazines. The little ones want bread and butter. I please all of them, most days, which is more than the President manages.
