# More of the camps round Fort Dearborn on 14 August 1812: Potawatomi, Ottawa, Ojibwe and
# Miami men and women, and the settlement's hired people. Reconstruction, liberty L-tales-3553.
# Nothing here is a ceremony told as attested: where a belief is spoken of, it is in passing.

## a12-pm-river-mouth
roles: potawatomi_man
q: What is this river called in your language?
meet: chandonnai_jean_baptiste

Shikaakwa, near enough, for the wild leek that grows thick along its banks in spring. The smell of it hangs over the marsh in April. The French wrote it down as Chicagou and the Americans dropped the end. Chandonnai, the trader's clerk, can say it the old way and the French way and the American way. Ask him to say all three.

?? Is it a good place?
For a village, no: too wet, too many mosquitoes, the ground floods every spring. For a crossing, the best on the lakes. Every canoe from the north going down to the Illinois comes through here and over the portage. Whoever sits at this river mouth hears everything first.

?? Is that why the Americans built here?
Yes. They did not build here for the leeks.

## a12-pm-horse
roles: potawatomi_man, miami
q: That is a fine horse. Where did it come from?

Traded from the Sauk, who had it from somebody on the Missouri, who had it from somebody further west. A horse walks a long way before it reaches you. I gave four good beaver packs and a gun for him, and I have never regretted it.

?? Does he have a name?
He has a name I will not tell a stranger, and a name I call him when he will not cross water, which I will not tell you either. He is fast on the prairie and stupid in the woods. Like some men I know.

?? Will you ride tomorrow?
Everyone who has a horse will be on it tomorrow, one way or another. The soldiers are walking. Think about that.

## a12-pm-ottawa
roles: potawatomi_man
q: Is everyone in the camps Potawatomi?

Most, but not all. There are Ottawa from the north, and Ojibwe, and a few Winnebago and Kickapoo who came to see what would happen. We three, Potawatomi, Ottawa and Ojibwe, call ourselves brothers: the three fires. We came down the lakes together long ago, and we still speak nearly the same language.

?? Do the brothers agree about the Americans?
Brothers never agree. The Ojibwe in the far north barely see an American from one year to the next. The Ottawa trade with the British at Mackinac. We Potawatomi live next to the Americans. We have the most to gain and the most to lose.

?? And the Winnebago?
They are angry and they are not our relatives, and when something bad happens, people say: the Winnebago did it. Sometimes it is true. It is very convenient for everybody else when it is.

## a12-pm-guns
roles: potawatomi_man
q: Where do your people get guns?

From the traders, mostly the British ones at Mackinac and Malden, and from the Americans here when they were selling. Every autumn a man needs powder, lead, flints and a gun that works. Without them his family does not eat in winter. A gun is a hoe for meat.

?? Do the British give them free?
The British give presents every year: guns, blankets, kettles, silver, tobacco. They call it keeping the friendship bright. The Americans give presents too, but fewer, and they want land at the end of it. A man notices which friend asks for nothing back.

?? Can you mend a gun?
I can change a flint and clean the pan. For anything more there is a smith at the fort who mends guns for the villages. Mended them, I should say. He will not mend any more of them, now.

## a12-pm-names-places
roles: potawatomi_man, potawatomi_elder
q: Where is your village?
meet: waubansee

On the Kankakee, three days' walk south and east, where the river runs slow through the marsh and the wild rice grows. Forty lodges in summer. In winter we scatter to the hunting grounds and come back in spring to plant.

?? Is it near anyone I might meet?
Waubansee's people are on the Fox, west of us. He is here, by the river with his men. He is a war leader, but a careful one. He will tell you his own mind about tomorrow, and not anybody else's. Go and find him.

?? Will you go home after this?
After tomorrow, yes. Whatever happens tomorrow, my wife wants the corn in, and the corn does not care about the Americans.

## a12-pm-french
roles: potawatomi_man
q: You speak French?

Enough to trade and enough to swear. My mother's sister married a Frenchman at St. Joseph, so I grew up with French cousins who ate their corn on a plate and crossed themselves before thunder. Half the people at this river have a French grandfather somewhere.

?? Do the French take sides?
The French here have no country to take sides for. Their king is gone, their flag is gone, their priests are mostly gone. They have their families, which are half ours, and their trade, which is all of ours. They will stand with whoever lets them keep both.

?? Who is the most French person here?
Ouilmette, across the river, who says he will stay when the Americans go. He came here as a boatman more than twenty years ago and married into the Potawatomi and never left. He thinks nobody will hurt him. He may be right. We have known him a long time.

## a12-pm-wampum
roles: potawatomi_man, potawatomi_elder
q: What is that belt of beads?

Wampum. Small shell beads, white and purple, strung and woven into a belt. A belt carries words. When a message goes from one nation to another, the belt goes with it, so the people who receive it know the message is true and the sender means it.

?? Can you read it?
I can tell you what some of the patterns mean: the white road is peace, the dark is grief or war. To know what a particular belt says, you have to ask the man who carried it and heard the words spoken over it. The beads hold the promise. The man holds the words.

?? Has a belt come this summer?
Many. From Tecumseh, from the British, from the villages on the Wabash. Some white, some dark. I will not tell you which ones my village accepted. You can guess.

## a12-pm-canoe
roles: potawatomi_man
q: Did you make that canoe?

My uncle and I, two summers ago. Birch bark from the north, sewn with spruce root, sealed with pitch, over cedar ribs. A bark canoe weighs no more than a man can carry on his shoulders, which is why we can cross the portage with it and the French can, and the Americans with their heavy boats cannot.

?? What about dugouts?
Dugouts are for slow water: a whole tree trunk, burned and scraped out. Heavy, but they last, and you cannot tear them on a snag. Most of the canoes on this river are dugouts now. The good birch is far to the north.

?? Will you take me out?
Not tonight. Tonight nobody goes out on the river who does not have to. Ask me in a better summer.

## a12-pm-kinzie
roles: potawatomi_man
q: What do you think of the trader Kinzie?
meet: kinzie_john_elder

Shawneeawkee, we call him, the silversmith, because he worked silver when he first came among us, and still makes brooches for our women. He has lived at this river longer than the fort. He knows every family and what every family owes him, to the muskrat.

?? Is he your friend?
He is the friend of every man who pays him. That is not an insult. That is what a trader is. But he also feeds people in bad winters and does not ask. Some of the chiefs have said quietly that his family must not be harmed tomorrow. Go and look at his house. You will see warriors sitting in front of it who are not there to attack it.

?? What about the man he killed?
Lalime, the interpreter, in June, by the fort. They had quarrelled for years. Some say Lalime drew first. The captain did nothing. Kinzie went away for a while and came back. Among my people, a death is answered by a gift to the family or by another death. The Americans seem to have no way at all.

## a12-pm-sacred
roles: potawatomi_man, potawatomi_elder
q: Is there a sacred place near here?

There are places along the lake and up the rivers where people go to be alone and hear what the spirits want of them. I will not tell you where. A sacred place told to a stranger is no longer a place, only a spot on the trader's map.

?? Can you tell me anything?
That the lake is alive and should be spoken to before you put a canoe on it. That a man leaves tobacco when he takes something from the land. That is all I will say, and it is more than most of my relatives would.

## a12-pm-main-poc
roles: potawatomi_man
q: Who is the man everyone stops talking around?
meet: main_poc_runner

A runner of Main Poc's band, from the Kankakee, wearing the red belt. Main Poc is the war leader born with a hand that has no fingers, whose power is so strong even other war leaders are afraid of him. He has gone to the British at Malden. His runners carry his words to the camps here.

?? What are his words?
That the Americans are a sickness and the only cure is to drive them out of the whole country. That the Great Spirit gave the land to the nations and no paper can take it away. Many young men here have heard those words all summer.

?? Should I talk to the runner?
You can try. He will not hurt you here, in front of everyone. But he will look at you as if he is counting you. Go and see for yourself, and do not stay long.

## a12-pw-rice
roles: potawatomi_woman
q: What is wild rice?

A grass that grows in shallow water, taller than a man, with grain at the top. In late summer we pole canoes into the rice beds and bend the stalks over and knock the grain into the bottom of the canoe with two sticks. It is the best food there is. The Ojibwe live on it.

?? Is it ready now?
Nearly. In a few weeks, the rice on the Kankakee will be ripe. My sisters are already there, waiting. I came here for the goods the captain promised. I wish I had stayed with my sisters.

?? How do you cook it?
Parch it in a kettle over the fire, then dance on it in a pit to loosen the husks, then winnow it in a birch basket on a windy day. Then boil it with maple sugar or with duck. If you ever eat it, you will not want bread again.

## a12-pw-trader-wife
roles: potawatomi_woman
q: Do Potawatomi women marry Frenchmen?
meet: ouilmette_archange

Many do. Archange, across the river, is married to the Frenchman Ouilmette, and her father was a Frenchman too, and her mother one of us. A trader with a Potawatomi wife has a hundred relatives in the camps who will trade with him and nobody else. That is good for him. A Potawatomi woman with a trader husband has kettles and cloth and a house with a floor. That is good for her.

?? Is it love, or trade?
Both, often. Archange and her husband have been together since before the fort was built and have a house full of children. Go and see her. She will laugh at your question and give you something to eat.

?? Will she go with the soldiers?
No. She will stay with her husband and her children, on her own river, with her own relatives all round her. Who would harm her? Half the young men in the camps call her aunt.

## a12-pw-mats
roles: potawatomi_woman
q: What are you weaving?

A mat of cattail leaves, for the winter lodge. I cut the leaves in summer, dry them, and sew them together with a bone needle and basswood cord. Six of these will cover a lodge. Twenty mats a summer, every summer, since I was twelve.

?? Is it hard work?
Not hard. Long. You do it while you talk, while you watch the children, while the men argue. My grandmother said that a woman's hands remember what her head forgets. I do not need to look at the needle any more.

?? Do you sell them?
The trader's wife bought two for her floor. She said they were warmer than any rug from Detroit. She was right.

## a12-pw-sister
roles: potawatomi_woman
q: Where is your family?

My husband is in the council tonight with the other men. My mother is in the lodge with my children. My brother is somewhere with the young men by the river, painting his face, and I have not been able to talk to him all day.

?? What would you tell him?
To remember who fed him last winter. The soldiers' wives gave us flour when the corn ran out in March. To remember the children in the fort are children. He knows all that. Whether he listens to it tomorrow, I cannot tell you.

## a12-mi-wells-horse
roles: miami
q: How far did you ride to get here?

From Fort Wayne, at the head of the Maumee, along the old trail by the St. Joseph River and round the bottom of the lake. Something like a hundred and fifty miles. We did it in less than a week, pushing hard. Apekonit said every day counted.

?? Was it a hard ride?
Hot. Flies. Rivers to swim the horses across. And the people in the villages along the way watching us and saying nothing. We knew what they were thinking: those are the Miami who ride with the Americans. Not all of my relatives approve.

?? Going back will be harder?
Going back we will be walking at the pace of oxen and wagons and children. It will take two weeks. Every day of it in open country. You do not need me to explain that to you.

## a12-mi-relatives
roles: miami
q: Do the Miami and the Potawatomi get along?

We are neighbours, which means we sometimes do and sometimes do not. The Potawatomi came south into country that was once ours. We have married each other's daughters for a hundred years. We have also stolen each other's horses for a hundred years. Both things are true at once.

?? Do you have cousins in the camps?
Yes. I saw one this morning by the river. He looked at me and I looked at him and neither of us said a word. Tomorrow we may be on different sides of the same field. Tonight we are just two men who know each other's mothers.

## a12-mi-wayne
roles: miami
q: Have you fought before?

At the Fallen Timbers, eighteen summers ago, when I was very young, against General Wayne's soldiers. The Americans won. The nations made the treaty at Greenville. Since then the Miami have kept the peace, as Little Turtle said we should.

?? Was it hard to make peace with men you had fought?
Hard? It was the hardest thing my people ever did. But Little Turtle was right: the Americans were too many. You can fight a war you can win or you can make a peace you can live with. He chose to live. Not everyone agreed with him, and not everyone does now.

## a12-pc-wild
roles: potawatomi_child
q: Do you know any animals?

Muskrats, they live in houses of mud in the marsh. Beavers, they cut down trees with their teeth. Raccoons, they wash their food. Skunks, you do not go near them. Bears, my grandfather saw one. Wolves, you hear them at night. Turtles, I caught one as big as a kettle.

?? What did you do with the turtle?
My mother made soup. I wanted to keep it. She said a turtle is not a dog. I said why not. She did not have a good answer.

?? What is your favourite?
The otter. It slides down the riverbank on its belly, over and over, just because it likes it. My uncle says that is a waste of time. I think the otter knows something my uncle does not.

## a12-pc-stories
roles: potawatomi_child
q: Does anyone tell you stories?

My grandmother, in the winter, when the snow is deep. Never in summer. Stories are for winter, she says, when the spirits are asleep and cannot hear us talking about them. If you tell a winter story in summer, a snake might come into your bed.

?? Tell me one anyway?
No! It is summer! You want a snake in my bed? Ask me again when it snows.

## a12-hs-servant
roles: household_servant
q: Who do you work for?

For the officers' families, in the kitchen and the wash-house and the garden, from before light until after dark. Cooking, carrying, scrubbing, minding the children. The work does not stop because the army is leaving. If anything it doubles.

?? Are you going with them?
I go where the family goes. Nobody asked me if I wanted to. I have packed their things and then my own, which took less time.

?? What do you think will happen?
I think the people who have the least say will pay the most, as usual. I hope I am wrong. I have been wrong before, once or twice.
