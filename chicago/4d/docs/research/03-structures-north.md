## 1. PRIORITY BUILDING — Sauganash Hotel / Eagle Exchange Tavern

**Name:** Eagle Exchange Tavern (1829–1831) → Sauganash Hotel (1831–1838) → Chicago Hotel (1838) → later Railroad House. `[DOC]`

**Location:** **Southeast corner of Lake St and Market St** (today Lake St & N Wacker Dr), on the *south* side of the main stem / *east* bank of the south branch, facing the forks diagonally to the NW. `[DOC]` — chicagology/prefire273 ("near what is now the south‑east corner of Lake and Market streets"); DRLOIH; Chicago Landmark "Site of the Sauganash Hotel/Wigwam," now 191 N Wacker.

> **Critical geometry note:** the building was **moved**. Beaubien bought a small log cabin on the south bank near the Forks from James Kinzie in 1826/27 and opened it as a tavern in 1829. After the **1830 James Thompson plat**, the cabin was found to sit *in the middle of a platted street*, so he relocated it to the Lake/Market corner, and the frame hotel was built onto it there. `[DOC]` (DRLOIH, Beaubien). So the 1829 log core and the 1831 frame block occupy the *second* site, not the original one. `[INF]`

**Construction:**
- 1829: single-story **log** cabin, "small," Eagle Exchange Tavern. Tavern license issued 9 June 1830. `[DOC]`
- 1831: **frame addition** built onto the log structure → two stories → "Chicago's first hotel." `[DOC]` Wikipedia; DRLOIH; Wau‑Bun.
- Dimensions of either element: **not found in any source I could reach.** `[GAP]`

**Appearance — the primary description.** Juliette Kinzie, *Wau‑Bun* (1856, describing 1831):

> "At the Point, on the south side, stood a house just completed by Mark Beaubien. It was a pretentious white two‑story building, with bright‑blue wooden shutters, the admiration of all the little circle at Wolf Point. Here a canoe ferry was kept to transport people across the south branch of the river."

`[DOC]` — authorama transcription of *Wau‑Bun* ch. XVII. This is the **only near‑contemporary description** and is the load-bearing evidence for: white painted clapboard, two stories, **bright blue shutters**. Note "just completed" corroborates 1831.

Secondary/derived: "Greek Revival trim," "symmetry of its facade…typical to contemporary Greek Revival practiced on the East Coast," contrasting with "the other eleven buildings of Chicago." `[DOC as a claim]` (Wikipedia) but this is a **modern art-historical gloss, not a period observation** `[INF]` — treat the Greek Revival attribution as lower confidence than the color/shutters/stories.

**Galleries / porch:** No period source I found attests a gallery or two-tier porch. Later 19th‑c. illustrations commonly show a full-width front porch or gallery. **`[DISPUTED]` / `[CONJ]`** — I could not locate any scholarly treatment adjudicating between the competing later depictions (the Blanchard & Shober 1867 "Wolf's Point in 1833" chromolithograph, the various CHM drawings, and the 1902 Calisphere image "Mark Beaubien's Tavern. The Sauganash, in 1835"). **Recommend flagging the porch/gallery as unresolved in the model.** All surviving images are retrospective (1860s–1900s), drawn 30–70 years after the fact, and none has a documented eyewitness basis.

**Immediate surroundings (what to model adjacent):**
- **Philo Carpenter's drug store** — Chicago's first, in a **log building on Lake St immediately adjacent to the Sauganash's public bar**. Carpenter arrived 18 July 1832. `[DOC]` Standing and operating 1835 `[INF]`.
- Chicago's first **billiard table** in a public house was here. `[DOC]`
- Beaubien's **canoe ferry** across the south branch, worked by rope, ran from the Point. `[DOC]` for 1831; by July 1835 probably superseded by the Lake/Randolph log raft bridge `[INF]`.

**Status on 1835‑07‑01: STANDING and operating as a hotel.** `[DOC]`
**Occupancy/function in 1835:** Beaubiens owned it **until 1834**; in **1835 sold to "a Mr. Davis."** `[DOC]` (Wikipedia; DRLOIH hotels chronology: "Operators: Mark Beaubien; Davis (1835)"). `[CONJ]` — this may be the same **John Davis** listed as operating the Steamboat Hotel on North Water St in 1835. Mark Beaubien himself had moved on: he built the **Exchange Coffee House** at Lake & Wells in 1834. `[DOC]`

**Later fate:** Renamed Chicago Hotel 1838; the dining room housed the Chicago Theatre Company from Nov 1837 (Chicago's first commercial theater). **Destroyed by fire 4 March 1851** `[DOC]` (DRLOIH gives the precise date; Wikipedia says only "1851"). Site vacant/unknown 1851–1860; the **Wigwam** — a two-story temporary hall of "plain pine boards," second-story gallery, curved ceiling for acoustics, capacity 10–12,000, built in just over a month — was erected on the site in 1860 for the Republican National Convention. `[DOC]`

**Cultural note for 1835 scene-setting:** On **18 Aug 1835** the last great Potawatomi war dance passed directly by the Sauganash, then "filled with young married folk who had arrived in Chicago a few weeks before." `[DOC]` (chicagology/lastwardance). That is six weeks *after* your target date but describes the same summer's building population.

## 2. WEST BANK OF THE FORKS

### 2.1 Wolf Point Tavern / Wolf Tavern
- **Aliases:** Rat Castle, Old Geese's Tavern, Taylor's Tavern, Travelers' Home, Western Stage House. `[DOC]`
- **Location:** **West bank** of the river at the forks, north of the Lake St crossing; modern coordinates given as **W Wacker Dr & Canal St**. `[DOC]` (Wikipedia; DRLOIH; chicagology/prefire273 places it "north of Lake Street Bridge").
- **Built:** **1828 by James Kinzie** `[DOC]` (Wikipedia, DRLOIH Wolf Point article) — **`[DISPUTED]`**: DRLOIH's hotels chronology gives **1823, by James Kinzie and David Hall**. The 1828 date is better supported.
- **Type:** **log**; chicagology/prefire273 describes Wentworth's house as **"partly log and partly frame."** `[DOC]` — so model it as a log core with a frame extension by the early 1830s `[INF]`.
- **Appearance:** a **painted sign of a wolf** hung outside by approximately 1833 — the single most distinctive visual feature. `[DOC]`
- **Operators:** Archibald Caldwell (liquor license 8 Dec 1829) → Elijah "Old Geese" Wentworth (1830–31/32) → Charles (and Mary) Taylor (1831/32–1833) → **William Walters (1833–1836)**. `[DOC]`
- **1832:** headquarters of Gen. Winfield Scott during the Black Hawk War. `[DOC]`
- **Rates:** **CORRECTED 2026-08-11 — the sentence this line reported does not exist.** It was
  tagged `[DOC]` to `drloih_wolf_point`; that page carries no prices of any kind, and Andreas
  returns zero matches for "stable a horse", "12 1/2 cents", "twelve and a half cents" or
  "horse-keeping". The 12½¢ is the rate for a MAN and was carried across to the horse; the
  "13¢" appears to have been invented in the crossing. What IS attested is better and tighter:
  Andreas scan p. 249, the Cook County Court of County Commissioners at Chicago, **13 April
  1831**, sets the tavern rates — **"For each horse fed 25 / Keeping horse one night 50 /
  Lodging for each man per night 12½"** — at the same term that granted the county's first two
  tavern licences, to Elijah Wentworth for $7 and Samuel Miller for $5. Wentworth kept the Wolf
  Point Tavern from early 1830, so this house is one of exactly two in the county licensed
  under that schedule. `[DOC]` for the 1831 schedule; the old figures are withdrawn.
- **Status 1835‑07‑01: STANDING.** `[DOC]` **`[DISPUTED]` on function:** Wikipedia/DRLOIH have Walters operating it 1833–1836; DRLOIH's hotels chronology says it "ceased operations as a public house in 1834"; another account says that after Miller retired in 1834 the Wolf Point name "changed successively to the Travelers' Home and the Western Stage House." **Best reading:** building standing, still in innkeeping use under a changed name in mid‑1835. `[INF]`

### 2.2 Rev. Jesse Walker's cabin / first Methodist meeting house
- First Methodist society organized **14 June 1831**, ten members, in a log cabin. By 1834 the congregation met in a **newly built log cabin**. `[DOC]`
- **Location `[DISPUTED]`:** chicagology/prefire273 puts the "oblong log building erected by Father Walker for worship and schooling" **north of Wentworth's tavern** (i.e., **west bank**); Wikipedia/DRLOIH say the 1834 cabin was on the **north bank**. An 1835 painting (Chicago History Museum) reportedly shows "Wolf's Tavern, Miller's House and the cabin of Reverend Jesse Walker" together in one view. `[DOC]`
- **Status 1835‑07‑01:** standing `[DOC]`. Walker himself **died in 1835** `[DOC]`. In **1838** the congregation floated the cabin across the river and rolled it on logs to Washington & Clark. `[DOC]`
- Compare Wau‑Bun (1831): "a small square log building, originally designed for a school‑house, but occasionally used as a place of worship whenever any itinerant minister presented himself" — "a little remote from these residences." `[DOC]`

### 2.3 James Kinzie's residence
- **Location:** south of Wentworth's tavern, west bank. `[DOC]` (chicagology/prefire273)
- Type/date/appearance: **not attested in sources reached.** `[GAP]`

### 2.4 Robert A. Kinzie's store
- Storehouse dealing in **groceries and Indian goods**, at Wolf Point. `[DOC]` (chicagology/prefire273). Type, exact corner, and 1835 status **unattested.** `[GAP]`

### 2.5 Green Tree Tavern
- **Built 1833**, **northeast corner of Lake St and Canal St** (i.e., west bank, one block west of the river). `[DOC]` — consistent across Wikipedia, chicagology/prefire127, DRLOIH.
- **Builder:** James Kinzie, "the first structure ever erected in the place for hotel purposes." Carpenter/superintendent **`[DISPUTED]`**: chicagology names "Philo Cobb"; DRLOIH names **Silas B. Cobb**. Silas B. Cobb is the historically real person; "Philo Cobb" is likely a conflation with Philo Carpenter. `[INF]`
- **Type `[DISPUTED]`:** chicagology/prefire127 and DRLOIH: **two‑story frame**. chicagology/prefire278: the Green Tree House was **"originally constructed of logs before weatherboarding was added later."** For a 1835 model this matters — recommend two-story, weatherboarded, with the log-core question flagged.
- **Appearance (unusually well documented, because it survived):** two stories; rooms roughly **12 × 12 ft**; **low ceilings ~7½ ft**; **board partitions**; **whitewashed walls**; small‑paned windows; front facing Canal St with the long side on Lake St; bar‑room at one end serving as office/reception; a hall with stairway dividing the building; dining room clearable for dancing; attic used for lodging. `[DOC]` (chicagology/prefire127)
- Name said to derive from a solitary tree nearby (variously oak / green tree). `[DOC]`, `[DISPUTED]` on species.
- **Status 1835‑07‑01: STANDING, in operation.** Landlord **Chester Ingersoll, 1834–1837**, associated with Martin M. Ford `[DOC]` (chicagology). `[DISPUTED]`: DRLOIH lists David Clark and Edward Parsons among operators without dates.
- **Survival — important for texture/appearance evidence:** moved in **1880** to the SE corner of Milwaukee Ave & Fulton St (31–37 Milwaukee Ave), used as a boarding house and saloon, and **demolished August 1902**. It was therefore **photographed** in its later life — those photographs are the closest thing to direct visual evidence of any Wolf Point structure. `[DOC]` **Caveat:** any photo shows the building after ~47 years of alteration and a move. `[INF]`

### 2.6 Western Hotel
- **`[DISPUTED]` date:** chicagology/prefire278 says **built 1834 by W.H. Stow**; DRLOIH hotels chronology says **1835**.
- **Location:** **southeast corner of Randolph St and Canal St** — west side, just south of the Green Tree. `[DOC]`
- **Type:** two‑story frame, roughly **40 × 60 ft in an L**, with stables and yard. `[DOC]` (chicagology) — DRLOIH contradicts with "small structure" `[DISPUTED]`.
- Function: "one of the best hotels," serving **farmers and teamsters** — i.e., the west-side wagon-trade house. `[DOC]`
- **Status 1835‑07‑01:** standing if the 1834 date is right `[INF]`; under construction or brand new if 1835. Flag.

## 3. NORTH BANK OF THE FORKS

### 3.1 Samuel Miller's House / Miller's Tavern (Fork Tavern)
- **Location:** **north bank at the forks** — on the point of land between the **north branch** and the **main stem**, directly opposite Wolf Point. `[DOC]` (Wikipedia; chicagology/prefire273 "on the point between the North Branch and main channel"; Wau‑Bun "on the north side of the river…near the forks").
  **`[DISPUTED]`:** DRLOIH's hotels chronology says "south branch of Chicago River, opposite Wolf Point" — **this appears to be an error**; four independent sources put Miller on the north bank. `[INF]`
- **Dates:** store opened **c. 1827–1829** by **Samuel and John Miller**; **enlarged in 1830 and converted to a tavern**, competing with the Wolf Point Tavern. `[DOC]`
- **Type:** **log cabin with a two‑story frame addition fronting the river.** `[DOC]` (DRLOIH) — chicagology calls it simply a "log structure"; the 1833 view is described as showing "a two‑story building and adjoining log cabin" on that bank, which corroborates the log+frame reading. `[DOC]`/`[INF]`
- **License:** first Peoria County tavern license, issued jointly to **Archibald Clybourn and Samuel Miller**, $100 bond; Miller licensed 13 April 1831. `[DOC]`
- **John Miller's log house (1831)** — built near his brother's tavern, used as a **tannery**, called "Chicago's first recorded factory." `[DOC]` Separate structure, north bank, same cluster.
- **Status 1835‑07‑01 — UNCERTAIN, most likely standing but no longer a tavern.** Evidence: Wau‑Bun (of 1831) already describes the Miller house as **"at this time vacant,"** Miller having "removed to *Rivière du Chemin*…which about this time began to be called Michigan City"; DRLOIH says the business converted to a store, run by **P.F.W. Peck** after 1832; another account says "Miller retired from the field in 1834." `[DOC]` for all three, `[DISPUTED]` in their chronology. **Recommendation:** model the structure as present, in secondary/store or residential use, not innkeeping. `[INF]`

### 3.2 Miller's Ferry
- Authorized **2 June 1829** to **Samuel Miller and Archibald Clybourn**; Clybourn was the ferryman, crossing the **North Branch** between Miller's tavern and the Wolf Point Tavern. `[DOC]`
- **Status 1835‑07‑01: superseded** — replaced by the North Branch log bridge in winter 1831–32. `[DOC]`/`[INF]`

### 3.3 John Kinzie House ("the old Kinzie mansion") — **NOT STANDING on 1835‑07‑01**
- **Location:** **north bank of the main stem, directly facing Fort Dearborn**, at the foot of present Pine St, near modern **N Michigan Ave & Kinzie St**. `[DOC]`
- **Origin:** Jean Baptiste Point du Sable's cabin, c. 1784–1790s; developed by du Sable into "a commodious, well‑furnished French‑style house with numerous outbuildings"; Jean Le Lalime 1800–1803; **John Kinzie from 1804**, who enlarged and improved it. `[DOC]`
- **Appearance (Juliette Kinzie):** "a long, low building, with a **piazza** extending along its front, a range of **four or five rooms**." Log core reported at **22 × 40 ft**. Two large **cottonwoods** behind (one survived as a landmark into the early 20th c.); **Lombardy poplars** shading a broad green between house and river; a cultivated garden to the north; outbuildings including **dairy, bakehouse, stables, and lodging rooms for the French engagés**. `[DOC]`
- **Occupancy chronology after Kinzie's 1828 death:** Anson Taylor (store + home) 1829–31 → Dr. E. D. Harmon (home + office) 1831 → **Jonathan N. Bailey (home + the first post office) 1831** → Mark Noble Sr. 1831–32 → Judge Richard Young (six weeks) 1832 → **unoccupied and "dilapidated beyond repair" from 1832**. `[DOC]`
- **Status: "nonexistent by 1835."** `[DOC]` (DRLOIH Kinzie chronology; corroborated: "unoccupied in 1833, and reported gone by 1835"). The Kinzie Mansion Plaque dates it **1784–1832**. `[DOC]`
- **Reconstruction recommendation:** do **not** place the structure on 1835‑07‑01. `[DOC]`‑backed absence. Whether a ruin, chimney stack, foundation, or the two cottonwoods remained is **unattested** — the cottonwood survival into the 20th c. is documented, so **at least one large cottonwood should be present** at the site. `[DOC]`/`[INF]`

### 3.4 The Indian Agency House — "Cobweb Castle"
- **Location:** **southwest corner of Wolcott St and North Water St** (= N State St & N Water St), north bank, east of the forks. `[DOC]` (Wau‑Bun)
- **Appearance (Wau‑Bun, verbatim):** "a substantial, compact little building of **logs hewed and squared**, with a centre, two wings, and, strictly speaking, two tails, since, when there was found no more room for additions at the sides, they were placed in the rear." Surrounded by **log residences for the agency employees**. `[DOC]`
- Nicknamed **"Cobweb Castle"** in contrast to the South/West Side's "Rat Castle." `[DOC]`
- **Status 1835‑07‑01: `[CONJ]`** — I found no source giving its fate. John H. Kinzie's agency wound down after the 1833 Treaty of Chicago and the 1835 removal; the building most plausibly still stood in July 1835. Flag as low-confidence.

### 3.5 Antoine Ouilmette's cabin
- **Location:** **north bank of the Chicago River, a short distance west of du Sable's cabin**; also described as "a small cabin at the rear of the Kinzie mansion." `[DOC]`
- Ouilmette resident in Chicago from **July 1790 to 1826**; voted in the Chicago poll of 7 Aug 1826; then moved to **Gross Pointe** (Wilmette/Evanston), variously dated **1826** or **c. 1829**. `[DISPUTED]`
- **Status 1835‑07‑01: `[CONJ]` — almost certainly gone.** No source attests its survival past the 1820s; the Kinzie mansion group it belonged to was derelict by 1832. **Recommend omitting**, or modelling as a ruin at most. `[INF]`

### 3.6 Clybourn(e) family cabins and slaughterhouse
- Jonas Clybourn with sons **Archibald and Hanley** arrived **August 1824**; family settled on the **north branch**, building **two log cabins**. `[DOC]`
- **Archibald Clybourn's log slaughterhouse, 1827**, on the **north branch**, supplying beef to the Fort Dearborn garrison; associated stockyard later called Bull's Head. `[DOC]`
- Archibald's larger house (later described as a 20‑room mansion) built **1829** on what is now **Elston Ave**. `[DOC]` — well north/northwest of the forks, outside the 1833 town limits (which stopped at Ohio St). `[INF]`
- **Status 1835‑07‑01:** standing `[INF]`. Precise locations along the north branch **not pinned down in reachable sources** `[GAP]`.

### 3.7 Billy Caldwell (Sauganash) — residence(s)
Two distinct claims, both partly attested:
1. **Government-built frame house, 1828**, erected for Caldwell by the **U.S. Indian Department**, at the **southeast corner of Chicago Ave and N State St** — often called "Chicago's first frame house." `[DOC as a claim]`, but at least one source notes the story is **unverified**. `[DISPUTED]`
2. **Wau‑Bun (1831), at Wolf Point:** "Billy Caldwell, the Sau‑ga‑nash, too, resided here occasionally, with his wife, who was a daughter of Nee‑scot‑nee‑meg." Adjacent: "two or three **log cabins** occupied by **Robinson**, the Pottowattamie chief, and some of his wife's connexions." `[DOC]`
- Caldwell's **1,600‑acre 1829 treaty reserve** lay along the **north branch** northwest of town (modern Sauganash/Edgebrook). `[DOC]`
- **Status 1835‑07‑01:** Caldwell **led his people west in the 1835 removal** `[DOC]`. The State/Chicago Ave house's condition and occupancy in July 1835 is **unattested** `[GAP]`. The Wolf Point log cabins of Robinson/Caldwell are likewise unattested for 1835 `[CONJ]`.

### 3.8 Lake House Hotel — **UNDER CONSTRUCTION on 1835‑07‑01**
- **Location:** north bank block bounded by **Kinzie, Rush, and Michigan (Water) Streets** — the modern Wrigley Building site; "foot of Rush Street bridge." `[DOC]`
- **Ground broken 1835; opened fall 1836.** `[DOC]`
- **Type:** **three stories and a basement, BRICK** — Chicago's first luxury hotel; cost "nearly $100,000." `[DOC]`
- **Backers:** Gurdon S. Hubbard, Gen. David S. Hunter, John H. Kinzie, Dr. W. B. Egan, Maj. James B. Campbell. `[DOC]` — note **Hunter** here, which is your "Hunter" lead.
- **Reconstruction note:** on 1835‑07‑01 this should be a **construction site / partial masonry shell**, not a finished building. `[INF]` Exact progress by July **unattested** `[GAP]`.

### 3.9 Steamboat Hotel (later American Hotel / American Inn)
- **Location:** **North Water St near Kinzie St** — north bank. `[DOC]`
- **1835:** operated by **John Davis**; Nov 1835 J. Dorsey and J. Force; 1836 William McCorristen renames it American Hotel/Inn. `[DOC]`
- **Status 1835‑07‑01:** standing/operating `[INF]` (source dates its start to 1835 without a month). Construction type **unattested** `[GAP]`.

### 3.10 Warehouses on the river
- **Kinzie & Hunter** and **Dole & Newberry** each had warehouses "with its dock along the river front." `[DOC]` — the source does not give banks for both.
- **Newberry & Dole:** a **square frame building on South Water Street**, "Newberry and Dole's Forwarding and Commission House," in views of **c. 1835** — i.e., the **south** bank at that date. `[DOC]` Their famous **grain warehouse/elevator on the north side of the river just east of the present Rush Street bridge** dates to **1838** and belongs to the *first grain shipment* era, **not 1835**. `[DOC]` — **do not place the north-bank Newberry & Dole elevator in an 1835 model.** `[INF]`
- **Kinzie & Hunter:** bank and date **unattested** in sources reached `[GAP]`. Given John H. Kinzie's north-side landholdings, a north-bank location is plausible `[CONJ]`.
- "The whole section about the eastern end of N. Water and Kinzie streets was known as **the Sands**." `[DOC]` — sandy, low, marginal ground; useful for terrain treatment.

### 3.11 Wabansia and Kinzie's Addition
- **Feb 1833:** **John H. Kinzie and Lieut. Hunter** came to Chicago "to lay out lots and open streets through their property, as the place had become a town of about fifty inhabitants." `[DOC]`
- The plat **"Chicago with the School Section and Wabansia and Kinzie's Addition"** exists in an **1834** state (Encyclopedia of Chicago, item 10634; LoC g4104c.ct007620) and a version **corrected 17 June 1835 by surveyor J. Wooley, approved 26 Aug 1835 by the State of Illinois**. `[DOC]` — **This is the single most useful cartographic source for your date**: the corrected plat is contemporaneous to within two weeks of 1835‑07‑01.
- Wabonsia/Kinzie's Addition lay north of the main stem in today's **Fulton River District / River North**; absorbed into Chicago between 1833 and the 1837 city charter. `[DOC]`
- **Reconstruction note:** in July 1835 this is **surveyed and platted but almost entirely unbuilt** — streets staked, lots being traded in the speculative boom, very few structures. `[INF]`
- A small burying‑ground existed on the North Branch in the Wabansia addition (attested retrospectively in 1849 when coffins washed into the river). `[DOC]`, position unknown `[GAP]`.

### 3.12 Brickyard
- **Spring 1833:** **Tyler K. Blodgett** established Chicago's first brickyard **on the North Side**; **Henry S. Lampman** first brickmaker. `[DOC]` Exact site **unattested** `[GAP]`. Relevant: it makes the 1835 Lake House brick locally plausible `[INF]`.

## 5. BRIDGES AND CROSSINGS — state on 1835‑07‑01

| Crossing | Built | Description | Status 1835‑07‑01 |
|---|---|---|---|
| **North Branch** | winter **1831–32**, replacing Clybourn's ferry | Log construction, **~10 ft wide, clearing the water by ~6 ft**. ## 7. What the Forks looked and felt like, summer 1835

`[INF]` unless otherwise marked, synthesised from the documented items above.

Chicago in July 1835 was a **twenty-month-old town of roughly 3,000 people that had grown roughly tenfold in two years** `[DOC]` — a speculative boom-town in the middle of a land mania, with surveyors' stakes running through wet prairie in Wabansia and Kinzie's Addition north of the river, platted only weeks earlier (17 June 1835) `[DOC]`, and virtually nothing built on them yet.

The forks themselves were still the town's social hinge but were **already losing primacy to the Lake Street / South Water Street axis to the east**. The gravitational shift is legible in the buildings: Beaubien had abandoned the Sauganash in 1834 for the **Exchange Coffee House at Lake & Wells** `[DOC]`; the Tremont House (three-story wood, 1833) and Mansion House stood at Lake & Dearborn `[DOC]`; the post office had moved off the forks to Franklin & South Water in 1832 `[DOC]`; and the town's most ambitious project of the year — the **three-story brick Lake House** — was rising not at Wolf Point but a half-mile east on the north bank at Rush and Kinzie `[DOC]`. Wolf Point in July 1835 was **the old town, one step past its peak**.

Visually, at the confluence: on the **west bank**, the log-and-frame Wolf Point Tavern with its painted wolf sign, James Kinzie's house, Robert Kinzie's store, the Methodist log meeting-house; a block west on Lake at Canal, the two-story whitewashed **Green Tree** and, at Randolph and Canal, the L-shaped frame **Western Hotel** with its teamsters' yard and stables. On the **north bank point**, Miller's log-and-frame house, past its tavern days, with John Miller's tannery cabin nearby. On the **south bank at Lake and Market**, the **white two-story Sauganash with its bright blue shutters** `[DOC]` — the smartest building at the forks and by now under a new owner, "Mr. Davis" `[DOC]` — with Philo Carpenter's log drug store immediately alongside on Lake `[DOC]`.

Three crossings tied it together, all crude: a ~10-ft-wide log bridge over the north branch, a **floating raft of logs** over the south branch near Lake, and, a few blocks east, the town's one piece of engineering pride — the **200-foot Dearborn Street drawbridge** with its chain-worked 60-foot draw `[DOC]`.

The ground was low, wet and sandy — the stretch around North Water and Kinzie was literally called **"the Sands"** `[DOC]`; docks and wharves had been going in along both banks since the 1833 harbor dredging `[DOC]`.

And overwhelmingly present that summer: **the removal**. Thousands of Potawatomi were assembled around Chicago awaiting westward removal under the 1833 treaty. Six weeks after your date, on **18 Aug 1835**, some 800 warriors out of about 5,000 assembled people danced from the Council House on the north bank, west along the north bank, across the North Branch bridge, south down West Water St, over the south-branch log raft bridge, past the Sauganash — whose windows were full of newlyweds who had arrived in town weeks earlier — and on to Fort Dearborn. `[DOC]` An eyewitness: "like a carnival of demon spirits who had broken loose from the confines of hell," bodies "covered over with a great variety of brilliant paints," feather work trailing nearly to the ground. `[DOC]` Another account notes Chicago "had begun to present an appearance that would justify the name of a town," striking to Native visitors who remembered when "the grass grew waist deep." `[DOC]` **On 1835‑07‑01 this gathering was already forming.** `[INF]`

## 8. Source quality notes and unresolved gaps

**Sources actually reached and used:**
- Juliette A. M. Kinzie, *Wau‑Bun: The Early Day in the North‑West* (1856), ch. XVII "Chicago in 1831" — https://www.authorama.com/wau-bun-19.html — **the only near-primary descriptive text**, but written 25 years after the events and describing 1831, not 1835.
- chicagology.com: `/prefire/prefire273/` (Wolf's Point and the Town of Chicago), `/prefire/prefire127/` (Green Tree Tavern), `/prefire/prefire278/` (Town of Chicago), `/prefire/prefire330/` (Point-au-Sable Cabin/Kinzie House), `/chicagopostoffice/firstpostoffice/`, `/fortdearborn/lastwardance/`, `/skyscrapers/skyscrapers133/earle7/` (1834 bridge). Much of chicagology's text is transcribed from Andreas and from Fergus's Historical Series.
- Digital Research Library of Illinois History Journal (drloihjournal.blogspot.com): Wolf Point beginnings; Mark Beaubien; chronology of early Chicago hotels; Kinzie house chronology; Antoine Ouilmette; the Wigwam. Dense and useful but **internally inconsistent** and unfootnoted — I have flagged its conflicts above.
- Wikipedia: Wolf Point, Chicago; Sauganash Hotel.
- Illinois State Archives, "Early Chicago 1833–1871," doc 23 — population table.
- Encyclopedia of Chicago item 10634 and LoC g4104c.ct007620 — the 1834/1835 Wabansia & Kinzie's Addition plat.

**Sources I could not extract:**
- **`chicagology.com/prefire/` has no 1833 reconstructed map with a numbered key** — it is an A–Z index page only. The numbered key you're thinking of belongs to **Walter Conley & O. E. Stelzer, "Map of Chicago about 1833" (1932/33 centennial map)**, sold by New World Cartographic (nwcartographic.com/products/12313). Fragments of that key surfaced in search snippets — **No. 11 Green Tree Hotel (W Lake & Canal, 1833), No. 12 Old Kinzie Mansion (dated 1832 on the map), No. 14 Sauganash Hotel (1831)** — but neither the dealer page nor any indexed page reproduces the full legend. **To get it you need a high-resolution scan of the Conley map itself** (Newberry Library or Chicago History Museum map collections are the likely holders). Note the map is a **1932 retrospective reconstruction**, not a period document — treat its key as secondary. Also note its "Old Kinzie Mansion, built 1832" entry conflicts with every textual source.
- **Andreas, *History of Chicago* vol. 1** — the archive.org `_djvu.txt` is too large for WebFetch, which returned only front matter and index. **The index gives you the page targets: "Eagle Exchange (tavern)" p. 106, and "Wharfs, Piers and Early Hotels" pp. 626–631.** Fetch those specific page images/text directly (`archive.org/details/historyofchicago01andr` page-level endpoints, or HathiTrust) — this is the highest-value unopened source for exact dimensions, materials, and 1835 occupancy.
- Encyclopedia of Chicago (`encyclopedia.chicagohistory.org`) returned **HTTP 503** throughout; hmdb.org, wttw.com, geographicus.com and grokipedia returned **403** to the fetcher.

**Highest-priority remaining gaps for the reconstruction:**
1. **Dimensions and plan of the Sauganash** (log core and frame block) — nowhere attested in reachable sources. Andreas p. 106 is the best bet.
2. **Whether the Sauganash had a porch/gallery** — all imagery is retrospective and mutually inconsistent; no scholarly adjudication located. Model as unresolved.
3. **The full Conley 1833 numbered key.**
4. **Kinzie & Hunter warehouse** — bank, date, size.
5. **Exact 1835 status of Cobweb Castle, Billy Caldwell's State St house, and the Robinson cabins.**
6. **Construction progress of the Lake House on 1835‑07‑01.**
7. **North Branch bridge condition in 1835** — the 1832 structure was foot-traffic-only as late as summer 1833, yet carried a mass procession in Aug 1835; a rebuild between those dates is likely but unattested.
