# FAUNA BRIEFING — Chicago Town Site, 1 July 1835

**Scope:** Chicago River forks (Wolf Point) east to Lake Michigan; Kinzie St south to ~Madison St.
**Tags:** `[DOC]` = attested in a period or near-period source for this place/era · `[INF]` = inferred from regional/period evidence + ecology, not attested at this exact spot/date · `[CONJ]` = plausible, no direct support found.

## 0. Reading the sources — four caveats that change what you render

**0.1 "Prairie wolf" ≠ gray wolf.** In 1830s Illinois usage, *prairie wolf* / *brush wolf* meant the **coyote** (*Canis latrans*); "big," "gray," or "timber" wolf meant true *Canis lupus*. County bounty records frequently made no distinction. `[DOC]` — [Pantagraph, "Settlers waged war of extinction against wolves"](https://pantagraph.com/news/local/pfop-settlers-waged-war-of-extinction-against-wolves/article_e85c5c05-7809-5275-b180-80e352607d1f.html); [Coyote — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coyote). **Practical upshot:** the animals in the Chicago 1830s accounts that are explicitly called "prairie wolves" should be modeled as coyotes (~30–40 lb, buff/grizzled, narrow muzzle), not as timber wolves. Where a source just says "wolves" (e.g. the Wentworth meat-house incident) the species is ambiguous — `[INF]` coyote is the safer default for a wolf entering a settled village in daytime.

**0.2 Most of the wildlife testimony is retrospective reminiscence,** collected by Andreas in 1884 from men recalling 1830–1840. Treat the *fact* of the animal as `[DOC]`, the *precise year* as soft.

**0.3 July suppresses the spectacle.** Every headline Chicago wildlife event in the record — passenger pigeon flights, prairie-chicken booming, waterfowl clouds, wolf hunts on the snow — is a **spring, fall, or winter** phenomenon. A 1 July scene is the *quietest* possible wildlife date in the Chicago year: breeding-season territoriality, dispersed birds, young-of-year, heavy insect load, and essentially no migration. Budget accordingly: audio > visible animation.

**0.4 Chicago in July 1835 is a boomtown, not a wilderness.** Gurdon Hubbard, testifying in Andreas: *"Chicago grew very fast and in 1835 there must have been two thousand five hundred people there."* `[DOC]` — [Andreas vol. 1 full-text search](https://ia601809.us.archive.org/fulltext/inside.php?item_id=historyofchicago01andr&doc=historyofchicago01andr&path=/30/items/historyofchicago01andr&q=hogs). Wild-mammal density inside the platted grid should be **low**; the fauna is concentrated at the edges — the marsh strip along the river, the sloughs, the prairie beyond the plat, the north-side timber, and the lakeshore.

## 1. WILD MAMMALS

### 1.1 Wolves / coyotes — the best-attested wild mammal at this site

- **Wolf Point name — the safest attribution is *not* the animal.** Juliette Kinzie, *Wau-Bun* (1856): the place was called Wolf Point *"from its having been the residence of an Indian named 'Moa-way,' or 'the Wolf.'"* `[DOC]` — [chicagology, Wolf's Point and the Town of Chicago](https://chicagology.com/prefire/prefire273/); [Wolf Point, Chicago — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolf_Point,_Chicago). Competing origin stories (tavern sign; a spot where wolves gathered at night) are later and unresolved — Wikipedia states flatly that the origin "is unknown." `[DOC]` that the story is contested.
- **A wolf killed inside the settlement, ca. 1830.** Andreas vol. 1, p. 1293, verbatim: *"Wolves were in those days quite numerous; one had the audacity to enter in the day time Wentworth's meat-house, and was by him killed. His house had for a sign a tall sapling topped off just above a prominent branch…"* `[DOC]` — [Andreas p. 1293 via IA full-text](https://ia601809.us.archive.org/fulltext/inside.php?item_id=historyofchicago01andr&doc=historyofchicago01andr&path=/30/items/historyofchicago01andr&q=%22audacity+to+enter%22). Note the same passage places Elijah Wentworth's tavern and Samuel Miller's tavern at the Forks with a log bridge over the North Branch just north of Miller's — i.e., this happened **at Wolf Point itself**. A painted wolf sign was hung at the tavern by ~1833. `[DOC]` — [DRLOIH Journal, Wolf Point beginnings](https://drloihjournal.blogspot.com/2018/11/wolf-point-beginnings-in-chicago-illinois.html).
- **Wolves audible in daylight inside the town.** Andreas vol. 1, p. 433, a schoolgirl's reminiscence: *"What few buildings there were then were mostly on Water Street… It was not uncommon in going to or from school, to see prairie wolves, and we could hear them howl any time in the day."* `[DOC]` — [Andreas p. 433](https://ia601809.us.archive.org/fulltext/inside.php?item_id=historyofchicago01andr&doc=historyofchicago01andr&path=/30/items/historyofchicago01andr&q=%22prairie+wolves%22). This is the single most render-relevant wildlife quote for the town site: **coyotes visible and audible in daytime, within sight of Water Street**.
- **Wolves killed settlers' pigs — 1833, on the record.** Charles Latrobe's eyewitness inventory of Chicago during the 1833 treaty, reprinted in Andreas p. 263: *"…dealers in pigs, poultry, and potatoes; men pursuing Indian claims, some for tracts of land, others like our friend 'Snipe,' for pigs which the wolves had eaten…"* `[DOC]` — [Andreas p. 263](https://ia601809.us.archive.org/fulltext/inside.php?item_id=historyofchicago01andr&doc=historyofchicago01andr&path=/30/items/historyofchicago01andr&q=%22the+wolves+had+eaten%22).
- **Wolf coursing on the prairie, winter 1835–36** — the closest-dated account to your target. Andreas p. 291, verbatim: *"Mr. Lincoln had a favorite horse, an iron grey, and quite fleet on foot, particularly so when in pursuit of a prairie wolf. Many a time in the winter of 1835-36 I have seen Mr. Lincoln mount his horse when a wolf was in sight on the prairie toward Bridgeport, and within an hour's time come in with the wolf…"* `[DOC]` — [Andreas p. 291](https://ia601809.us.archive.org/fulltext/inside.php?item_id=historyofchicago01andr&doc=historyofchicago01andr&path=/30/items/historyofchicago01andr&q=%22Mr.+Lincoln+mount+his+horse%22). **This is a tavern-keeper named Lincoln, not Abraham Lincoln** — label carefully in any provenance note. It also fixes a **direction**: coyotes routinely visible "on the prairie toward Bridgeport," i.e. SSW of the town, up the South Branch.
- **Wolf bounties: NOT yet in force in 1835 in this county, as far as I can document.** The first county cash bounties for wolf scalps in the immediate region are dated **1838** (Lake County, IL). `[DOC]` — [Lake County History: A Century of Wolf Hunts](https://lakecountyhistory.blogspot.com/2019/01/a-century-of-wolf-hunts.html). I found **no** Cook County wolf-bounty record for 1835 in Andreas (searches for "wolf scalps" returned only Indian-war scalp references). **Negative finding — do not stage a bounty scene at 1835.** `[DOC negative, with the caveat that absence in a full-text search is not proof]`
- **July behavior:** coyote pups born Apr–May are out of the den and travelling with adults by July; adults hunt crepuscularly on rodents, and scavenge. `[INF]`

### 1.2 Muskrat — the signature small mammal of the town site

- *"Our public Square [where the Court House and City Hall now stand] was then a pond, where the Indians had trapped the muskrat, and where the first settlers hunted ducks."* `[DOC]` — [chicagology prefire273](https://chicagology.com/prefire/prefire273/). The Public Square is Randolph–Washington / Clark–LaSalle: **inside your zone**.
- Andreas, on Chicago as an Indian Agency of ~14 houses and 75–100 people: *"The staple business seemed to be carried on by the Indians and run-away soldiers, who hunted ducks and muskrats in the marshes. There was a great deal of low land, and mostly destitute of timber."* `[DOC]` — [Andreas, "ducks" search](https://ia601809.us.archive.org/fulltext/inside.php?item_id=historyofchicago01andr&doc=historyofchicago01andr&path=/30/items/historyofchicago01andr&q=ducks). Dates to ~1828–30 (Hubbard occupying the fort), so `[INF]` for continuity into 1835 — but the marshes were still there.
- **Muskrat had cash value at Chicago:** Andreas p. 203 quotes a fur-trade letter, *"The cash price of good muskrat skins at this place during the past summer was 25 cents per skin"*; a later Chicago price table gives *"Muskrat, per skin 03¢ @ .10."* `[DOC]` for the quotes — [Andreas "muskrat" search](https://ia601809.us.archive.org/fulltext/inside.php?item_id=historyofchicago01andr&doc=historyofchicago01andr&path=/30/items/historyofchicago01andr&q=muskrat); **year of the price table unverified** — treat as `[DOC, undated]`.
- **July:** muskrats are active year-round, most visible at dawn/dusk; push-ups are a winter feature, but **bank burrows, cut-cattail feeding platforms, and V-wakes in still water** are correct for July. `[INF]`

### 1.3 Deer (white-tailed)

- Regional presence firmly attested; the 1897 Chicago Academy of Sciences retrospective states deer were still being killed in the Chicago area **as late as the 1870s**. `[DOC, retrospective]` — [chicagology, 1830 — Chicago as a Hunting Post](https://chicagology.com/prefire/huntingpost/) (source: *Chicago Tribune*, 10 June 1897).
- Chicago fur/produce price tables in Andreas list *"Deer, per skin 75 @ 1.00"* and an export line *"Deer Skins, pounds 28,259."* `[DOC, undated in my extraction]` — [Andreas "Deer Skins"](https://ia601809.us.archive.org/fulltext/inside.php?item_id=historyofchicago01andr&doc=historyofchicago01andr&path=/30/items/historyofchicago01andr&q=%22Deer+Skins%22). The 28,259 lb figure implies a substantial regional deer harvest funnelling through Chicago.
- **Inside the town grid on a July day: unlikely.** Deer would be in the north-side timber and the groves beyond the plat, and by July are in hard antler-growth (bucks in velvet) with does hiding spotted fawns. `[INF]`

### 1.4 Fox, raccoon, mink, otter, skunk, opossum, woodchuck, rabbit, squirrel

All of these are listed for the Chicago region by the Chicago Academy of Sciences retrospective: *red and grey fox, brown and black mink, otter, raccoon, opossum, skunk, muskrat, woodchuck/groundhog, striped and grey gophers, western fox squirrel, grey rabbit, meadow/prairie mouse, moles and short-tailed shrew.* `[DOC, retrospective / regional]` — [chicagology huntingpost](https://chicagology.com/prefire/huntingpost/). Juliette Kinzie's fur-trade list independently attests the trade species: *"beaver, otter, marten, mink, silver-gray and red fox, wolf, bear, and wild-cat, musk-rat, and smoked deer-skins."* `[DOC]` — [Wau-Bun ch. II, authorama](http://www.authorama.com/wau-bun-4.html).

For the **town site specifically in July 1835**, all are `[INF]`: raccoon and skunk are the two most likely to be *seen in town* (night, garbage, slaughter offal); mink along the river bank; fox on the prairie margin; grey rabbit and fox squirrel in the north-side timber and yard edges.

### 1.5 Beaver — treat as effectively absent from the town site

- No Chicago-proper 1830s beaver record surfaced. The Academy retrospective places beaver **"particularly in the Calumet region,"** i.e. 12+ miles south. `[DOC, retrospective]` — [chicagology huntingpost](https://chicagology.com/prefire/huntingpost/).
- Statewide: beaver "becoming rare in Illinois by the mid-1800s," eliminated from the state by the early 1900s, reintroduced from the 1920s. `[DOC]` — [Illinois DNR, American beaver](https://dnr.illinois.gov/education/wildaboutpages/wildaboutwildmammals/wildaboutmammalsrodents/wawmamericanbeaver.html); [Forest Preserve District of Will County](https://www.reconnectwithnature.org/headlines/news/5-fun-beaver-facts-international-beaver-day). The beaver-hat market had already collapsed by the 1830s (silk hats), reducing trapping pressure but not restoring local populations.
- **Verdict: do not place beaver, lodges, or beaver-cut stumps at the forks in July 1835.** `[INF — negative]`

## 2. BIRDS

### 2.1 Passenger pigeon — spectacular, but almost certainly NOT on 1 July

- **The Chicago record:** a Chicago newspaper of **17 September 1836** reported that within the past several days *"our town was swarming with pigeons, the horizon in almost every direction was black with them."* `[DOC]` — [Project Passenger Pigeon / Chicago Academy of Sciences Nature Museum](https://naturemuseum.org/cas/project-passenger-pigeon); Joel Greenberg's Chicago-region work is the anchor here ([Greenberg, *A Natural History of the Chicago Region*](https://books.google.com/books/about/A_Natural_History_of_the_Chicago_Region.html?id=OXA1VVOSUg4C)).
- **Seasonality:** that record is **mid-September** — a fall movement. Passenger pigeons nested in huge colonies in spring (roughly March–May) and the great river-of-birds flights were spring and autumn. On **1 July** you would expect **small, loose, wandering post-breeding flocks and family groups** feeding on early mast and grain, not a sky-darkening flight. `[INF]`
- **Render guidance:** if you show pigeons at all, show **tens, not millions** — fast, direct, long-tailed birds crossing over the timber. A darkened sky on 1 July would be an anachronism of season, not of species.

### 2.2 Greater prairie-chicken (*Tympanuchus cupido pinnatus*) — present, but silent in July

- Documented **in the Chicago area** by Marquette, 1674–75; estimated in the **millions** in Illinois at settlement; standard fare on early settlers' tables, in boarding houses, hotels and market stalls, "highly esteemed." `[DOC]` — [Outdoor Illinois Journal, Greater Prairie-chicken](https://outdoor.wildlifeillinois.org/articles/greater-prairie-chicken-once-the-quintessential-game-bird-of-the-prairie-state); [Illinois State Museum](https://story.illinoisstatemuseum.org/content/greater-prairie-chicken).
- John White's *Early Accounts of the Ecology of Cook County* (2002) has a dedicated prairie-chicken section, confirming these birds are a Cook County staple in the early sporting literature. `[DOC — that the section exists; I could not retrieve the text, ResearchGate returns 403]` — [White 2002 on ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/273000448_Early_Accounts_of_the_Ecology_of_Cook_County).
- **July behavior — critical:** the famous **booming on leks is a March–May display.** By 1 July the leks are silent and the birds are dispersed: **hens with half-grown broods creeping through tall grass on the west-side prairie**, flushing explosively on whirring wings if approached. `[INF]`

### 2.3 Waterfowl and the town's ponds

- **Attested, specifically here:** *"…where the first settlers hunted ducks"* on the Public Square pond, and Indians/runaway soldiers *"hunted ducks and muskrats in the marshes."* `[DOC]` — [chicagology prefire273](https://chicagology.com/prefire/prefire273/); [Andreas "ducks"](https://ia601809.us.archive.org/fulltext/inside.php?item_id=historyofchicago01andr&doc=historyofchicago01andr&path=/30/items/historyofchicago01andr&q=ducks).
- **Regional wet-prairie assemblage:** ducks, geese, swans, rails, prairie-chickens, sandhill cranes, warblers, red-winged blackbirds and herons; plus turtles, frogs and muskrats. `[DOC — habitat-level]` — [Illinois DNR, *Wetlands*](https://dnr.illinois.gov/content/dam/soi/en/web/dnr/education/documents/onlineintroillinoisnatres-9-.pdf).
- **Deep-time baseline at this exact spot:** Marquette's journal, 6 April 1675, on Lake Michigan and the Calumet: *"The two lakes… are full of bustards, geese, ducks, cranes and other birds that we do not know."* `[DOC, 1675]` — [Andreas "ducks"](https://ia601809.us.archive.org/fulltext/inside.php?item_id=historyofchicago01andr&doc=historyofchicago01andr&path=/30/items/historyofchicago01andr&q=ducks).
- **July composition:** duck *hunting* is a fall activity; on 1 July the sloughs hold **breeding residents** — blue-winged teal and mallard hens with ducklings, wood duck in the timbered river margins, plus **pied-billed grebe, American bittern, least bittern, Virginia rail, sora, common gallinule** in the cattails and **red-winged blackbird** everywhere. Most adult ducks are entering **eclipse plumage / flightless wing moult** in late June–July — dull, skulking, not flying. `[INF]` No V-formations, no big rafts.

### 2.4 Sandhill crane

- Sandhill cranes **probably bred fairly commonly in Illinois until about 1890**, with the last Illinois nest found in 1872. `[DOC]` — [Outdoor Illinois Journal, Illinois' Cranes](https://outdoor.wildlifeillinois.org/articles/illinois-cranes-a-storied-past-and-a-hopeful-future); marsh nest sites — [Illinois DNR, sandhill crane](https://dnr.illinois.gov/education/wildaboutpages/wildaboutbirds/wildaboutbirdsrails/wabsandhillcrane.html).
- **Therefore in July 1835 cranes are breeding residents, not migrants.** Expect **pairs with one or two rusty-brown colts** in the larger marshes; the unison call carries miles. This is one of the few 1 July sounds that is both spectacular and defensible. `[INF, strong]`
- Do **not** stage a migration spectacle — the huge staging flocks are October–November and February–March.

### 2.5 Shorebirds, gulls, terns at the lakeshore and river mouth

- **Shorebirds:** the Chicago lakeshore is a documented shorebird corridor with 35+ species at the beaches — but overwhelmingly on **migration**. `[DOC, modern]` — [BCN, The Lake Michigan Flyway](https://bcnbirds.org/wp-content/uploads/BCN-The-Lake-Michigan-Flyway.pdf). **On 1 July the lakefront is at its annual shorebird minimum** — the spring flight is over, southbound adult shorebirds do not arrive in numbers until mid-July. Expect only **killdeer** (a local breeder, noisy, on bare/gravelly ground and roadsides), **spotted sandpiper** (breeding on the beach/river margin, teetering gait), and possibly the first one or two southbound adults. `[INF]`
- **Gulls — the biggest anachronism risk in the whole brief.** **Ring-billed gulls, the default modern Chicago gull, were rare/near-extirpated in eastern North America and only became the most abundant Great Lakes colonial waterbird after 1916 protection.** `[DOC]` — [Waterbirds, "The Rise and Fall of Ring-Billed Gulls"](https://bioone.org/journals/waterbirds/volume-39/issue-sp1/063.039.sp101/The-Rise-and-Fall-of-Ring-Billed-Gulls-Larus-delawarensis/10.1675/063.039.sp101.short). **Do not populate the 1835 river mouth with a modern parking-lot gull flock.** A small number of **herring gulls** and **common terns** (the historic Great Lakes breeders) is the defensible choice. `[INF]`
- **Bald eagle:** once common in northeastern Illinois; the last Cook County nests were recorded in **1896 and 1897**. `[DOC]` — [Chicago Bird Alliance, The History of Bald Eagles in Chicago](https://chicagobirdalliance.org/blog/2026/7/2/the-history-of-bald-eagles-in-chicago). So in 1835 eagles are **breeding-season residents of the region**, and an adult or a brown subadult over the harbour/river mouth is well within reason — but I found **no specific 1830s Chicago eagle sighting**. Use sparingly, one bird, high. `[INF]`

### 2.6 Songbirds of the oak savanna and north-side timber

No 1830s Chicago bird list survives in the sources I could reach. The Academy retrospective's bird section is a bare taxonomic count (nine thrushes, one bluebird, three warblers, one chickadee/titmouse for northern Illinois). `[DOC, thin]` — [chicagology huntingpost](https://chicagology.com/prefire/huntingpost/). Oak ecosystems covered ~1 million acres / 22% of the Chicago region at the 1830s public land survey. `[DOC]` — [Oak Ecosystem Recovery Plan, Chicago Wilderness region](https://mortonarb.org/app/uploads/2024/09/Oak-Ecosystem-Recovery-Plan.pdf).

For a **July dawn/day soundscape in the north-side timber and savanna edge**, the defensible `[INF]` set — all still breeding in Cook County oak habitat and all singing on 1 July — is: **red-headed woodpecker** (explicitly named as a Cook County savanna indicator by the Forest Preserves — [FPDCC, Our Ecosystems](https://fpdcc.com/nature/a-tour-of-our-ecosystems/)), northern flicker, eastern wood-pewee, great crested flycatcher, eastern kingbird, blue jay, black-capped chickadee, house wren, wood thrush, American robin, brown thrasher, gray catbird, indigo bunting, eastern towhee, Baltimore oriole, common yellowthroat (marsh edge), field/song/vesper sparrow (prairie), eastern meadowlark and bobolink (prairie), red-winged blackbird, mourning dove, northern bobwhite. **Absent by definition:** house sparrow (N. America 1851) and European starling (1890) — `[DOC — negative, hard anachronism check]`.

**A charming, dated, verifiable detail:** in 1837 a visitor stepping out of a Chicago hotel onto a three-foot plank sidewalk *"saw to my astonishment a flock of quail on the plank."* `[DOC]` — [Andreas p. 977](https://ia601809.us.archive.org/fulltext/inside.php?item_id=historyofchicago01andr&doc=historyofchicago01andr&path=/30/items/historyofchicago01andr&q=%22prairie+wolves%22). Two years after your date, in the middle of town: **bobwhite quail on the sidewalk is a documented Chicago street-level bird.**

## 3. FISH & AQUATIC LIFE

### 3.1 What was in the water

- Andreas vol. 1, p. 1219 gives the fullest list of *"the ichthyological specimens that are found in the waters adjacent to Chicago"*: **three darters; four perches; one bass; eight sunfishes; one each pirate perch and maigre [freshwater drum]; four sculpins; one cod [burbot]; two sticklebacks; one each silverside and toothed minnow; two pikes; one trout-perch; seven salmon [whitefish, ciscoes, lake trout]; one each gizzard shad and herring; twelve carps [minnows]; seven suckers; three catfishes; and one each of the eel and the sturgeon family.** `[DOC, 1884 compilation]` — [Andreas p. 1219](https://ia601809.us.archive.org/fulltext/inside.php?item_id=historyofchicago01andr&doc=historyofchicago01andr&path=/30/items/historyofchicago01andr&q=%22Sturgeon+family%22). Compiled in 1884, i.e. *after* fifty years of degradation — so it is a **conservative floor** for 1835, not a ceiling. `[INF]` for 1835.
- **Lake sturgeon**: abundant in Lake Michigan and its nearshore waters at settlement, spawning in all the major tributaries. `[DOC]` — [Michigan Sea Grant, Lake Sturgeon](https://www.michiganseagrant.org/topics/ecosystems-and-habitats/native-species-and-biodiversity/lake-sturgeon/); [Illinois DNR, lake sturgeon](https://dnr.illinois.gov/education/wildaboutpages/wildaboutfishes/wafsturgeon/waflakesturgeon.html).
- **Whitefish and lake trout** were *the* valued Lake Michigan food fishes. Juliette Kinzie calls them *"trout and whitefish, those treasures of the deep."* `[DOC]` — [Wau-Bun ch. II](http://www.authorama.com/wau-bun-4.html).
- **Pre-settlement Chicago River:** a slow-moving prairie stream draining low-lying wetlands via a North and a South Branch; "fish flourished." `[DOC, general]` — [Friends of the Chicago River](https://www.chicagoriver.org/about-the-river/river-ecology-and-wildlife/fish).

### 3.2 Was there a commercial fishery at Chicago in July 1835? — probably not yet, at scale

- Commercial fishing along the Lake Michigan shore **began in the 1830s**, and in the 1830s the **American Fur Company organized the first large-scale Great Lakes commercial fishery** — a venture that **failed**, and was followed by other operations only in the 1840s–50s. `[DOC]` — [Michigan DNR, history of Great Lakes commercial fishing](https://www.michigan.gov/dnr/managing-resources/fisheries/business/commercial/history-of-state-licensed-great-lakes-commercial-fishing); [Great Lakes Now](https://www.greatlakesnow.org/2019/11/25/great-lakes-commercial-fishing-history/). The centre of gravity was **Mackinac**, where ~1,200 barrels of whitefish and trout were taken in 1836. `[DOC]` — [Clarke Historical Library, CMU](https://www.cmich.edu/research/clarke-historical-library/explore-collection/explore-online/native-american-material/native-american-treaty-rights/contemporary-issues/treaty-rights-and-the-great-lakes-fishery).
- **Sturgeon were not a commercial target in 1835** — in the early 1800s fishermen "didn't think lake sturgeon were worth eating" and dried them for **steamship fuel**; sturgeon only became valuable after the 1860s. `[DOC]` — [Michigan Sea Grant](https://www.michiganseagrant.org/topics/ecosystems-and-habitats/native-species-and-biodiversity/lake-sturgeon/).
- **The Encyclopedia of Chicago's "Fishing" article** ([encyclopedia.chicagohistory.org/pages/300069.html](http://www.encyclopedia.chicagohistory.org/pages/300069.html)) is the right authority for Chicago-specific dates; **the server returned 503 on three attempts** — flag this as an **open verification item**.
- **Render verdict:** at Chicago on 1 July 1835, show **subsistence and small-market fishing** — a shore seine, set lines, spearing, a skiff or two, fish drying — **not** a barrel-stacked commercial fishery. `[INF]`

### 3.3 Frogs, turtles, mussels

- **Frogs, at a named intersection, in July.** The *Chicago American*, **9 July 1836**, called attention to *"a pond of water on Lake Street, corner of La Salle, inhabited by frogs. It smells strong now, and in a few days will send out a horrible stench."* `[DOC]` — [chicagology, South Water Street](https://chicagology.com/prefire/prefire062/). **This is your single best-dated July fauna fact:** same season, one year later, one block from the Public Square, inside your zone. `[INF]` that the pond and its frogs were there in July 1835.
- **July frog chorus composition** for a wet Illinois prairie/marsh: green frog (banjo "gunk"), bullfrog (deep jug-o-rum), gray treefrog (trill), cricket frog (pebble-clicking), leopard frog. Chorus frog and spring peeper peak March–May and are largely finished by 1 July. `[INF]`
- **Turtles:** common snapping turtle occurs throughout Illinois; the Chicago area supports ~a dozen turtle species including painted, musk, spiny softshell, and (state-endangered today) Blanding's and spotted. `[DOC, modern]` — [Friends of the Chicago River, Herps](https://www.chicagoriver.org/about-the-river/river-ecology-and-wildlife/herps); [Shedd Aquarium, Turtles in Our Midst](https://www.sheddaquarium.org/stories/turtles-in-our-midst). **Painted turtles basking on logs** is the animatable behaviour, and July is nesting season — females crossing open ground to dig. `[INF]`
- **Freshwater mussels:** long harvested in the Great Lakes region for food, tools and jewellery by Native peoples, and later over-harvested by settlers for the pearl-button trade. `[DOC]` — [Shedd Aquarium, Uncovering Mussels in the Rivers and Creeks of the Chicago Area](https://www.sheddaquarium.org/stories/uncovering-mussels-in-the-rivers-and-creeks-of-the-chicago-area). For 1835: **live mussel beds in the river bottom + shell middens/scatter on the banks** are defensible; button-cutting is a much later industry. `[INF]`
- **Snakes and other herps** in the regional list: rattlesnakes, hog-nosed, king, black, garter, water, grass snakes; salamanders and lizards. `[DOC, regional/retrospective]` — [chicagology huntingpost](https://chicagology.com/prefire/huntingpost/); [WBEZ Curious City](https://www.wbez.org/curious-city/2013/03/05/what-would-chicago-look-like-if-settlers-hadnt-changed-it). Garter and water snakes are the plausible town-edge species. `[INF]`

## 4. INSECTS

### 4.1 Mosquitoes — the defining July experience, and it is documented

- **Chicago, 1834:** residents were *"situated in the midst of sloughs and marshes and the sluggish Chicago River,"* in log buildings with **no window screens**; the slough *"produced clouds of mosquitoes carrying the ague to nearby homesteads."* `[DOC, secondary quoting period material]` — surfaced from [historyonthefox, "The ague: If it's not one thing, it's another"](https://historyonthefox.wordpress.com/2015/07/20/the-ague-if-its-not-one-thing-its-another/) (**note: this page returned HTTP 403 on direct fetch; the text above comes from the search index, so treat the exact wording as unverified**).
- **Jerry Church, 1830, at the Chicago portage region:** he and a companion *"had to lay down to sleep in part of an old canoe that had floated ashore, and fight the musquitoes all night"*; elsewhere *"musquitoes and gallinippers"* were *"thick enough."* `[DOC, period diary — same 403 caveat on the host page]` — same source. ("Gallinipper" = a large biting fly/mosquito in period usage.)
- **Note on Andreas:** full-text searches of Andreas vol. 1 for *mosquito*, *mosquitoes*, and the period spelling *musquitoes* **all returned zero matches**. `[DOC — negative]` — Andreas is a business-and-civic history and does not record the nuisance. Do not expect to source mosquitoes from Andreas.
- **Ague (malaria) was endemic** to the Illinois wet-prairie frontier and the I&M Canal works were later "plagued by malaria, rattlesnakes, mosquitoes, and leeches." `[DOC]` — [WBEZ Curious City](https://www.wbez.org/curious-city/2013/03/05/what-would-chicago-look-like-if-settlers-hadnt-changed-it). **Render implication:** smudge fires, mosquito bars/netting over beds, people covered up at dusk, hands moving at faces — the *human behaviour* is the visible signal, since the insects themselves won't read at scale.

### 4.2 Fireflies

- *Photinus pyralis* (common eastern firefly / "big dipper") is **common and native to Illinois**, and **in Chicago "is most often seen in July evenings in fields, gardens, and edges."** It needs moisture at all life stages and favours damp soil, long grass, wet meadows and pond margins. `[DOC, modern natural history]` — [Illinois DNR, common eastern firefly](https://dnr.illinois.gov/education/wildaboutpages/wildaboutinvertebrates/wildaboutbeetles/waicommoneasternfirefly.html); [Edgewater Glen Natives](https://www.glennatives.com/blog/photinus-pyralis-common-eastern-firefly-big-dipper-firefly); [Firefly Atlas, Big dipper firefly](https://www.fireflyatlas.org/focal-species/big-dipper-firefly).
- **This is the highest-value, lowest-risk atmospheric element in the whole brief.** A wet-prairie, slough-riddled July Chicago at dusk should be **dense** with fireflies over the grass and marsh margins — signature **J-shaped upswept flash** for *P. pyralis* males, ~every 5–6 seconds at dusk. `[INF]` for 1835, and the pre-drainage landscape would have supported *more*, not fewer, than today.

### 4.3 The July insect soundscape

- **Annual / "dog-day" cicadas** (*Neotibicen* spp.) are on the wing **July–September**. `[DOC]` — [Outdoor Illinois Journal, The Magical Screaming Cicadas](https://outdoor.wildlifeillinois.org/articles/the-magical-screaming-cicadas).
- **Prairie cicada** (*Megatibicen dorsatus*) — *"a sound unique to the tallgrass prairie… adults emerge in mid-summer,"* lower-pitched than the dog-day cicada; look and listen for it in July–August in original prairie. `[DOC]` — [Illinois State Museum, Prairie Cicada](https://story.illinoisstatemuseum.org/content/prairie-cicada); [Illinois News Bureau, Following the sounds of prairie cicadas](https://news.illinois.edu/view/6367/1101498406). **For a west-side tallgrass scene this is the historically distinctive sound**, and the same sources note that because the prairie was destroyed before it was studied, *"we may never know what the cacophony of insect songs sounded like in this distinctive habitat"* — a good provenance caveat to attach.
- **Periodical cicadas — explicit negative.** Northern Illinois is **Brood XIII** territory (17-year). `[DOC]` — [Brood XIII, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brood_XIII). Anchoring on the documented 2024 emergence and stepping back by 17s gives 2007, 1990, …, 1854, **1837**, 1820. **1835 was not a Brood XIII year** (and Brood XIII emerges late May–June, not July, in any case). `[INF — arithmetic from the documented cycle]` **Do not add a periodical cicada emergence.**
- **Also correct for 1 July, all `[INF]`:** ground crickets and field crickets (building through July), meadow katydids in the wet prairie (fine, high, continuous), true katydids in the timber from mid-July, dragonflies and damselflies hawking over the sloughs and river (very animatable — green darners, skimmers), deer flies and horse flies around livestock and horses in daylight, butterflies on July prairie bloom (monarch, regal fritillary, swallowtails), and a heavy blowfly presence around the slaughterhouses and the river's dead-animal dumping (see §5.5).

## 5. DOMESTIC ANIMALS — the built-environment texture

### 5.1 Pigs at large — and a nuance worth getting right

- **The town code of 7 November 1833** — Chicago's first code of laws — **forbade residents to let pigs wander in the streets**, alongside bans on firing guns, racing horses in the principal streets, exhibiting a stallion publicly, stealing bridge timber, and **throwing dead animals in the river**. Enforcement depended on citizens and county officers; there was no police force, and half of any fine went to the informer. `[DOC]` — [chicagology, Town of Chicago](https://chicagology.com/prefire/prefire278/).
- **But Andreas records a later, "first" city ordinance:** *"April 21, hogs were first prohibited from running at large in the streets of Chicago by ordinance of the city. Prior to that time the too numerous presence of this animal had been frequently animadverted upon in the columns of the Press, as having become an unmitigated nuisance."* `[DOC]` — [Andreas p. 323](https://ia601809.us.archive.org/fulltext/inside.php?item_id=historyofchicago01andr&doc=historyofchicago01andr&path=/30/items/historyofchicago01andr&q=%22running+at+large%22). Chicago was not a **city** until 1837, so this is a **post-1837 April 21** (most likely 1839; I could not confirm the year — **open verification item**, and the 1839 *Laws and Ordinances of the City of Chicago* is print-only at the Chicago History Museum per [CPL](https://www.chipublib.org/blogs/post/the-chicago-municipal-code-how-to-find-and-research-historical-codes/)).
- **Reconciliation, and the render call:** the 1833 town rule existed and was **evidently a dead letter** — the press was still calling loose hogs "an unmitigated nuisance" years later, and the city had to legislate afresh. **In July 1835, put pigs in the streets.** The ordinance is a sign the problem was real, not that it was solved. `[INF, well-supported]`
- **Independent 1833 confirmation of free-range pigs and poultry:** Latrobe's Chicago inventory names *"dealers in pigs, poultry, and potatoes"* and men claiming compensation *"for pigs which the wolves had eaten."* `[DOC]` — [Andreas p. 263](https://ia601809.us.archive.org/fulltext/inside.php?item_id=historyofchicago01andr&doc=historyofchicago01andr&path=/30/items/historyofchicago01andr&q=%22the+wolves+had+eaten%22). **Free-ranging pigs + wolves eating them is a single documented Chicago fact — an excellent provenance-tracked detail.**

### 5.2 Cattle, oxen, and the slaughterhouses — correcting the drive direction

- **Archibald Clybourne** was the **Government butcher for the Pottawatomies** and ran a **log slaughter-house on the east side of the North Branch**; in 1833 he packed there some **250 head of cattle and about 2,000 hogs**, and George W. Dole packed a further 250 cattle and 1,000 hogs at the same place. `[DOC]` — [Andreas "hogs" search](https://ia601809.us.archive.org/fulltext/inside.php?item_id=historyofchicago01andr&doc=historyofchicago01andr&path=/30/items/historyofchicago01andr&q=hogs). He also held the federal contract to supply beef to Fort Dearborn, from a stockyard called Bull's Head. `[DOC, secondary]` — [Archibald Clybourn, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archibald_Clybourn).
- **Other operations live in 1834–35:** Dole's earliest packing (1832, 200 cattle + 350 hogs; 338 hogs in December 1832) near **Dearborn & South Water**, with the packing-house near the SE corner of Dearborn and South Water; **Newberry & Dole's slaughter-house on the South Branch (1834, ~300 cattle + 1,400 hogs)**; and **Gurdon Hubbard packing in the old bank building at the corner of Lake and LaSalle in 1834 — 5,000 hogs, which for want of barrels had to be "stowed away in bulk" until barrels arrived from Cleveland the next spring at $1 apiece.** `[DOC]` — same Andreas source. **Lake & LaSalle is the Frog Pond corner** — a packing house and a stinking frog pond at the same intersection is a real, sourceable juxtaposition.
- **The drives came from the SOUTH, not up the North Branch.** Hubbard, testifying: *"Very few hogs. The hogs had all to come from Wabash, one hundred and fifty miles down… We then went down to the Wabash country, as we called it, and bought cattle and hogs and drove them up for market."* `[DOC]` — [Andreas "hogs"](https://ia601809.us.archive.org/fulltext/inside.php?item_id=historyofchicago01andr&doc=historyofchicago01andr&path=/30/items/historyofchicago01andr&q=hogs). **Correction to the brief's premise:** Clybourn's slaughterhouse was **on** the North Branch, but the stock came **up from the Wabash country** along the Vincennes trace — approach the town from the south/southeast, then move north to the slaughterhouse. `[DOC]`
- **Seasonality — important.** Packing was a **cold-weather** business (December killing; barrels made "during the winter"). On **1 July 1835 there is no packing season.** What you *should* see is **fresh butchering for local consumption and for the garrison** — a few beeves, a killing yard, hides on frames, flies, dogs, offal, and buzzards/crows/gulls at the waste. `[INF]`
- **Beef price in the market report of the period:** *"beef, fresh, sold by butchers, 8 cents per pound."* `[DOC, undated in extraction]` — same Andreas source.

### 5.3 Horses

- **Horse-dealers and horse-stealers are in Latrobe's 1833 inventory of Chicago** — *"you will find horse-dealers, and horse stealers — rogues of every description."* `[DOC]` — [Andreas p. 263](https://ia601809.us.archive.org/fulltext/inside.php?item_id=historyofchicago01andr&doc=historyofchicago01andr&path=/30/items/historyofchicago01andr&q=%22the+Indians+howled%22).
- **Indian horse herds around the town:** Patrick Shirreff (1833) describes the forests and prairies in the neighbourhood *studded with the tents of Indians and numerous herds of horses browsing in all directions.* `[DOC]` — [Shirreff, Chicago (1833)](https://minnstate.pressbooks.pub/ushistory1/chapter/chicago-1833/). **This is a 1833 condition; by July 1835 the Potawatomi were in their final Chicago summer** — they assembled at Chicago in **August 1835** for the last annuity payment and their final departure west. `[DOC]` — [chicagology, Chicago's Last Great War Dance](https://chicagology.com/fortdearborn/lastwardance/); [Patrick T. Reardon, Chicago's Trail of Tears](https://patricktreardon.com/essay-chicagos-trail-of-tears/). So a **1 July 1835** scene sits **weeks before** that gathering: Potawatomi ponies around the town's margins are `[INF]`, and the great encampment itself would be an anachronism by a month or two.
- The Andreas prairie-wolf coursing account confirms **private saddle horses kept at taverns** and fast enough to run down a coyote. `[DOC]`
- **Dray and stage teams:** `[INF]` — a town of ~2,500 with lake commerce, a new harbour and a land boom necessarily runs draft teams; I did not find an 1835-specific Chicago teamster/stage census.

### 5.4 Chickens, dogs, and the fort's livestock

- **Poultry:** "dealers in pigs, poultry, and potatoes," 1833. `[DOC]` — Andreas p. 263. Free-ranging dooryard chickens are `[INF]` and near-certain.
- **Dogs:** I found **no** direct Chicago-1835 dog ordinance or description in these sources. `[CONJ]` — but a frontier town with pigs at large, a slaughter-house economy and an Indian trade is an unambiguous dog town; place them, and label the claim as inference, not attestation.
- **Fort Dearborn:** garrisoned by *"a company of United States troops, about fifty in number, many of whom were invalids"*; the ground adjoining the fort on the south was **enclosed and cultivated as a garden**. Troops were finally withdrawn **29 December 1836** under Brevet-Major Joseph Plympton. `[DOC]` — [Currey, *The Story of Old Fort Dearborn* (Gutenberg)](https://www.mirrorservice.org/sites/ftp.ibiblio.org/pub/docs/books/gutenberg/5/2/3/4/52346/52346-h/52346-h.htm); [chicagology, Fort Dearborn History](https://chicagology.com/prefire/prefire052/). Latrobe in 1833 refers to *"the slender garrison."* `[DOC]` — Andreas p. 263. **So in July 1835 the fort is still occupied.** Specific 1835 fort livestock (cows, oxen, draft animals) is **not documented in what I could reach** — `[CONJ]`; the safe, sourced elements are the **garden** and the fact that Clybourne held the **beef contract**, implying beef arrived on the hoof or as carcass rather than being pastured at the fort.

### 5.5 The river as a carcass sink

The 1833 town code specifically prohibited **discarding dead animals in the river** `[DOC]` — [chicagology prefire278](https://chicagology.com/prefire/prefire278/) — which is direct evidence that people were doing it. Combined with the frog-pond stench complaint of July 1836, the July river texture is: **slack water, floating and shore-stranded carcasses, flies, crows, scavenging dogs and pigs.** `[INF, well-supported]`

**Three details worth foregrounding in the reconstruction, because each is documented, dated, in-zone, and July-appropriate:** the **frog pond at Lake & LaSalle** (*Chicago American*, 9 July 1836 — and the same corner where Hubbard packed 5,000 hogs in 1834); **pigs at large that wolves ate** (Latrobe, 1833, in Andreas); and **coyotes visible and howling in broad daylight within sight of Water Street** (Andreas p. 433).
