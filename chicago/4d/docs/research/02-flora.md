## 1. VEGETATION COMMUNITIES ON THE SITE

### 1.1 The flat lake plain — wet and wet-mesic prairie
Andreas's compiled early testimony: **"The West Side was an open prairie, entirely free from timber, except for a grove up the South Branch."** South of Fort Dearborn was **"a free and boundless prospect of open prairie, skirted by a belt of timber on the river side, and shut in on the left by the view of the lake and the sand hills of the battle ground."** [S6]

This is *lacustrine* wet prairie on dense, slowly permeable glacial-lake clay — not the rolling mesic prairie of the moraines west of town. Classified in the Illinois Natural Areas Inventory as **wet prairie**, NVC association *Spartina pectinata – Carex spp. – Calamagrostis canadensis* wet meadow (CEGL002224). [S7]

### 1.2 Sloughs and marsh
Two documented wet features inside the platted town:
- **A slough running from the Public Square area (Randolph/Clark/LaSalle/Washington), through the site of the Tremont House (Lake & Dearborn), to the river at State Street.** [S6] This is the single most important vegetation feature *inside* the grid — a linear sedge/marsh strip cutting diagonally across blocks that were nominally "platted."
- **The river-shore strip:** "a strip of land along the river shore that was still more marshy, where land and water mingled, **covered with tall grass, reeds and rushes**." [S6]

The South Side generally was "low and marshy ground." [S6]

### 1.3 Lakeshore — beach, low dune ridges, and fresh 1834 sand
"White sand hills both to the north and south" of the town; the "sand hills of the battle ground" (the 1812 site, ~18th St) closed the southern view. [S3] [S6] The North Side timber was continuous **"except for sandy hills near the lake and marshy areas."** [S6]

**1835-specific:** Congress appropriated harbor funds in 1833; the Corps cut a direct channel through the bar and built piers, completed **1834**. Sand immediately began accreting **north of the north pier** (eventually ~1,200 ft of new land). [S8] So in summer 1835 the lakefront just north of the river mouth is **raw, bare, unvegetated sand**, at most colonised by annual pioneers — not established marram turf. South of the cut, the abandoned old outlet channel and relict bar are drying/reworking.

### 1.4 Riverbank / floodplain timber
"On the South Side, a body of timber grew along the river, extending east as far as Wells Street, and following the bend of the river, crossed Clark Street, and extending south two or three miles." [S6] This is the South Water Street / South Branch riparian belt — the timber that fronted the river inside the town.

### 1.5 The "Dense Forest" northeast of the forks — what it actually was

This is the best-answered question in the file, and the answer is **not upland oak forest.**

**Direct PLS evidence, T39N R14E:** surveyor **J. Walls, 1821, section line 4–5: "First part of this mile a swampy timber thicket."** McBride & Bowles single this out: *"There was also that spot north of what is now the Chicago loop that the survey crew described as a 'swampy timber thicket.'"* They also note: **"Cook County had the only willow thickets described by the PLS."** [S4]

**Andreas's testimony:** the North Side carried **"a body of thrifty heavy growth of timber,"** exceptions being the sandy hills near the lake and the marshy places. [S6] Note "thrifty" in 19th-c. usage = *vigorous, fast-growing*, i.e. young/dense pole timber — consistent with a wet thicket, not with 300-year-old oaks.

**Fire geography argues the same way.** McBride & Bowles found greater tree density and fire-intolerant species (**sugar maple, ash, basswood**) concentrated **on the east sides of watercourses**, which acted as firebreaks against prairie fire moving west→east. [S4] The timber NE of the forks sits east of the North Branch and west of the lake — a doubly fire-protected wedge. That is precisely why the densest timber on the whole town site is there, and why it is the one place you may legitimately render canopy closure.

**Therefore the "Dense Forest" label should be built as a mosaic, not a monoculture:**
1. **Swamp/wet-mesic forest core** (elm–ash–silver maple, with swamp white oak) on the poorly drained clay flats — the "swampy timber thicket";
2. **Willow–dogwood shrub thicket** in the swales and along backwater sloughs (Cook Co. = the *only* PLS willow thickets);
3. **Bur oak / white oak on the low sand and gravel beach ridges** threading through it;
4. **Small fire-protected pockets with basswood, sugar maple, red oak** at highest density;
5. **American hazel (*Corylus americana*) understory** — McBride & Bowles's headline surprise finding: hazel was *the* dominant shrub-layer plant of these woodlands, "although uncommon now." [S4]

## 3. THE 'CHECAGOU' PLANT — WHAT IT WAS AND WHERE IT GREW

**Etymology chain.** Miami-Illinois ***šikaakwa***, French-rendered *chicagoua / chicagou*; the spelling was introduced by **Fr. Jacques Gravier in the 1690s**, "attempting to express the inflection the Indians gave to the last syllable." [S16] **Henri Joutel, 1687–88**, three weeks in the area: the place "has taken this name because of the quantity of garlic which grows in the forests in this region." [S16] [S17]

**Species identification.** **John F. Swenson**, the standard modern treatment, argues from Joutel's own account — the *"ail sauvage"* (wild garlic) taste, its explicit contrast with the native onion, **and its maple-forest habitat** — that the plant "point[s] unambiguously to ***Allium tricoccum***" (wild leek / ramp). [S16] [S17] The linguistic counter-argument (Michael McCafferty) is that *šikaakwa* denotes the striped-skunk odour-root and can, in context, name either the plant or the animal — the plant reading is not disputed for the place name. [S16]

**Where it grew — the renderer-relevant part.**
- ***Allium tricoccum* is a spring ephemeral of rich, moist, fire-protected deciduous woods** — Joutel's "forests," maple/basswood woods. On this site that means **inside the timber NE of the forks and in the mesic river-bank woods**, *not* on the open prairie.
- Andreas's compiled early testimony puts a *different* plant in the open: **"Wild onions grew in great abundance"** along the river shores **among sedge grass** — the Native name *Chi-ka-go* was said to derive from these. [S6] Sedge-grass river margin is *Allium canadense* (wild garlic) and *A. cernuum* (nodding onion) habitat, not ramp habitat.
- Swenson's larger argument locates the ramp's bulk along the **wooded banks of the wider Chicago/Des Plaines river system and the portage corridor** (Mud Lake, lower Des Plaines) rather than at the river mouth specifically. [S17]

> ⚠ **PHENOLOGY TRAP FOR A JULY SCENE.** *Allium tricoccum* leaves emerge in April–May and **wither and disappear before the flowering scape appears in June–July**. In a summer-1835 render, a ramp colony is **bare ground with leafless 20–40 cm flower/fruit stalks bearing white umbels or green three-lobed capsules** — no green leaves. If you want visible onion foliage in July, instance ***Allium canadense*** (river-shore sedge margins, bulbil-bearing umbels) and ***A. cernuum*** (nodding pink umbels, July–Aug, on the sandier prairie and ridges) instead.

# PLANTING PALETTE
**Season: mid-July 1835.** Densities are per hectare unless noted; heights are the *July* height, not peak-season. Tag key: **[D] documented** (a named source asserts it for this site/region), **[I] inferred** (regional PLS/ecological data + site conditions), **[C] conjectural** (plausible, unverified).

> **Global phenology rules — apply before anything else.**
> 1. **Big bluestem, Indian grass, switchgrass do NOT have flowering culms in mid-July.** They are leafy vegetative clumps at ~50–60% of their September height. Rendering 2 m "turkey-foot" seed heads in July is the single most common historical-reconstruction error.
> 2. **Prairie cordgrass and bluejoint DO flower now** — cordgrass is the tall element in July.
> 3. **Cattail spikes are brown and mature in July.**
> 4. **Ramps (*Allium tricoccum*) have no leaves in July** (see §3).
> 5. **Spring woodland ephemerals are gone.** Woodland floor in July = *Laportea canadensis*, ferns, *Ageratina altissima* (not yet in flower), bare leaf litter.
> 6. Mid-July is the **peak forb-diversity moment** of the prairie year — "the number of prairie and savanna forbs coming into bloom rises dramatically in July." [S11]

1. **Query the Morton Arboretum "Witness Trees of Illinois" ArcGIS layer for the actual section corners of T39N R14E sections 3–9 and T40N R14E** — that gives you *site-exact* species and diameters instead of the seven-county average, and will settle the oak-vs-elm/ash proportion in the "Dense Forest" definitively. [S20]
2. **Pull the 1821 Walls field notes and the 1832/1834 Cook County plat sheets** from the Illinois State Archives / Illinois Digital Archives to read the timber boundary as the surveyor drew it, rather than as the 1933 Conley & Steltzer reconstruction interpreted it. [S21] [S22]
3. **Confirm the "Wm. Jones" farm** directly on the Conley & Steltzer sheet (Chicago Historical Society; Caroline McIlvaine advising) — I could not open the map's legend, so both the "Dense Forest" label and the Jones attribution here rest on your reading, not mine. [S30]

**Two flags for the modeller:** (a) the 1933 Conley/Steltzer map is a **1930s reconstruction**, not a period document — treat its "Dense Forest" as a 20th-century interpretation to be checked against the 1821 PLS, which says *swampy timber thicket*; (b) the entire lakefront east of the 1835 corporate limit had just been re-engineered — **the 1834 harbour cut means the shoreline geometry of summer 1835 differs from every pre-1833 map you might use.**
